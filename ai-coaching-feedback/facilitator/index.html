05 · When things go sideways

If it breaks, you have a move

If Projector or the site fails mid-session: Whiteboard the course: the prep-outsource line, SBI, and the two-question boundary test all draw in seconds. The lab becomes a group walk-through: read each step's options aloud, room votes, you read the coaching. Capstone moves to the printed card.
If Learner devices can't join: Run facilitator-only: trainers play well on the projector with A/B/C hand votes; the capstone moves to paper.
If Someone names a real colleague or starts describing an identifiable situation: Protect them warmly and immediately: 'Hold that. Roles, never names; the exercises work on the pattern, not the person.' If something serious surfaced, route it privately to HR or the EAP at the break, never from the front of the room.
If The room reacts to the surveillance section with 'but my platform offers exactly that feature': Honor it; the tools genuinely do. The answer is the two-question test: aggregate or individual, and would you tell the team? A vendor shipping a feature doesn't make it a good management practice, and VU's approved-tool decisions plus the light set the actual boundary.
If A learner asks whether AI-written feedback is dishonest: Draw the authorship line: structure and phrasing help is the same help a book or a template gives; judgment and examples must be yours, verified. The dishonesty starts where the verification stops: signing an assessment you didn't make.

06 · Tough questions, ready answers

Say each answer out loud once before you teach

Isn't using AI for feedback just lazy management?

Using it to avoid the conversation is. Using it to prepare is the opposite: the managers who rehearse, structure their observations, and check their drafts for character judgments walk in MORE prepared than the ones who wing it. The lazy version and the diligent version use the same tool; the difference is whether the human conversation got more of you or less.

If I de-identify, can't AI still guess who I mean on a small team?

Sometimes, which is why de-identification is necessary but not sufficient: the second rule is approved VU tools only, where the data handling is contractually governed. Together they cover the realistic risk. And note what de-identification is really for: it forces you to describe behavior and situation rather than person, which makes the feedback better anyway.

My people will find out I used AI to prep. Isn't that a trust problem?

Only if it was a secret. Everything this course teaches survives being said out loud: 'I structured my notes before our 1:1 so I'd be clearer' is diligence, and most people read it that way. The trust problem comes from the practices that need secrecy, which is exactly what the out-loud test screens for.

Where's the line with engagement survey data?

The platform's anonymization is the line, and it's also the deal that keeps surveys honest. Aggregate themes into conversations: always fine. Any attempt to de-anonymize, match writing styles, or narrow by demographic slice until individuals emerge: never, because the first time a team suspects it, honest surveys are over.

Section 01 · The Principle

AI preps the conversation. You have it.

Rehearsing a hard conversation, structuring messy notes into clear feedback, generating good coaching questions: that's preparation, and AI is excellent at it. Writing words you'll sign without reading, or whispering lines mid-conversation: that's outsourcing the one part of management that only works human to human. The line matters because trust is built in the room, and your people can tell which side of it you're on.

Play · Prep or outsource?

Why this matters: the line is easy to state and easy to drift across. Five real manager moves; call each one.

The data rule, before anything else

Coaching and feedback work runs on information about people, which is exactly what the traffic light guards:

GreenPublic or generic information. Go.
YellowInternal work. Approved VU tools only.
RedPrivate information about people. Never.

The working recipe: de-identify first (roles and situations, never names), keep it in approved VU tools, and describe behavior you observed rather than character you inferred. If a platform with embedded coaching AI is approved at VU, that approval is what makes it a lane; the light still decides what goes in.

Go deeper

In practice

The context behind this session: SHRM's Navigating AI in the Workplace research finds managers report about 50 percent of their work involves AI assistance, versus 34 percent for individual contributors, so the question is no longer whether managers use AI on people work but whether they use it well. And the market has noticed: platforms like Culture Amp's AI Coach, Lattice, and 15Five now embed AI coaching support directly into the flow of work, built on organizational psychology principles. The skills in this session transfer to whichever tools VU approves; the principles, prep not outsource, de-identify, behavior not character, transfer to all of them.

  2. Compare: where exactly did the "never" start for each of you?
  3. One pair shares their sharpest line with the room.

Section 02 · The Draft

From rough notes to SBI.

The best feedback structure is decades old: Situation (when and where), Behavior (what you observed, camera-true), Impact (what it caused). AI is genuinely good at reshaping your messy notes into that structure. Whether the result helps or hurts depends entirely on what you feed it, what you ask for, and what you do with the draft. Run the lab and find out.

Practice · The Feedback Prep Lab

Why this matters: the scenario is real: a solid analyst on your team has missed two handoffs this month, and Thursday's 1:1 is the moment. Choose how you prep, then see how the conversation lands.

Choose all four steps

Quick check: Which of these is the Behavior part of SBI actually supposed to contain?

Go deeper

In practice

A prep prompt worth stealing, names removed before anything else: "Here are my rough observations about a situation with a team member (role only). Structure them as Situation, Behavior, Impact. Flag anything that reads as a judgment of character rather than a description of behavior, and list the questions I should ask before assuming my read is right." That last clause matters most: the draft's job is to make you more prepared to listen, and a good prep surfaces what you might be wrong about. Then the rewrite pass: your voice, verified examples, and nothing you wouldn't say looking at them.

As a group · 4 min

Why this matters: the character-versus-behavior distinction becomes reflex through small reps.

  1. Pairs. Convert these two verdicts into camera-true behavior lines: "she's disengaged lately" and "he's not a team player."
  2. Read the best conversions aloud; the room checks each: would a camera agree?
  3. Notice the pattern: every conversion forced you to recall a specific moment. That recall is the prep.

Flying solo? Take one piece of feedback you owe someone and write the S, the B, and the I in three lines, behavior camera-true. That's the input for your next AI-assisted draft.

Section 03 · The Signals

Coaching radar, with a boundary.

Two data sources make managers better coaches without touching anyone's privacy: aggregate signals your platforms already anonymize (engagement survey themes), and your own 1:1 notes, which record what you already know but stopped seeing. AI is good at both: summarizing themes into discussion questions, and spotting the topic you've postponed four weeks running. The boundary is just as clear: analyzing an individual who hasn't asked for it is surveillance, and it poisons the trust the coaching was for.

Play · Signal or surveillance?

Why this matters: the same tools do both, so the boundary has to live in you. Five uses; call each one.

The two-question boundary test

1. Is it aggregate, or is it about one person who hasn't asked? Team-level themes are coaching radar; individual analysis without consent is surveillance, whatever the tool calls it. 2. Would you tell the team you do this? If the practice needs to stay secret to keep working, it's already over the line. Everything this course teaches survives being said out loud.

Go deeper

In practice

The 1:1 notes move deserves its own habit, because it's the highest-value, lowest-risk use in this course: your own notes, de-identified (roles for names before they touch a tool), in an approved tool, with a monthly prompt like "what topics keep appearing without resolving, what have I committed to and not done, and what has this person asked for more than once?" Managers who run it report the same surprise: the pattern was visible all along, in their own handwriting. Engagement platforms do the equivalent at team level and anonymize by design; your job is to turn themes into one good conversation, never into a hunt for who said what.

As a group · 3 min

Why this matters: saying the boundary out loud, to a colleague, is what makes it durable back at your desk.

  1. Pairs: each pick the one signal use from this section you'll actually adopt.
  2. Then each name the use closest to your own line, the one you'd be tempted by under pressure.
  3. Trade the counter-move: what would you say to a peer manager reaching for it?

Flying solo? Run the two-question boundary test on your current practices. Anything that fails question 2 gets retired today.

Facilitator scriptFull 2 min · Core 2

Convert cards into spoken commitments and capture the Level 1 check.

Say

“The whole session in one breath: AI preps, you converse. De-identified, approved lanes, behavior not character, aggregate not individual. You have a card. Say your conversation, role only, and your prep day out loud. Spoken commitments stick.”

Do
  1. Read the featured next step: prep, then the conversation, nothing between you and them.
  2. Fist-to-five on confidence; note the number for your log.
  3. Commitment round: conversation (role only) and prep day, out loud or in chat.
  4. Point at the cheat sheet and prep card buttons; tell them the 7-day pulse is coming; stop on time.

Validate: Fist-to-five mostly 3+; a majority state a role and a day.

Watch for: Cards without dates; the round catches them.

Transition

“Go run the prep. Then close the laptop and go be the manager the prep was for.”

60-min core: Core: fist-to-five plus a fast round; the ending survives every cut.
