The data rule · the same card in every AI course

Before anything goes into an AI tool, one three-second question: what color is this?

🟢
Green · Public or generic
Questions about public topics, generic drafts, brainstorming, "explain this to me." Any approved tool.
🟡
Yellow · Internal work
Your drafts, internal announcements, budgets, grant drafts, internal policies. Approved Vanderbilt tools only, signed in with your VU account. Never a personal account or a random free site.
🔴
Red · Private information about people
Names with grades, health details, personnel issues, passwords, anything confidential about a real person. No AI tool, full stop.

Inside the light: Vanderbilt classifies data in levels, and each approved tool has a ceiling. ChatGPT EDU (VU account) is approved up to Level 2. Amplify and Microsoft Copilot (VU account) are approved for Level 3 work. Children's names, photos, and records go in no AI tool.

The hard cases: ask "who is this about" before "how sensitive does it feel." A student's essay is about a student. Meeting notes naming colleagues are about colleagues until the names come out. A recommendation letter is about its subject.

Never, in any tool: Social Security numbers · Student records or grades (FERPA) · Health information (HIPAA) · Children's names, photos, and records · Salaries and personnel files · Passwords and credentials · Anything confidential about a real person.

This card is the working shorthand for Vanderbilt IT's data classification and approved-tool guidance: vanderbilt.edu/it/policies. When the two ever disagree, the policy wins.

Section 01 · The Line

AI drafts the instrument. You own the verdict.

Every talent process has two layers. The instruments: job descriptions, interview questions, scoring rubrics, skills taxonomies, the tools that make judgment consistent. And the verdicts: hire, promote, rate, succeed. AI is a strong drafter of instruments and a dangerous maker of verdicts, because it inherits the bias in its data and delivers it with confidence. Automated employment decisions are regulated territory (NYC Local Law 144 requires bias audits of automated hiring tools, and the EEOC has issued guidance on adverse impact from AI in selection), and the discipline that keeps you effective and defensible is the same one: instruments drafted, verdicts human.

The data rule, non-negotiable here

Talent work is where the traffic light matters most, because nearly everything in it identifies a person:

GreenPublic or generic information. Go.
YellowInternal work. Approved VU tools only.
RedPrivate information about people. Never.

Resumes, reviews, ratings, and anything with a name are red in unapproved tools, full stop. Job descriptions and role criteria are yellow: approved tools only. And anything touching candidate evaluation at VU runs with HR, in HR's approved systems, before AI touches it at all.

Go deeper

In practice

Industry coverage (People Managing People; Rework) tracks AI spreading across screening, interview scoring, skills inference, succession, and internal matching, and it also tracks the failures: screening models that learned yesterday's biases, scoring tools that couldn't explain a rejection, and jurisdictions responding with bias-audit requirements for automated employment decisions. The pattern behind every failure is the same: an instrument quietly became a verdict-maker. The pattern behind the successes is this session: better instruments, drafted fast, in human hands. One more reason the line matters: structured interviews with anchored scoring are among the best-validated selection methods there are, so instrument quality is where the real gains live anyway.

As a group · 3 min

Why this matters: your own talent process almost certainly has a spot where the line is blurry today.

  1. In pairs: name the last talent decision you were part of (role, not person). Where did instruments end and verdict begin?
  2. Was any part of the verdict effectively delegated: to a score, a screen, a ranking?
  3. One pair shares the blurriest spot they found.

Flying solo? Write down your next talent decision and draw the line: which parts are instruments AI could draft, and where does the human verdict start?

The core idea

AI can rank anything. Never let it rank people.

Section 02 · The Rubric

A 9-box worth trusting.

The 9-box (performance across, potential up) is only as good as its definitions, and in most organizations those definitions are vibes. That's how a talent grid becomes politics with a spreadsheet. The fix is the rubric: criteria you can observe, anchored levels a stranger could apply, and independent scoring. AI helps at every step, drafting criteria from the job description, proposing anchors, stress-testing for bias magnets, as long as it never touches the scoring. Build one now and see how your next talent review goes.

Go deeper

In practice

The same build works for interviews, and it's the highest-value instrument most managers never make: paste the job description into an approved tool and ask for the five competencies the role actually needs, two behavioral questions per competency ("tell me about a time..."), and a three-level anchored scoring guide per question, with concrete examples of a 1, a 2, and a 3. Then the human passes: cut what you can't observe in an interview, check the anchors against real answers you've heard, and have every interviewer score independently before comparing. Ask AI to review your final rubric for bias magnets too, vague criteria like "polish" and "culture fit" that let similarity sneak in dressed as standards.

As a group · 4 min

Why this matters: anchor-writing is a skill, and one rep with colleagues calibrates it faster than any explanation.

  1. Pairs. Take the criterion "communicates clearly" and write a level-3 anchor: a concrete, observable example a stranger could recognize.
  2. Swap with another pair: could you apply their anchor without asking questions? If not, sharpen it together.
  3. Notice what happened to "communicates clearly": it stopped being a compliment and became a standard.

Flying solo? Write one level-3 anchor for a criterion you actually use in reviews. The test: would two colleagues apply it the same way without talking to you?

Section 03 · The Skills Map

Map the skills. Close the gaps.

A team skills map answers the question every planning cycle asks: do we have the capabilities next year needs? AI makes the mapping cheap: draft a skill taxonomy from your team's job descriptions, lay it against your goals, and draft development moves for the gaps. Two disciplines keep it clean: the map runs de-identified (roles and skills, never names attached to scores in any tool), and skills inference is a draft: the person is always the source of truth about their own skills.

Play · Useful and safe?

Why this matters: skills work sits right on the data line, and the same idea can be safe or reckless depending on what goes in. Five moves; call each one.

The gap-closing recipe

1. Taxonomy from the team's job descriptions, drafted in an approved tool. 2. Each person self-rates and adds what the JD missed; people are the source of truth on their own skills. 3. The de-identified map (roles, skills, levels) goes against next year's goals; AI drafts the gap list. 4. For each gap, three closing moves: grow (a development assignment), borrow (internal matching or a partner team), or hire. The development conversations that follow are human, one to one, and use section 01 of the coaching course.

Go deeper

In practice

Succession gets the same treatment as everything else in this course: AI can draft the instrument, a succession profile for a key role (the capabilities, experiences, and relationships the role actually requires, drawn from the JD and the role's real outcomes), and the readiness criteria a candidate would show. What it never does is name the successor, score the candidates, or read performance reviews. When leaders ask AI "who should succeed me," they're handing the highest-stakes verdict in management to a system that can't be held accountable for it. Draft the profile, then do succession the human way: conversations, evidence, and a calibrated decision that someone with a name signs.

As a group · 3 min

Why this matters: the grow-borrow-hire frame turns a gap list from an anxiety into a plan.

  1. Pairs: name one skill your team will need more of next year (skill, not person).
  2. Sketch all three closes in one line each: the grow move, the borrow move, the hire move.
  3. Call your most realistic close to the room; notice how often it's grow or borrow, not hire.

Flying solo? Same drill on your own: one gap, three closes, one line each. Keep it; the capstone builds on it.
