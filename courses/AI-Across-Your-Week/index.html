Across Your Week · The CHART Integrator · Vanderbilt

One trick is a party.
Seven is a practice.

Gallup keeps asking working adults how they use AI and what it actually does for them, and the sharpest finding is about range: people who use AI for seven or more distinct tasks are roughly twice as likely to report real productivity gains as people who use it for one or two. Breadth is the multiplier. Each CHART course builds one use; this capstone strings them together across one real week, yours, and ends with a Week Map: five or more assists on your actual calendar, each with its method and its check.

By the end of this session, you will be able to

  1. Explain why breadth of AI use predicts productivity gains better than mastery of any single trick
  2. Inventory a real week into recurring, AI-matchable blocks with an honest hour cost on each
  3. Match each block to the CHART method that fits its verb: drafts, answers, minutes, slides, ideas, decisions, numbers
  4. Stack two methods on one task while every link in the chain keeps its own check
  5. Commit to a five-assist week on your actual calendar, with the saved time banked where it can be seen

Section 01 · The Breadth Effect

The multiplier is breadth.

Most people who use AI at work found one trick, an email draft or a quick summary, and stopped there. The research says stopping there is exactly where the gains stop too. Before the method, the evidence: four findings from Gallup's workplace AI research, and most rooms guess them wrong.

The one-trick user

One or two uses

Drafts the occasional email, summarizes the occasional document. The habit lives in one corner of the week, the rest of the calendar never changes, and the reported gains stay modest.

The seven-plus user

Seven or more distinct uses

Drafts, answers, minutes, slides, numbers: AI shows up wherever the week repeats itself. Roughly twice as likely to report real productivity gains, because the assists compound across the calendar.

Go deeper

In practice

The source for the game is Gallup's workplace AI research, the long-running survey of how employees actually use AI on the job. The pattern across its findings: adoption keeps spreading, most users park at one or two occasional uses, and the people reporting real productivity gains are the frequent, broad users with AI threaded through many distinct tasks. The plateau has a simple mechanic. One use case saves minutes in one place while the rest of the week goes untouched, so the gain never feels real and the habit never grows. Seven uses reach seven corners of the calendar, the assists start feeding each other, and the saved time gets big enough to notice. Which raises the question this course answers: where in YOUR week would seven assists even go?

As a group · 4 min

Why this matters: counting your own distinct uses makes the plateau personal in about ninety seconds.

  1. Everyone privately counts their distinct AI uses in the last month. Different tasks count separately; the same email draft ten times counts once.
  2. Hands: who counted one or two? Three to six? Seven or more? Watch the room's shape appear.
  3. Pairs: the higher counter names one use their partner does not have yet. Thirty seconds each way.

Flying solo? Count your own distinct uses from the last month, honestly. Write the number down; the capstone asks you to grow it, and the before-and-after is your evidence.

The core idea

The gain is not in the task. It is in the habit.

Section 02 · The Inventory

First, see the week as blocks.

You cannot place seven assists on a week you have never looked at. So the Week Map starts with an inventory: the recurring blocks your calendar actually runs on, written down with an honest hour cost next to each. Most weeks are built from a handful of repeating shapes.

Meetings

The recurring rooms

Team meetings, one-on-ones, committees. The blocks themselves stay; what AI can absorb is the record-keeping and the follow-up they generate.

Writing

The recurring pages

Status updates, newsletters, announcements, the same email in forty costumes. Usually the biggest single pile of draftable hours in the week.

Reporting

The recurring numbers

Exports, dashboards, the spreadsheet that lands every Thursday. Reading data and explaining what changed eats more of the week than most people admit.

Inbox

The daily drip

Triage, routine replies, the requests that arrive shaped like the last hundred. Small individually, enormous by Friday.

Prep and research

The looking-things-up hours

Policy questions, vendor comparisons, background before a meeting. The block people forget to count because it hides inside other blocks.

The honest hour

Cost each block for real

Next to each block, the hours it truly takes in a normal week. The honest number is usually higher than the guess, and the gap is where the Week Map pays.

One rule before you write anything

An inventory can drift into naming people: the difficult report, the slow approver. Keep it blocks, roles, and workflows. And the traffic light from Start Smarter governs every assist you place this week:

GreenPublic or generic information. Go.
YellowInternal work. Approved VU tools only.
RedPrivate information about people. Never.

If a block touches red data, that block's assist stays out of unapproved tools, whatever the time savings look like. The light outranks the map.

Go deeper

In practice

Three habits make an inventory worth keeping. Work from last week's actual calendar and sent folder, because memory flatters the week and the calendar tells the truth. Keep blocks at the size of an appointment, an hour or three, since "administration" is too big to match a method to and "opened the spreadsheet" is too small. And write the honest hour in ink: if the Thursday report truly costs four hours across the week, saying four is what makes the later math real. Five to eight blocks is plenty. The inventory is private and nobody audits it; it is simply the surface the next section's matching runs on.

As a group · 5 min

Why this matters: hearing someone else's week read aloud surfaces the blocks you forgot were work.

  1. Pairs. One person reads their three blocks and the honest hours out loud, blocks and roles only.
  2. The partner asks one question: "what recurring work did you leave out?" There is always one; it usually hides in prep or inbox.
  3. Swap. Each person leaves with four blocks instead of three.

Flying solo? Open last week's calendar and sent folder for two minutes and add the block you forgot. The fourth block found this way is usually the most repetitive one, which makes it the easiest match in the next section.

Section 03 · The Method Match

Match the verb, not the topic.

Here is the whole CHART toolbox on one slide: seven methods, each built by its own course. The matching rule that makes the toolbox usable is simple. Ignore what the block is about and ask what you are doing in it. A writing verb takes the drafts method, a question verb takes answers, a deciding verb takes decisions. Verbs match; topics mislead.

Verb: asking

Answers

Research with ask, anchor, check: get the answer, anchor it to a source, verify before you rely on it.

Verb: writing

First Drafts

AI writes the first pass from your real material; you rewrite the judgment parts and check every claim.

Verb: capturing

Minutes

Meetings into a record: decisions, owners, deadlines, checked against your own notes before it ships.

Verb: presenting

Slides

A brief before a deck: message, audience, three points, then slides. You keep the recommendation slide.

Verb: inventing

Ideas

Volume first, judgment second: many options fast, then your standards pick what survives.

Verb: choosing

Decisions

Options, criteria, and tradeoffs laid out for you to weigh. The call itself stays yours.

Verb: explaining data

Numbers

Spreadsheets into plain language: what changed, what drives it, spot-checked against the source file.

Play · Match the method

Why this matters: the toolbox only pays when you can sight-read a block and reach for the right method. Five real week-blocks; find each one's verb, then its method.

Quick check: Your block is "prepare the quarterly budget summary for the finance committee." What tells you which method fits?

Go deeper

In practice

Every method on this slide is its own CHART course, with the full technique and its checks: the First Drafts course for the writing verb, the Answers course for the asking verb, the Minutes course for meetings, plus Slides, Ideas, Decisions, and Numbers, all sitting alongside Start Smarter in the Course Library. You do not need all seven to build a Week Map; you need the two or three whose verbs dominate your inventory. Match your blocks first, then take the course for any method you reached for and found thin. That order matters: the week tells you which courses to take, which beats taking courses and hoping a week shows up.

As a group · 5 min

Why this matters: matching someone else's blocks is easier than matching your own, and the misses teach the verb rule fast.

  1. Pairs. Swap the inventories you wrote in section 02, three or four blocks each.
  2. Match every block of your partner's week to a method, saying the verb out loud first: "this is a writing verb, so drafts."
  3. Compare with what the owner would have picked. Disagreements almost always mean the block hides two verbs, which is a preview of the next section.

Flying solo? Match your own three blocks: say the verb, then the method. If one block will not settle on a single verb, split it in two; multi-verb blocks are tomorrow's stacks.

Section 04 · The Stack

Two methods, one task: the stack.

Some blocks hold two verbs, and that is where the biggest savings live. The meeting produces a record and the record needs a follow-up email: minutes into drafts. The export needs explaining and the explanation needs a deck: numbers into slides. Chaining methods is the seven-plus user's signature move, with one law attached: every link keeps its own check, because a chain is only as trustworthy as its weakest verify.

Minutes into Drafts

Meeting to follow-up

The recording becomes minutes, checked against your notes. The checked minutes become the action-item email, read before it sends. Two checks, one afternoon saved.

Numbers into Slides

Export to deck

The spreadsheet becomes a plain-language read, spot-checked against the source. The checked read becomes a slide brief, and you keep the recommendation slide.

Answers into Decisions

Research to the call

The question becomes sourced answers, anchored and verified. The verified answers become a decision brief with criteria and tradeoffs. The call stays yours.

The weakest-verify law

An error that slips through link one rides the chain in a nicer costume: a wrong figure in the minutes becomes a confident wrong figure in the follow-up email. So the rule for every stack is unglamorous and firm. Check the output of link one before it feeds link two. Skip a check to move faster and you have chosen the chain's ceiling; the weakest verify sets it.

Play · Build the chain

Why this matters: stacks are the move that separates the seven-plus user from the one-trick user. Five real tasks; pick the two-method stack that fits.

Go deeper

In practice

Start with a two-link stack and resist longer ones for the first month. Every added link multiplies the ways an unverified claim can travel, and a three-link chain with soft checks fails more expensively than three separate assists. The check between links can be quick: three figures traced to the source file, the decision list read against your own jottings, one claim followed back to the policy page. Quick is fine; skipped is the failure. And notice what stacking does to your time math: a stack saves the handoff hours, the retyping and reformatting between tools, which is often more than either method saves alone. That is why the Week Map asks for one stack, singular, done well.

As a group · 5 min

Why this matters: finding the stack hiding in your own inventory is the moment this section becomes a plan.

  1. Pairs. Reread your heaviest block from section 02 and name its verbs. Most heavy blocks hold two.
  2. Build its stack out loud: method one, the check between, method two, the final check.
  3. Stress-test your partner's chain with one question: "which check would you skip on a busy Thursday?" That check is the one to write down.

Flying solo? Write your heaviest block's stack in four lines: method one, its check, method two, its check. Mark the check you would be most tempted to skip; the lab is about to show you why.

Go deeper

In practice

Watch where the lab punishes you. Never for using AI on too many blocks; always for the design around it: an unchecked draft feeding a deck, an auto-reply speaking for you, saved hours with no name on them. The strong week has a texture worth noticing too. Its wins are boring: twenty minutes here, an hour there, checks that each take three minutes. Breadth gains rarely announce themselves; they accumulate, which is exactly why the last block, the bank, matters so much. If nobody names where six quiet hours went, the week-four story becomes "we're busier than ever," and the whole practice loses its evidence.

As a group · 6 min

Why this matters: translating the coordinator's week into your own is the rehearsal for the capstone.

  1. Hands by tier: who reached the strong week, who hit week-three trouble? One mid-or-weak learner reads their coaching aloud; the room learns from the postmortem.
  2. Pairs: name which of the coordinator's four blocks looks most like YOUR week, and which option you honestly run today.
  3. Each pair states one upgrade out loud: the block, the method, and the check that comes with it.

Flying solo? Rerun the lab once, changing only your weakest block, and watch the week-four story change. Then write down which of your real blocks that weakest choice resembles.

Section 06 · Bank the Time

Saved time evaporates. Unless you bank it.

Here is the finding that makes the last move necessary. Coaches and researchers who study how leaders spend their calendars, Leadership Circle among them, keep meeting the same pattern: time recovered by any efficiency quietly refills with meetings and busywork unless it is deliberately reassigned. The hours your map saves this week already have a dozen claimants. Banking means naming the destination before the time arrives.

The bank, in three parts

1. Name the destination: the backlog project, the deep-work block, leaving on time on Fridays. A real thing, said out loud. 2. Put it on the calendar: a named block where the saved hours land, visible to whoever sees your calendar. 3. Report it: one line in your next one-on-one about what the assists saved and what the time bought. Visible gains survive; invisible ones get reabsorbed.

Quick check: The minutes method starts saving you two hours a week. What does this section say to do first?

Play · Judge the bank

Why this matters: evaporation is quiet, and busywork wears a productive costume. Five accounts of what happened to saved time; call each one.

Go deeper

In practice

The bank is also what makes your practice legible to other people. A manager who hears "AI saves me time" hears a claim; a manager who sees a calendar block named for the assessment project, and hears one line a month about what the assists saved and what the time bought, sees evidence. That visibility protects the gain twice over: the named block is harder for meeting invitations to colonize, and the reported line builds the case for the practice spreading to the team. One more thing worth saying plainly: leaving on time is a legitimate bank. Recovered hours do not owe anyone more output; they owe a destination, and home is one.

As a group · 4 min

Why this matters: a destination said out loud to another person is much harder to abandon than one you thought quietly.

  1. Everyone writes one line, privately: "the first hours my Week Map saves go to ___."
  2. Pairs: read your line to your partner. The partner asks one question: "when will I see that on your calendar?"
  3. A few volunteers share with the room. Listen for the pattern: the believable banks are specific.

Flying solo? Write the line, then open your calendar and create the block right now, named for its destination, in the first week your map runs. The bank exists when the calendar says it does.
