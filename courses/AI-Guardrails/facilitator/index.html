05 · When things go sideways

If it breaks, you have a move

If Projector or the site fails mid-session: Teach from the whiteboard: the traffic light, the four lines, and the misled test all draw in seconds. The classification and disclosure drills never needed a screen; read the trainer cases aloud from the printed cheat sheet and have the room vote by hands. The builder and capstone move to the printed agreement.
If Learner devices can't join (wifi or filters): Run facilitator-only: the trainers play well on the projector with the room voting A/B/C by hands; the private builder and capstone move to paper. You lose typing, not learning.
If You're 10+ minutes behind by the Guardrails Lab (05): Declare the Core path silently: pair drills become solo passes, debriefs shrink to one voice, and the closing tightens to the fist-to-five plus commitment round. Never cut the lab, the builder, or the capstone; they carry the session's practice objectives.
If Someone names a colleague or starts prosecuting a specific person's AI use: Protect the norm warmly: 'Hold that. Tools and situations, never names. The lines work on situations, not people.' If it keeps happening, there is usually a real conflict underneath; move the person to a private follow-up and keep the room on situations.
If Someone discloses a possible real incident, like student data already pasted into a personal tool: This outranks the agenda. Thank them for saying it out loud, do not adjudicate from the front of the room, and route it: their manager and the appropriate policy owner, today, framed as cleanup rather than punishment. Then use the moment, without the details, to teach why early surfacing is the cheap outcome the written lines exist to produce.
If The room is skeptical: 'this is compliance theater, nobody will follow a page': Agree with the premise and flip it: pages nobody follows are announced pages. Point at the working agreement standard: written WITH the team, findable, revisited. Then ask the skeptic what their team's current rule is; the honest answer is usually 'there isn't one,' which makes the case better than you can.
If The room is the opposite: enthusiasts who want AI in everything today: Aim the enthusiasm at the lab: 'Great, you're writing the lines for the rollout.' Enthusiast teams fail through unread output and undisclosed use, so let the lab's weak tier and the ownership section do the sobering. The message that lands: guardrails are what let you go fast without becoming the cautionary tale.
If Someone asks about a tool not on the approved list, or claims the list is wrong: Never adjudicate a specific tool live. The move is the agreement's own rule: the page points at IT's list, and questions about the list go to IT. Park the tool question, promise the route, and use it as a live example of 'unclear cases route, never improvise.'

06 · Tough questions, ready answers

Say each answer out loud once before you teach

Am I in trouble for what I already pasted into a personal AI account?

The honest answer: this session is not an audit, and the goal is better decisions from today forward. If something specific is weighing on you, especially anything about students or colleagues, the right move is to tell your manager or the policy owner so it can be assessed calmly; early surfacing is consistently the cheap outcome, and hiding it is how small things become incidents. The written lines exist precisely so nobody has to make these calls alone under deadline again.

Why does a rec letter or student essay matter? I'm the one asking for the help, and I have good intentions.

Because the data is about someone else, and they never agreed to it entering a tool whose data handling you cannot see. FERPA does not have a good-intentions exemption, and neither does the person the letter describes. The workaround is usually easy: strip the identifying details and ask for structure help, or use an approved tool for the yellow parts. What never works is deciding on their behalf that the risk is fine.

Doesn't disclosure make us look lazy, like we didn't do the work?

The evidence runs the other way: readers punish discovered concealment far harder than disclosed assistance. A plain line, drafted with AI assistance and reviewed by the team, reads as a process statement, the same register as 'photo by' credits. What reads as lazy is unread AI output, and that is a line-two failure, not a disclosure problem. If a piece would embarrass you when disclosed, the piece needs more human work before it ships.

Half my team already uses AI quietly. Won't writing rules just drive it further underground?

Unwritten rules are what drove it underground; people hide what they cannot predict the reaction to. A written page with reasonable lines does the opposite: it tells people what is safe to say out loud, and teams that draft the page together usually surface the quiet use in the drafting meeting itself. The one thing that does drive it underground is treating the first honest disclosure as a disciplinary event, which is why the agreement frames routing and cleanup, never punishment.

Who enforces this? A team page has no teeth.

Line four is the teeth. When every output has a named owner who answers for it, the other lines get enforced by the person with the most at stake, which beats any compliance mechanism a one-page agreement could invent. And the page has a second kind of teeth: when something goes wrong, the difference between 'we had a written line and someone crossed it' and 'we had no rule at all' is enormous, for the team and for the person.

This will all change in six months. Why write rules for tools that keep moving?

The four lines are deliberately tool-agnostic. What goes in, who verifies, when we disclose, who owns it: those questions outlive any model release, and the quarterly revisit is how the answers track the tools. Notice the page also points at IT's approved list rather than copying it, for exactly this reason. Teams that wait for the tools to settle are choosing unwritten rules during the fastest-moving period, which is backwards.

What if my team's lines conflict with another team's lines?

For work inside your team, your page governs your team. For shared outputs, the stricter line wins while the two owners talk, which is itself a route-not-improvise case: raise it, settle it, and both pages gain a line. And no team's page can loosen university policy; the lines add local clarity on top of FERPA, IT's tool list, and the rest, never subtract from them.

Section 01 · Written Rules

Careful is a mood. Written is a policy.

Before the method, the evidence. Stanford's AI Index counts AI incidents worldwide, and the numbers move one direction: more use, more incidents. Meanwhile the careers publication Metaintro keeps making the same observation from the workplace side: AI policy is turning into line-manager work, because the useful rules are the ones close enough to the work to be followed. Most rooms guess these numbers wrong, and the gap between the guess and the data is where this session lives.

The pattern

Incidents rise with adoption

Reported AI misuse incidents nearly doubled year over year in the AI Index count. Nothing about the people changed; the volume of use did. Risk scales with use, whatever the intentions.

Team A

The team with vibes

Everyone means well. Each person quietly draws their own line about what to paste, when to double-check, and whether to mention AI at all. Six people, six invisible rulebooks.

Team B

The team with lines

Same tools, same pressures, one page. When the hard case lands on a deadline, the answer is already written down, and every new hire inherits it on day one.

In practice

The evidence lives at Stanford's AI Index, the long-running annual measurement of AI's trajectory, which tracks reported AI incidents alongside adoption; the misuse count nearly doubled year over year, and both curves point up. Section 02 · Line One

What goes in decides everything after.

Line one answers the question every other question depends on: which data may enter which tools. Start Smarter gave you the traffic light, and the light still runs everything. What this session adds is the part that trips real teams: the hard cases, where ordinary-looking work turns out to be about a person.

Line one starts here

The traffic light, exactly as Start Smarter taught it. Line one is this light written down for your team's actual work:

GreenPublic or generic information. Go.
YellowInternal work. Approved VU tools only.
RedPrivate information about people. Never.

The light outranks everything else in this course. Whatever a later section teaches about checking or disclosing, red data never enters an unapproved tool.

Red hides in plain sight

About a person means red

A student's essay is about a student. Meeting notes naming colleagues are about colleagues, until the names come out. A recommendation letter is about its subject. Ask "who is this about" before "how sensitive does it feel."

Yellow has a home

Internal work, approved tools

Budget drafts, grant drafts, internal policies: yellow. ChatGPT EDU, Amplify, and Copilot are approved because their agreements keep university work inside university walls. Yellow in a personal account is a rule already broken.

Green is narrower than it looks

Public on purpose, or generic

Published research, public web copy, and generic questions with nothing of yours pasted in. If it appeared in public on purpose, or contains nothing about your work or your people, go.

Play · Green, yellow, or red

Why this matters: the light is easy on a poster and harder at 4:55 on a deadline. Five hard cases; call the light on each.

Quick check: Your meeting notes name three colleagues. What makes them safe to bring into an approved tool?

Go deeper

In practice

The sorting question that catches the hard cases is "who is this about," asked before "how sensitive does it feel." FERPA protects student education records broadly: grades, schedules, advising notes, and in some contexts the fact of enrollment itself, which is why student work defaults to red however routine it looks. Health-adjacent information plays by stricter rules still. On the yellow lane, remember what approval means: ChatGPT EDU, Amplify, and Copilot are approved for internal work because of their agreements, never as a loophole for people data. And when a case genuinely will not classify, the move belongs in the agreement itself: treat it as red for today and ask the policy owner. Improvising under deadline is how the AI Index gets its numbers.

And when a case genuinely will not classify, treat it as red for today and ask; improvising under deadline is how the incident count grows.”

Do
  1. Walk the traffic light card verbatim and point at the room's poster: green public or generic, yellow internal in approved VU tools, red private information about people, never.
  2. Walk the three hard-case cards: red hides in plain sight, yellow has a home, green is narrower than it looks.
  3. Name the approved tools out loud: ChatGPT EDU, Amplify, Copilot, and say why approval matters: their agreements keep university work inside university walls.
  4. Send them to Green, yellow, or red: five hard cases on devices.
  5. Run the quick check on de-identification, then the pair drill: classify your team's two most-handled data types and bring the hardest call to the room.
  6. Let the room argue one hard call briefly; settle it with the tell: who is this about?
Ask

“From the pair drill: what was your hardest call, and what made it hard?”

Expect:

  • Meeting recordings and transcripts: work product tangled up with names
  • Student-adjacent data that feels routine: rosters, advising emails, grade spreadsheets
  • Something aggregated or anonymized, where the person-question gets genuinely subtle

Respond: Name the pattern: 'Notice the hard calls are almost never about sensitivity; they're about entanglement, people data braided into ordinary work. The fix is almost always the same: take the people out first, and the rest of the work turns yellow. When even that is unclear, that case goes on your page with a named person to ask.'

Debrief

The light, plus the hard cases, plus a route for the unclear ones: that is line one, and you just drafted its raw material from your own team's data.

Validate: 4+ of 5 on the trainer for most of the room; every learner has classified their own team's data types; the room can state the light unprompted and name the approved tools.

Watch for: Two drifts: 'it's fine because the tool promises privacy' (a prompt is a request; the agreement and the light decide, not the promise), and red-light violations wearing efficiency clothes ('AI could draft all my advisee emails!'). Correct both immediately and kindly; the light correction matters more than the flow of the room.

Transition

“Line one decides what goes in. Line two decides what happens before anything comes out.”

60-min core: Core: light and hard-case cards at full strength, trainer on devices; the pair classification becomes a solo pass with the hardest call written down for the team meeting.

Section 03 · Line Two

Size the check to the blast radius.

Line two says which outputs need a named human check before they travel. The wrong answer is "all of them," because a team that checks everything equally soon checks nothing well. The right answer scales with blast radius: how far the output travels, and who acts on it when it lands.

No check needed

Stays on your screen

Brainstorms, idea lists, drafts nobody else will see. If it never leaves your screen, the only reader it can mislead is you, and you were in the room when it was made.

Quick read

Travels to a few

Internal notes, agendas, summaries a colleague will skim. One attentive pass by the person who ships it: does this say what I mean, and is anything off?

Full source check

Numbers, names, dates, policy

Anything a student acts on, anything external, anything carrying figures or policy claims. Checked against the source system by a named person before it moves.

Line two, written down

Who checks: a named person, usually whoever ships it. Against what: the actual source: the policy page, the data system, the calendar. Memory is not a source, and neither is "it reads right," because reading right is the one thing AI output always does. Before it goes where: the check happens before the output reaches anyone who will act on it. A check that happens after has a different name: a correction.

Play · Size the check

Why this matters: over-checking burns the attention your student-facing work needs, and under-checking ships the wrong deadline. Five outputs; size each check.

Quick check: One question sizes the verification. Which is it?

Go deeper

In practice

Two things keep line two alive after week one. First, the name: a check assigned to "someone" dies on the first busy Friday, quietly, and looks fine right up to the correction, which is why the written line names who checks each output type. Second, the source: AI errors arrive fluent and confident, so the check runs against the policy page, the data system, or the calendar, never against how the draft reads. And the line has a budget clause worth writing down: if verifying an output costs what producing it did, that output was a bad candidate for AI drafting in the first place. Send it back to a human and spend the checking attention where drafting actually pays.

As a group · 5 min

Why this matters: line two is only real when it names your team's actual outputs and actual checkers.

  1. Pairs: list the three outputs your team ships most often.
  2. Size each check out loud: none, quick read, or full source check, and say the blast radius that justifies it.
  3. For each full source check, name the checker and the source they check against. Those sentences go straight into your agreement.

Flying solo? Same drill on paper: your team's three most-shipped outputs, a check size for each, and a named checker plus source for the biggest one.

Facilitator scriptFull 11 min · Core 8

Install verification sized to blast radius: no check for what stays private, a quick read for what travels to a few, a named full source check for numbers, names, dates, policy, and anything acted on.

Say

“Line two says which outputs need a named human check before they travel, and the wrong answer is all of them. A team that checks everything equally soon checks nothing well. So we size the check to the blast radius: how far does the output travel, and who acts on it when it lands? Stays on your screen: no check; the only reader it can mislead is you. Travels to a few colleagues: one attentive read by whoever ships it. But numbers, names, dates, policy claims, anything a student acts on or anything leaving the university: full source check, against the actual policy page or data system, never against memory, and never against whether it reads right, because reading right is the one thing AI output always does. And the check has a name attached, because a check assigned to 'someone' dies on the first busy Friday.”

Do
  1. Land the counterintuitive opener: 'check everything' is the wrong answer, because a team that checks everything equally soon checks nothing well.
  2. Walk the three tiers: no check, quick read, full source check, with blast radius as the sorting question.
  3. Walk the gold rubric card: who checks, against what, before it goes where. Stress that memory is not a source and 'reads right' is what AI output always does.
  4. Send them to Size the Check: five outputs on devices.
  5. Run the quick check, then the pair drill: size the checks for your team's three most-shipped outputs and name the checker for the biggest one.
Ask

“From the sizing drill: which of your team's outputs got the full source check, and who is the named checker?”

Expect:

  • Student-facing answers and anything with deadlines or requirements
  • Numbers that travel upward: dashboards, briefing slides, reports
  • An honest 'we ship that one unchecked today,' usually about a newsletter or FAQ

Respond: Treat the honest answer as the win: 'That sentence, that output plus a name plus a source, is line two of your agreement, and you just wrote it. The gap you found is the reason the page exists.'

Debrief

Who checks, against what, before it goes where, sized to blast radius: line two. Cheap where stakes are low, rigorous where someone acts on the answer.

Validate: 4+ of 5 on the trainer; pair drills produce named checkers and named sources; the room can say why over-checking is a real failure mode.

Watch for: The over-correction: learners concluding every AI output needs heavyweight review. Keep the budget idea alive: verification spends attention, attention is scarce, and wasting it on brainstorms is how student-facing checks get starved.

Transition

“Line three is the awkward one: when do we tell people AI helped? One question settles it.”

60-min core: Core: tiers and rubric at full strength, trainer on devices; the pair drill becomes a solo sizing of your own three outputs.

Section 04 · Line Three

Would the reader feel misled?

Line three answers the question teams find most awkward: when do we tell readers that AI helped? One test settles nearly every case. Imagine the reader learning, later, exactly how this was made. If they would feel misled, disclose. If you can't say no with a straight face, disclose. The test works because you have to face an actual reader to run it.

Disclose

The reader is owed it

External documents, anything carrying the university's voice, and any assessment or feedback on a person's work. People have a right to know how judgment about them was formed.

No disclosure needed

You own the words

Brainstorms, outlines, and first drafts you rewrote until they're genuinely yours. Disclosure follows the reader's expectations, and no reader expects a memo to list its brainstorming partners.

The honest middle

Unsure? Say so and ask

Funder rules, journal policies, and brand-new formats produce genuinely unclear cases. Ask the policy owner instead of improvising, then write the answer into the agreement so nobody asks twice.

Play · Disclose or not

Why this matters: the misled test only becomes instinct through reps. Five scenarios; make the call on each.

Quick check: The dependable test for disclosure is…

Go deeper

In practice

Disclosure in the wild is one plain sentence: "Drafted with AI assistance and reviewed by the events team." Cheap, honest, and forgettable, which is the point; the goal is a reader who never feels tricked, never a ceremony. The asymmetry is the whole argument: the sentence costs nothing, and discovered concealment taxes everything your office publishes afterward. Assessments are the strictest case and worth naming in your agreement explicitly: when AI helps evaluate a person's work, the person is owed that fact, because they're entitled to know how judgment about them was formed. And when a format's rules are genuinely unclear, grant proposals being the classic case, the agreement's answer is already written: say so and ask the owner of the rules.

As a group · 4 min

Why this matters: hearing a disclosure line read aloud tells you instantly whether it sounds honest or evasive, and calibrates yours.

  1. Pairs: pick one real output type your team ships.
  2. Run the misled test out loud and make the call: disclose or no.
  3. If it's disclose, draft the one-line disclosure and read it to the room. The room votes: honest or evasive? Rewrite until it's honest.

Flying solo? Take your most public output, run the misled test, and draft the one-line disclosure you would actually print. Say it out loud once; your ear will catch what your eye missed.

Facilitator scriptFull 10 min · Core 6

Install the misled test as the dependable disclosure rule, with the three zones: disclose (external, university's voice, judgments of people), no disclosure (owned drafts and brainstorms), and the honest middle (say so and ask).

Say

“Line three answers the question teams find most awkward: when do we tell readers AI helped? One test settles nearly every case. Imagine the reader finding out, later, exactly how this was made. Do
  1. Open with the test itself: imagine the reader learning exactly how this was made. Misled means disclose.

  4. Send them to Disclose or Not: five scenarios on devices, including the genuinely unclear funder case.
  5. Run the quick check, then the pair drill: run the test on one real output, draft the one-line disclosure, and read a few to the room for the honest-or-evasive vote.
Ask

“From the drill: read us your one-line disclosure. Room, call it: honest or evasive?”

Expect:

  • Plain winners: 'Drafted with AI assistance; reviewed by the advising team'
  • Evasive contenders: buried, hedged, or lawyer-flavored versions
  • Someone asks whether the line makes them look lazy

Respond: For the lazy worry, give the reframe from the bank: readers punish discovered concealment far harder than disclosed assistance, and a plain process line reads like a photo credit. If a piece would embarrass you when disclosed, it needs more human work, which is a line-two problem, never a reason to hide the process.

Debrief

One test, three zones, one plain sentence. Line three is the cheapest trust insurance your team can buy.

Validate: 4+ of 5 on the trainer; drafted disclosure lines are one plain sentence; the room can say the test from memory and knows the unclear-case route.

Watch for: The percentage trap: learners wanting a rule like 'disclose over 50 percent AI.' Redirect to the reader every time: a fully AI-drafted brainstorm owes nobody anything, and a lightly edited assessment may still owe its subject the truth. The test beats any threshold.

Transition

“Last line, shortest line, and it carries the other three: who owns the output.”

Section 05 · Line Four

The byline is a promise.

Line four is the shortest, and it carries the other three. The human who ships an output answers for it: every claim, every error, every reading of its tone. "The AI wrote it" has never once worked as an accountability answer, here or anywhere, which leads to the plainest rule in this course: you cannot ship what you have not read.

Line four, written down

One name per output: whoever hits send, publish, or submit has read every word and answers for all of them. The tool never appears in the accountability chain: if the output is wrong, the correction carries a person's name and the fix belongs to that person. Ownership powers the other lines: a person who must answer for an output suddenly cares what went into it, who checked it, and whether the reader was told.

Quick check: An AI-drafted FAQ answer ships with a wrong deadline and a student misses it. Who answers for that?

Practice · The Guardrails Lab

Why this matters: your team is rolling out AI help on two regular outputs: the weekly staff newsletter and a student-facing FAQ page. Write the four lines for the rollout, then see where the team stands at month one. Every choice here is one a real team has made, one way or the other.

Write all four lines
Go deeper

In practice

Notice what the lab punishes. Never AI use itself; always the missing line around it: data with no rule, checks with no name, disclosure left to chance, errors with no owner. That matches how real incidents read after the fact: the postmortem almost never says the tool failed, it says nobody had decided who was responsible for what the tool touched. Line four is the enforcement mechanism the other three borrow. When a team skips it, lines one through three decay into suggestions; when it's real, the named owner personally audits the rest, because their name is the one on the correction. That's also why collective ownership fails so reliably: an output everyone owns has nobody who must read it.

As a group · 6 min

Why this matters: walking a real output down all four lines, once, out loud, is the fastest way to find the line your team leaves to chance.

  1. Pairs: pick one output your team ships every week.
  2. Walk it down the four lines aloud: what goes in, who verifies against what, would the reader feel misled, whose name owns it.
  3. Find the line currently running on vibes, and say what the written version would be. Bring the sharpest one to the room.

Flying solo? Run your most frequent output down the four lines on paper and mark the line currently left to chance. That line is the first sentence of your team's agreement.

Section 06 · The Agreement

One page, written together.

You have all four lines. Now write them for your actual team: one page, drafted with the people it covers, living somewhere findable, revisited quarterly. When a case doesn't fit the lines, the page says who to ask, because improvised policy under deadline is how incidents happen. And where VU policy already answers a question, your page points to it instead of competing with it.

The working agreement standard

Written with the team, never announced at it; people follow lines they helped draw. One page, findable: it lives where work starts, the team drive's front page or the onboarding doc, never in an email from March. Revisited quarterly: tools change, cases accumulate, and a stale agreement teaches people to ignore it. Unclear cases route, never improvise: the page names who to ask, and every answered question becomes a new line.

Play · Draft your Four Lines, privately

Why this matters: a starter draft turns the team conversation from a blank page into an edit, and edits actually happen. Nothing you type is saved or sent anywhere; tools and situations, never people's names.

3 · Line three: your team's disclosure default.
Write both lines and pick a default
Go deeper

In practice

Your page doesn't compete with university policy; it fills the space between policy and Tuesday afternoon. FERPA questions already have the registrar's guidance. The approved-tool list is IT's to maintain, and today it reads ChatGPT EDU, Amplify, Copilot; your page points at the list rather than copying it, so it can't go stale. Research and funder rules belong to the research office. What none of those documents can reach is the everyday call: can this meeting summary go in, who reads the newsletter before it ships, does the FAQ carry a disclosure line. That's the agreement's territory, and it's why the page works best written by the people who make those calls daily. One habit worth stealing: every time an unclear case gets routed and answered, the answer becomes a new line on the page. Agreements that grow this way stay trusted, because every line earned its place.

As a group · 5 min

Why this matters: a line that survives a partner's hard case will survive the team meeting.

  1. Pairs: swap the two lines you just drafted and read each other's aloud.
  2. Stress-test each other's line one with one hard case from your real work. Does the line answer it cleanly, or does it wobble?
  3. Tighten the wording together until both lines answer their hard cases. Keep the wobble stories; they're your team meeting's best material.

Flying solo? Stress-test your own draft against the hardest case from the traffic-light trainer in section 02, and tighten each line until it answers cleanly.

Facilitator scriptFull 5 min · Core 1

Convert cards into spoken commitments, capture the Level 1 reaction check, and point at the follow-up chain.

Say

“The whole session in one breath: incidents rise with use, so the rules go in writing. What goes in follows the light. Checks are sized to blast radius and carry a name. Disclosure follows the misled test. And the human who ships it answers for it. You have a draft and a card. Before you go: your team, and your meeting day. Say it out loud; spoken commitments stick.”

Do
  1. Read the featured card: the one next step is the 20-minute Four Lines conversation with the team, this week, ending with a home and a revisit date.
  2. Run the fist-to-five (Level 1): 'On a fist to five, how confident are you making the daily calls now: what goes in, who checks, when to disclose?' Scan and note the number; the 30-day re-poll asks it again.
  3. Run the commitment round, popcorn style: team and meeting day, out loud or in chat.
  4. Point at the cheat sheet and agreement buttons; the worksheet is built to be filled in during the team conversation itself.
  5. Tell them the 7-day pulse is coming, then land the last line and stop on time.
Ask

“Around the room, one line each: your team, and your meeting day.”

Expect:

  • 'The advising front office, Thursday'
  • 'My unit's comms pair, we talk Monday'
  • Someone commits to settling their unclear case with the registrar first

Respond: Thank each one, no commentary. For the case-settler, warmly: 'Good instinct. Bring the answer in writing to the team meeting; a settled argument is the best first line a page can have.'

Debrief

The session's success metric isn't in this room; it's the 7-day pulse: how many teams had the conversation, and where the pages live now.

Validate: Fist-to-five mostly 3+ (Level 1); a majority state a team and a day out loud or in chat. Count both; with the pulse and the 30-day re-poll, that's your evidence chain.

Watch for: Cards that quietly skipped the date. The commitment round catches them: no day, no commitment; help them pick one on the spot.

Transition

“Go book the conversation. And when the team's hard cases surprise you, and they will, that surprise is the sound of the agreement starting to work.”

60-min core: Core: fist-to-five plus a fast commitment round; the ending survives every cut.
