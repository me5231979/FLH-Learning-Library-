05 · When things go sideways

If it breaks, you have a move

If Projector or the site fails mid-session: Teach from the whiteboard: the four moves, the three piles, and the template hygiene rules all draw in seconds. The inventory and capstone move to the printed cards. The lab becomes a group walk-through: read each step's three options aloud and have the room vote by hands.
If Learner devices can't join (wifi or filters): Run facilitator-only: the trainers play well on the projector with the room voting A/B/C by hands; the private inventory and capstone move to paper. You lose typing, not learning.
If You're 10+ minutes behind by the Automation Lab (05): Declare the Core path silently: pair drills become solo passes, debriefs shrink to one voice, and the closing tightens to the fist-to-five plus commitment round. Never cut the lab, the inventory, or the capstone; they carry the session's practice objectives.
If Someone types or reads out a message containing a real person's details: Protect the norm warmly: 'Hold on, swap that name for a bracket before it goes anywhere.' Then use it as the live teach: that swap IS slot hygiene, and it is the same move that keeps people data out of AI tools. The interruption becomes the lesson.
If The room is skeptical: 'AI drafts sound robotic, my messages need a human touch': Agree with the instinct and redirect it: the template is YOUR past writing, distilled; AI just finds the skeleton in it. The voice rule exists for exactly this concern, and the Judge the Template game's too-generic examples show what happens when voice is ignored. The human touch survives in the slots you fill and the read before every send.
If The room is the opposite: automation enthusiasts who want to auto-send everything today: Aim the enthusiasm at the lab and let it do the sobering: the auto-send option produces the month-two disaster on screen. Then hold the line verbally: read-then-send has no exceptions, and the evaporation trap catches enthusiasts hardest because they save the most hours and name none of them.
If Someone raises a real data-privacy concern mid-flight: Stop and honor it; this is the one interruption that outranks the agenda. Restate the traffic light (green public, yellow internal in approved VU tools only, red private information about people, never), confirm their case against it, and if it's genuinely ambiguous, park it with a promise to route it to the right owner rather than improvising policy from the front of the room.
If A learner says 'my whole job is the repetitive layer, is this course automating me away?': Take it seriously and answer honestly, in front of the room if they asked in front of the room: the course automates formats, never judgment, and every exercise ends with a human reading, deciding, and sending. The bank exists precisely so recovered hours become visible higher-value work with the learner's name on it. Then offer the private follow-up; this worry usually has a specific story behind it.

06 · Tough questions, ready answers

Say each answer out loud once before you teach

Is this how our jobs get cut? Be honest.

The honest answer has two parts. This course moves formats to machines and keeps every decision, every send, and every relationship with the person doing the work; the bank exists so the recovered hours turn into visible, higher-value work with your name on it, which is the opposite of making you disposable. And nobody teaching a 90-minute course can promise what any organization decides years from now. What you control is whether your recovered time becomes named, visible contribution or invisible slack. The first kind builds the case for you; this course is the first kind.

My messages are all different. I don't really have repeats.

That is what almost everyone says before the audit, and the sent folder almost always disagrees. The repeats hide because they arrive one at a time and each one feels like a fresh task. Run the one-week noticing habit, or just scroll yesterday's sent mail and count the messages you have written before in near-identical form. If the count is genuinely zero, congratulations, you have already escaped the repeat tax, and the calendar rules and retirement test still apply to you.

Doesn't reading every AI draft cancel out the time saved?

Reading is fast; composing is slow. A routine reply that took four minutes to write takes thirty seconds to read and correct, and the template did the composing. The math only breaks when the draft is bad enough to need a full rewrite, which is the too-generic template failure, and the fix is a better skeleton, never skipping the read. The read is also carrying your accountability: five minutes a month at the send step is what stood between the lab's strong tier and the auto-sent disaster.

Am I allowed to put my work email into AI tools?

The traffic light answers it: internal work belongs in approved VU tools only (ChatGPT EDU, Amplify, Copilot), and private information about people belongs in no AI tool at all, ever. In practice: de-identified drafts and templates of your own messages are fine in approved tools; a thread containing a student's situation or a colleague's personnel matter is red, and you handle it yourself. When you are unsure which side of the line something is on, treat it as red and ask the data governance owner, not the tool.

What if I automate something and it makes a mistake?

Plan for it, because it will, occasionally, which is exactly why the system keeps your eyes at the send step. Under read-then-send, a mistake in a draft costs you a correction; the same mistake under auto-send costs you a public error with your name on it. So the answer is the design: slots so nothing stale carries over, a read before every send, and a spot check on the numbers that change. Notice the symmetry nobody mentions: your hand-built versions had mistakes too. The template plus the read usually produces fewer.

My manager measures me by the reports I produce. Retiring one sounds career-limiting.

Which is why the test retirement puts the manager in the loop before anything stops; you are proposing an experiment together, never presenting a fait accompli. The two-sentence ask ('I suspect nobody reads this; can we skip one cycle and see?') makes you look like someone who thinks about value, and the outcome is evidence either way: nobody notices and you both recover the hours, or someone notices and you have found the real audience and can shrink the report to what they actually use. Managers overwhelmingly say yes to the version framed as a test.

Section 01 · The Repeat Tax

Your week is quietly charging you a repeat tax.

There is the work, and then there is the re-doing of formats around the work: retyping the message you sent last Tuesday, rebuilding the table you built last month, re-answering the question you answered last week. That second layer is the repeat tax, and the research says it is the single best place to point AI. Before the method, the evidence, and most rooms guess these numbers wrong.

In practice

Two sources shape this session. Section 02 · Spot It

You can't automate what you haven't noticed.

The Repeat Audit has four moves: spot it, template it, route it, retire it. The first move is a noticing habit. The rule of thumb: anything you have done more than twice the same way is a candidate. The repeats hide in plain sight because they arrive one at a time, so learn the three tells that give them away.

Tell 1 · The search

You search for a past email to copy it

The search is the confession. You already treat that message as a template; it is just unsaved, unslotted, and rebuilt by hand every time.

Tell 2 · The rebuild

You rebuild the same table monthly

Same columns, same formulas, new numbers. The structure has not changed in a year; only the data has. Structure that repeats is a skeleton waiting to be saved.

Tell 3 · The echo

You type the same three sentences weekly

The reimbursement answer, the room-booking confirmation, the where-do-I-find-it reply. If your fingers know it by heart, it belongs in a saved reply with slots.

The data rule runs here too

Your inventory will include work that touches people. The tasks go on your list; the people data does not go into AI. The traffic light from Start Smarter still decides what any tool may see:

GreenPublic or generic information. Go.
YellowInternal work. Approved VU tools only.
RedPrivate information about people. Never.

A name baked into a template is a red flag twice over: it is private data sitting in a reusable file, and it is next month's embarrassing accident. The light outranks every technique in this course.

Go deeper

In practice

Run the audit as a one-week noticing habit: every time a task feels like deja vu, write its name on a running list, ten seconds, no analysis. At the end of the week rank the list by two questions. How often does it repeat, and how identical is each repeat? Frequency times sameness is the payoff math: a daily near-identical message beats a quarterly report that changes shape every time. And keep the boundary bright while you collect: candidates that involve people can go on the list by task name, and the people details stay out of any unapproved tool. The audit needs to know THAT you send the message; it never needs to know who received it.

As a group · 5 min

Why this matters: other people's repeats are easier to spot than your own, and a partner's questions sharpen your ranking fast.

  1. Pairs. Swap your top candidate from the inventory above: the task name only, never its contents.
  2. The partner asks the two payoff questions: how often, and how identical? Push for real numbers.
  3. Bring the room the strongest candidate you heard. The list on the board is proof the tax is everywhere, and it becomes the section 03 raw material.

Flying solo? Fill in the inventory above honestly, then star the candidate you would be embarrassed to still be doing by hand in three months. That star is your capstone task.

  5. Have them build the ranked output, then run the pair swap from Go deeper: task names only, the two payoff questions, best candidate to the room.

Section 03 · Template It

Build the skeleton once. Fill the slots forever.

The second move turns a repeat into a template. The build is simple: collect three past examples of the message or report, strip the names and identifying details, and ask AI for the shared structure with a slot for everything that changes. Then edit until it sounds like you on a good day. Ten minutes of building, repaid every single time the task comes back.

Template hygiene, three rules

1. Slots, never leftovers: every name, date, and number becomes a [BRACKET]. A specific left behind is next month's accident. 2. Your voice survives: if the template does not sound like you, you will rewrite it every time, and a template you rewrite is just a tax with extra steps. 3. De-identified sources only: strip people's details from the examples before AI sees them; the traffic light outranks the build. The cautionary tale is the leftover-name incident: an office reuses last month's update, and last month's client name lands in this month's inboxes. Nobody designs that failure. A missing slot does.

Play · Judge the template

Why this matters: the difference between a template that works and one that embarrasses you is visible before you save it, if you know the tells. Five templates; call each one.

Go deeper

In practice

The build session takes one sitting. Pull three past examples of the recurring message or report; three is enough for the pattern to show and few enough to de-identify carefully. Strip every name, email address, and personal detail first; the stripped versions are the only ones AI may see. Then the ask is one sentence: here are three examples of a message I send often, give me the reusable structure with bracketed slots for everything that changes. Edit the result twice: once for the slots (did it miss a date that changes?) and once for voice (would you actually write "per my previous correspondence"? you would not). Save it where your fingers will find it, and give it a name you will remember under deadline pressure.

As a group · 5 min

Why this matters: slotting one real message out loud installs the hygiene rules better than any list of them.

  1. Pairs. One volunteers a real recurring message, read aloud de-identified.
  2. Together, mark every part that changes between sends. Each changing part gets a bracket: [NAME], [DATE], [ANSWER].
  3. Read the slotted skeleton back. The test: could it ship next month with only the brackets filled? If yes, it passes. One pair reads theirs to the room.

Flying solo? Take your first-ranked candidate from section 02, pull three past examples, and bracket what changes. You just built your first template; the lab in section 05 shows the full system it lives in.

Section 04 · Route It

Rules sort. You decide.

Templates handle what you produce. The third move handles what arrives: the inbox and the calendar. The system is three piles, sorted by one question asked in order: does this need me today, this week, or at all? AI summarizes the long threads, drafts the routine replies from your templates, and flags what looks urgent. You keep two jobs forever: the decisions, and every single send. Nothing leaves until you have read it.

Pile 1

Needs me today

A real deadline inside the day with your name on it. Small pile, handled first, before anything else gets to make noise.

Pile 2

Needs me this week

Real asks without a same-day clock. This is where your templates earn their keep: AI drafts the reply, you read it, you send it.

Pile 3

Does not need me

Newsletters, confirmations, automated notices, threads you are only copied on. The biggest pile, and the one rules can sweep before you ever see it.

The calendar gets rules too

Default lengths: 25 and 50 minutes instead of 30 and 60; the shaved minutes are yours. Focus blocks: two recurring blocks a week, named for what happens in them, defended like meetings. The decline template: two warm sentences with slots for the meeting and the alternative, saved and ready, because declining gets easy exactly when you no longer have to compose it. And the accountability line that runs through all of it: rules sort, you decide. A rule may file a message or shorten a meeting; it may never answer for you.

Play · Triage the inbox

Why this matters: the piles only save time if the sorting is instant, and instant comes from practice. Five incoming items; pick the pile.

Quick check: In the three-pile system, which job is never AI's?

Go deeper

In practice

Set the system up in this order. First, spend one morning sorting by hand so you learn your own piles; the rules you write afterward will be rules you trust. Second, write the sweep rules for pile three: senders and subjects that never need you get filed on arrival, and a weekly ten-minute scan of the swept folder catches the rare miss. Third, bring in AI for pile two: thread summaries before you read, template-based drafts before you write, in approved VU tools only, because an inbox is internal work and often brushes against people data. When a thread contains private details about a person, the summary step is off the table and you read it yourself; the light outranks the system. And the send rule keeps no exceptions for busy days, because busy days are when the leftover slot ships.

As a group · 5 min

Why this matters: sorting real mail out loud, with a partner challenging your calls, calibrates the piles faster than any definition.

  1. Pairs. Each pull up, or recall, this morning's first five emails.
  2. Sort all ten into the piles out loud. The partner challenges every "needs me today" call: what is the actual deadline?
  3. Count the room: how many of the ten were pile three? It is usually about half, and that half is what your sweep rules are for.

Flying solo? Sort your last ten emails into the three piles. Count pile three, then write the one inbox rule that would have swept those messages before you saw them. Set it up today.

Section 05 · The Automation Lab

Rebuild the monthly report cycle.

Here is a cycle most offices will recognize: the monthly program report eats the first three days of every month. You chase program leads for inputs, rebuild the summary, write the report, send it, and the meetings pile up around all of it. Redesign the cycle one step at a time, then run two months and see what your design did.

Practice · The Automation Lab

Why this matters: every choice here is one you will make on your own task this week, and the failure modes are the real ones. Choose how each step runs, then run two months.

Choose all four steps
Go deeper

In practice

Notice where the lab punishes you. Never for automating too much; always for removing your own eyes: the unread send, the undefended calendar. That mirrors how this goes wrong in real offices. The failure story is almost never "the AI wrote it badly." It is a stale number in an unread draft going out under a real person's name, or two recovered hours dissolving into meetings because nothing was standing guard over them. Month one usually goes fine, which is exactly the trap: the early wins teach you to stop reading, and month two is when the skim lets the leftover through. The counter is structural, never willpower: read-then-send as a standing rule, and calendar rules that hold when you are too busy to defend anything manually.

As a group · 6 min

Why this matters: running the four-step design on your own task, before you build it, finds the weak step while it is still free to fix.

  1. Pairs. Take your starred candidate from section 02 and design its four steps out loud: gather, write, send, calendar.
  2. The partner hunts the weak step: where would a wrong thing slip through once you stop paying attention, around month two?
  3. Fix that step with the pattern: a template, an AI first pass, read-then-send, or a standing rule. Say the fixed version aloud.

Flying solo? Write the four steps of your own candidate and mark the one where you would stop paying attention by month two. That step gets the standing rule, today.

Section 06 · Retire and Bank

The bravest move is to stop.

Some recurring work should not be automated. It should end. The report nobody has replied to in three years, the standing meeting that could be a message: automating those just makes waste faster. The audit's last move has two halves. Retire what nothing needs, safely and with your manager in the loop. Then bank the recovered hours visibly, because the calendar is already circling them.

The safe test, then the bank

Test retirement: with your manager in the loop, skip one cycle of the suspect report or meeting and watch who notices. Nobody asks? You have your answer, and restart was always one email away. Then bank it: the hour you save is invisible, and the next meeting request is not. Name what the recovered time is for in the same breath that announces the change ("the reporting time now goes to the backlog cleanup"), give it a calendar home, and it survives. Leave it unnamed and it evaporates within weeks.

Quick check: What makes a test retirement safe?

Play · Keep, shrink, or retire

Why this matters: the sorting instinct is the skill: knowing what deserves your hours, what deserves a template, and what deserves a test. Five recurring obligations; make the call.

Go deeper

In practice

The retirement conversation with your manager is two sentences: "I suspect nobody reads the monthly activity report. Can we skip one cycle and see who notices?" Managers say yes to that far more often than people expect, because the test costs nothing and the evidence is theirs too. Keep the pieces ready to restart, and if someone does notice, you have learned something better: who the real audience is, which usually shrinks the report to the one section they actually wanted. Banking works the same way at any scale: the moment an automation lands, name the hour's new purpose out loud and put it on the calendar before the meetings find it. A named hour is defensible in a scheduling conversation; a vague "I freed up some time" has already lost.

As a group · 5 min

Why this matters: saying "this might deserve to stop" out loud, in a room, is the permission most people are waiting for.

  1. Small groups: each person names one recurring obligation they suspect has no real audience or purpose.
  2. The group sorts each one, keep, shrink, or test-retire, with one sentence of reasoning per call.
  3. Every test-retire gets its manager ask drafted on the spot, two sentences, ready to send this week.

Flying solo? Pick your most suspicious recurring obligation and draft the two sentences: the ask to your manager for a one-cycle test, and the line naming where the recovered time goes if nobody notices.
