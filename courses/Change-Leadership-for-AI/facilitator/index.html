05 · When things go sideways

If it breaks, you have a move

If Projector or the site fails mid-session: Teach from the whiteboard: the four method words, the honesty rules, and the traffic light all draw in seconds. The naming-line drill and the fumble-story drill never needed a screen; the capstone moves to the printed card. The lab becomes a group walk-through: read each moment's three options aloud and have the room vote, then read the coaching for what they chose.
If Learner devices can't join (wifi or filters): Run facilitator-only: the trainers play well on the projector with the room voting A/B/C by hands; the conversation-plan builder and capstone move to paper. You lose typing, not learning.
If You're 10+ minutes behind by the Change Lab (05): Declare the Core path silently: pair drills become solo passes, debriefs shrink to one voice, and the closing tightens to the fist-to-five plus commitment round. Never cut the lab, the builder, or the capstone; they carry the session's practice objectives.
If Someone names a colleague or starts describing a specific person's fear or resistance: Protect the norm warmly: 'Hold that. Situations and roles, never names. The method works on moments, not on people.' If it keeps happening, a real team conflict is probably in the room; offer a private follow-up and steer the exercises to the role level.
If Someone in the room is visibly living the fear, not managing it: their own role is the one changing: Do not teach past them. Acknowledge it plainly and briefly: 'Some of you are on the receiving end of this change too, and everything here works in both directions.' Offer the honest two-part answer as something they deserve to hear from their own leadership, and check in privately at the break. Their presence makes the room more honest, not less.
If The room is skeptical: 'AI is overhyped, this will blow over': Don't argue futures; the course doesn't depend on any AI prediction. The frame that lands: whatever the technology does, your team's fear about it is already real, and unmanaged fear costs you today. Change leadership pays off even if the tools plateau tomorrow.
If The room is the opposite: enthusiasts who think fear talk slows everything down: Aim the enthusiasm at the arithmetic from section 03: the enthusiasts were always coming along; the change is decided by the watchful and the worried. Then let the lab do the sobering: enthusiast instincts (push adoption, declare victory) score weak, and the coaching explains why.
If A manager says 'I can't be honest; leadership hasn't told ME what's happening': Validate it; this is common and real. The honest frame still works with a bigger unknown box: 'here's what I know, here's what I don't, and here's what I'm doing to find out' is a naming conversation a manager at any level can run. What you cannot do is transmit false certainty downward; that borrows trust you'll need later.
If Someone raises a real data-privacy concern mid-flight: Stop and honor it; this is the one interruption that outranks the agenda. Restate the traffic light (green public, yellow internal in approved VU tools only, red private information about people, never), confirm their case against it, and if it's genuinely ambiguous, park it with a promise to route it to the right owner rather than improvising policy from the front of the room.

06 · Tough questions, ready answers

Say each answer out loud once before you teach

Is this how our jobs get cut? Be honest.

The honest answer has two parts, and this course exists to teach it. Part one, what anyone can know: AI changes tasks before it changes roles, and in most organizations no role decision has been made when the tools arrive. Part two, what a manager can promise: that any news about the team's roles comes from the manager first, with the team in the room, and that saved hours get a named, visible destination. What no one should promise is that nothing will change; that promise cannot be kept, and the room knows it. The difference between a team in dread and a team in motion is usually whether someone answered this question honestly, early, and as many times as it was asked.

Isn't naming the fear just making people anxious about something they hadn't worried about yet?

The fear is already there; the research and every hallway conversation say so. Gallup finds most employees have heard no clear AI plan from leadership, and people do not fill silence with calm. Naming does not create the fear; it moves it from the hallway, where it grows, to the room, where it can be answered. The one real risk is naming without framing: if you say 'I know this is scary' and change the subject, you've confirmed the fear and answered nothing. The move is always name, then frame, in the same breath.

What if I honestly don't know whether roles will change? Leadership hasn't told me either.

Then say exactly that, because it is the honest frame: 'Here's what I know, here's what I don't, and here's what I'm doing to find out.' A manager who admits the unknown and visibly chases the answer builds more trust than one who transmits reassurances they don't believe. The promise you can always keep is procedural: whatever I learn, you hear it from me first. Teams forgive uncertainty; they do not forgive discovering that certainty was faked.

My team is small and busy. Do we really need an ethics 'session'? Can't I just forward the policy?

The policy sets the floor; it cannot tell your team what the floor means for your work, and it cannot host disagreement. The session can be one hour, once, plus a revisit date; the output is one page. What the hour buys you: the team's real reasoning out loud, the ambiguous cases surfaced before they become incidents, and shared ownership of the lines instead of quiet private interpretations. A forwarded PDF teaches your team that ethics is someone else's job, and the next hard call gets made alone, in silence, by whoever is most rushed that day.

What do I do about my one hard resister? They refuse to touch AI at all.

First, reframe: resistance is information, not insubordination. Under 'not for me' there is usually a specific worry: quality, craft identity, or the suspicion that using the tool digs their own grave. Ask what specifically feels off, in a 1:1, and listen without defending. Then offer a low-stakes experiment they design; people rarely sabotage what they built. And protect them from becoming the room's example; a resister shamed in public becomes a resister forever. If nothing moves after real listening and real invitation, that becomes an ordinary performance conversation about the role's requirements, held honestly, which is a different conversation from change leadership.

Doesn't all this fear talk assume the worst? Plenty of people are excited about AI.

Both are in your room at once, and the excited ones need you less. The arithmetic from section 03: enthusiasts experiment no matter what you do; the change is decided by the watchful and the worried, who are checking what happens to the first person who fumbles. Leading the excited is easy and loud; leading the worried is quiet and decisive. And enthusiasm has its own failure mode: it can silence the anxious, which is one of the three capstone failure modes, so channel it into experiments and keep the microphone available for the quiet.

How is this different from generic change management training?

The bones are shared; the specifics are not. Three things are distinctive about AI change: the fear is existential rather than procedural (the unsaid question is about being replaced, which is why naming matters more than usual), the ground keeps moving (capabilities shift quarterly, so decisions need revisit dates and the ethics container stays open), and there is a hard data floor (the traffic light) that no amount of enthusiasm may cross. Generic change models assume the change finishes; this one is built for a change that keeps changing.

Section 01 · Why Change Fails

Announced as technology. Experienced as a threat.

Here is the pattern behind most failed AI rollouts. Leadership announces a technology: licenses, features, timelines. The team experiences something else entirely: a question about their own future that nobody in charge seems willing to say out loud. The tools were never the hard part. Before the method, the evidence, and most rooms guess these numbers wrong.

The announcement

A technology story

Rollout comms talk about capabilities, licenses, and productivity. All true, and none of it answers the question actually forming in the room.

The experience

A threat assessment

Every person hears the same unsaid question: is this how my job changes? Unanswered, that question converts into quiet resistance that no training session can reach.

The 18 percent

Led on human ground

The leaders whose AI change works differ in curiosity, courage, and connection, Accenture finds. They ask real questions, say uncomfortable truths early, and stay close enough to hear the fear.

Section 02 · Name the Fear

Say the fear out loud before it says itself.

In every meeting about AI, one question sits under the table: am I being automated? Left unnamed, it does not go away; it converts into quiet resistance, polite nodding, and pilots that mysteriously stall. The naming move takes the question off the table and puts it in the room, on your terms, with honesty rules that keep you from promising what you can't. It is the first move of the method you'll use all session: Name, Frame, Invite, Hold.

1 · Name

Say the fear first

"Some of you are wondering if this is how your job changes. Let's talk about that directly." Naming it costs you nothing and buys the room's attention back from its own dread.

2 · Frame

Honest scope

What is changing, what is not, and what you don't know yet. All three parts said out loud. Pretended certainty reads as either a lie or a threat, and the room can tell.

3 · Invite

Into the design

People support what they help build. Curiosity is the stance, questions are contributions, and the team shapes how the change lands. Section 03 builds the safety this needs.

4 · Hold

For months

Change leadership outlives the announcement. Revisit, re-answer, protect the strugglers, keep the ethics conversation open as capabilities shift. Section 06 makes it a practice.

Play · Judge the opening line

Why this matters: your first sentence about an AI change sets the ceiling on everything after it. Five manager lines announcing an AI change; call each one.

The honesty rules

Never promise that nothing will change. You can't keep it, and when anything changes, your credibility goes with it. Promise how change will be handled: with the team in the room, visibly, with any role news heard from you first. Answer the saved-hours question out loud: if AI buys back time, say what that time is for. A team that suspects saved hours convert into cut roles will make sure there are no saved hours.

Go deeper

In practice

The naming move has a shape worth practicing: state the fear as a normal thing reasonable people feel, in the second person plural ("some of you are wondering"), then go straight to the honest frame. Two traps. First, naming without framing: saying "I know this is scary" and then changing the subject is worse than silence, because you confirmed the fear and answered nothing. Second, over-promising to end the discomfort: the room's short-term relief costs you every future announcement. The Gallup data is blunt about the vacuum: where leaders communicate no clear AI plan, employees write their own, and the story they write is rarely generous.

Section 03 · Safety to Stumble

The team that can stumble can change.

Learning a new way of working means being publicly bad at something for a while, and adults avoid that at almost any cost. So here is the uncomfortable arithmetic of AI adoption: your enthusiasts will experiment no matter what you do, and the change is actually decided by the anxious quiet ones, the people watching to see what happens to the first person who fumbles. Psychological safety is the name for what they're checking, and it is built out of visible moves, yours first.

Public experiments

Trying is normal here

Experiments happen in the open, in a named low-stakes sandbox, so nobody has to succeed in secret or fail in private. What gets demoed is the attempt, and misses are part of the demo.

No-blame misses

The miss teaches the team

When AI gets something wrong, the debrief asks what the process let through, never who to blame. One person punished for an AI miss ends every experiment on the team, permanently.

The leader goes first

Your fumbles, out loud

Nothing builds safety faster than the boss saying "I tried it on my own summary and it mangled it; here's what I learned." Going first is the one move only you can make.

Go deeper

In practice

The tell is participation drift: who has stopped speaking in meetings, who never mentions the tool, who does their real work the old way after hours. Those people are rarely stubborn; they are usually protecting themselves from a fumble they believe is unaffordable. Section 04 · The Ethics Conversation

The ethics conversation needs a host.

Every team using AI is already making ethics calls: what gets pasted into a tool, what ships without review, what feels wrong even though no rule forbids it. The only question is whether those calls happen in the open. Hosting the conversation means your team sits down, together, and works out where AI is fine for your work, where it is not, and why. Policy documents set the floor; the conversation decides what the floor means here. Outsourcing it to a PDF teaches your team that ethics is someone else's job.

Disagreement is material

Mine it, gently

When two people disagree about whether an AI use is acceptable, you've found exactly where your team's line needs drawing. Treat the disagreement as the agenda, never as an obstruction to get past.

Escalate the real ones

Know what's yours to decide

Some questions belong to policy owners: legal exposure, student data, anything the university governs centrally. The host's job is sorting: settle what's local, route what isn't, and never improvise policy from the front of the room.

The floor under every conversation

Your team's ethics session starts from the traffic light, the data rule shared across all the AI courses. Whatever your team decides, it decides above this floor:

GreenPublic or generic information. Go.
YellowInternal work. Approved VU tools only.
RedPrivate information about people. Never.

The light outranks enthusiasm, deadlines, and every technique in this course. If a proposed use touches private information about people, the conversation is over before it starts.

Quick check: In the ethics session, someone proposes running advising notes about named students through an AI tool to save time. What's the call?

Go deeper

In practice

Two hosting rules carry it. Ask before ruling: "where would you draw the line, and why?" gets you the team's real reasoning; announcing your line first gets you compliance and silence. Section 06 · Hold the Arc

The announcement was day one. The change takes months.

Most change leadership ends when the announcement does, and that is why most change fails politely, months later, where nobody is looking. Holding is the fourth move of the method and the least glamorous: keeping the container steady while capabilities shift, attention fades, and the fear comes back wearing new clothes. It runs on four habits, and on measuring the right thing: trust, alongside usage.

The revisit cadence

Reopen on purpose

A monthly pulse on the change: what's working, what isn't, what the tool can do now that it couldn't. Decisions get revisit dates, because a line drawn for last quarter's capabilities is quietly wrong by this one.

The quiet signals

Watch who stopped talking

Who has gone silent in meetings, who never mentions the tool, who brings you polish and never problems. Silence is the loudest data a team produces, and dashboards can't hear it.

Courage as a habit

Say the true thing early

The uncomfortable update, delivered before it's forced: what's behind schedule, what the tool still gets wrong, what you don't know yet. Every early truth makes the next one cheaper.

Connection as the multiplier

The 1:1 is the instrument

Real fears surface one-on-one, months after the meeting where everyone nodded. The fear question, asked plainly and heard without defensiveness, is your best sensor for how the change is actually going.

Quick check: Four months in, which of these says the change is actually healthy?
