Change That Sticks · Vanderbilt

The hard part of AI change
was never the AI.

Accenture's research on leadership in the AI era finds that only around 18 percent of leaders lead AI investments effectively, and what separates them has almost nothing to do with tool fluency. The differentiators are human: curiosity, courage, and connection. Your team's AI change will be decided in conversations: in the question nobody asks out loud, in whether people feel safe to stumble, and in whether you host the hard topics or hide from them. This session trains you to lead those conversations.

By the end of this session, you will be able to

  1. Explain why AI change fails on human ground rather than technical ground, and what the effective 18 percent do differently
  2. Name the fear and the job-security question directly, without false promises, using the honesty rules
  3. Build psychological safety around experimenting with AI: public experiments, no-blame misses, and going first
  4. Facilitate your team's own ethics and governance conversation instead of outsourcing it to a policy document
  5. Sustain change leadership over months: the revisit cadence, the quiet signals, and measuring trust alongside usage

Section 01 · Why Change Fails

Announced as technology. Experienced as a threat.

Here is the pattern behind most failed AI rollouts. Leadership announces a technology: licenses, features, timelines. The team experiences something else entirely: a question about their own future that nobody in charge seems willing to say out loud. The tools were never the hard part. Before the method, the evidence, and most rooms guess these numbers wrong.

The announcement

A technology story

Rollout comms talk about capabilities, licenses, and productivity. All true, and none of it answers the question actually forming in the room.

The experience

A threat assessment

Every person hears the same unsaid question: is this how my job changes? Unanswered, that question converts into quiet resistance that no training session can reach.

The 18 percent

Led on human ground

The leaders whose AI change works differ in curiosity, courage, and connection, Accenture finds. They ask real questions, say uncomfortable truths early, and stay close enough to hear the fear.

In practice

The evidence base for this session: Accenture's research on leadership in the age of AI finds only around 18 percent of leaders lead AI investments effectively, and the differentiators it names are curiosity, courage, and connection rather than technical depth. Gallup's workplace research fills in the employee side: most employees say leadership has communicated no clear plan for AI, and trust in leadership runs low enough that a vague announcement gets filled with the scariest available story. This course is the human layer of a pair: Workflow & Process Redesign covers the mechanics of where AI fits in the work, and Leading the Shift in the Course Library covers modeling the behavior yourself. Today is about the conversations neither of those can have for you.

As a group · 4 min

Why this matters: the announced-versus-experienced gap becomes personal the moment you look for it in your own history.

  1. In pairs: recall one workplace change you lived through that was announced as good news and received as bad news.
  2. Name what the announcement talked about, and what the room actually worried about. Two lists, usually with no overlap.
  3. Hands to the room: how many of those worries were ever addressed out loud? Count. The silence you just counted is the job this session trains.

Flying solo? Write down the AI change nearest your team. Two columns: what the official story says, and what your most anxious team member is probably thinking. The gap between the columns is your first conversation.

Section 02 · Name the Fear

Say the fear out loud before it says itself.

In every meeting about AI, one question sits under the table: am I being automated? Left unnamed, it does not go away; it converts into quiet resistance, polite nodding, and pilots that mysteriously stall. The naming move takes the question off the table and puts it in the room, on your terms, with honesty rules that keep you from promising what you can't. It is the first move of the method you'll use all session: Name, Frame, Invite, Hold.

1 · Name

Say the fear first

"Some of you are wondering if this is how your job changes. Let's talk about that directly." Naming it costs you nothing and buys the room's attention back from its own dread.

2 · Frame

Honest scope

What is changing, what is not, and what you don't know yet. All three parts said out loud. Pretended certainty reads as either a lie or a threat, and the room can tell.

3 · Invite

Into the design

People support what they help build. Curiosity is the stance, questions are contributions, and the team shapes how the change lands. Section 03 builds the safety this needs.

4 · Hold

For months

Change leadership outlives the announcement. Revisit, re-answer, protect the strugglers, keep the ethics conversation open as capabilities shift. Section 06 makes it a practice.

Play · Judge the opening line

Why this matters: your first sentence about an AI change sets the ceiling on everything after it. Five manager lines announcing an AI change; call each one.

The honesty rules

Never promise that nothing will change. You can't keep it, and when anything changes, your credibility goes with it. Promise how change will be handled: with the team in the room, visibly, with any role news heard from you first. Answer the saved-hours question out loud: if AI buys back time, say what that time is for. A team that suspects saved hours convert into cut roles will make sure there are no saved hours.

Go deeper

In practice

The naming move has a shape worth practicing: state the fear as a normal thing reasonable people feel, in the second person plural ("some of you are wondering"), then go straight to the honest frame. Two traps. First, naming without framing: saying "I know this is scary" and then changing the subject is worse than silence, because you confirmed the fear and answered nothing. Second, over-promising to end the discomfort: the room's short-term relief costs you every future announcement. The Gallup data is blunt about the vacuum: where leaders communicate no clear AI plan, employees write their own, and the story they write is rarely generous.

As a group · 5 min

Why this matters: the naming line feels risky until you've said one out loud and watched it land.

  1. Pairs. Each person drafts one naming line for a real AI change near their team: the fear said plainly, then the honest frame in a sentence.
  2. Say it to your partner as if they were your team. The partner answers with the hardest follow-up question they can think of.
  3. Two or three volunteers say their line to the room. After each, the room calls it: would you believe this manager?

Flying solo? Write your naming line and say it out loud twice. Then write the hardest question your team could ask back, and draft your honest answer to it, both parts: what you know, and what you promise.

Section 03 · Safety to Stumble

The team that can stumble can change.

Learning a new way of working means being publicly bad at something for a while, and adults avoid that at almost any cost. So here is the uncomfortable arithmetic of AI adoption: your enthusiasts will experiment no matter what you do, and the change is actually decided by the anxious quiet ones, the people watching to see what happens to the first person who fumbles. Psychological safety is the name for what they're checking, and it is built out of visible moves, yours first.

Public experiments

Trying is normal here

Experiments happen in the open, in a named low-stakes sandbox, so nobody has to succeed in secret or fail in private. What gets demoed is the attempt, and misses are part of the demo.

No-blame misses

The miss teaches the team

When AI gets something wrong, the debrief asks what the process let through, never who to blame. One person punished for an AI miss ends every experiment on the team, permanently.

The leader goes first

Your fumbles, out loud

Nothing builds safety faster than the boss saying "I tried it on my own summary and it mangled it; here's what I learned." Going first is the one move only you can make.

Go deeper

In practice

Watch the quiet ones, because they are the real vote. Enthusiast energy is loud and misleading; a rollout can look thriving while half the team has silently opted out. The tell is participation drift: who has stopped speaking in meetings, who never mentions the tool, who does their real work the old way after hours. Those people are rarely stubborn; they are usually protecting themselves from a fumble they believe is unaffordable. The fix is connection, one conversation at a time, and modeling: Leading the Shift, this course's sibling in the Course Library, goes deep on going first. And if your team needs the full workshop on speaking up, Building Brave Teams runs as this course's companion session.

As a group · 5 min

Why this matters: a fumble story you've told once in a safe room is available forever after in the rooms that count.

  1. Pairs. Tell your partner one real story of you getting something wrong with AI, or with any new tool. Thirty seconds, ordinary voice.
  2. Partner reflects back what the story made them feel about experimenting near you.
  3. Room round: what makes a leader's fumble story land as safety rather than as performance? Collect three answers.

Flying solo? Write the fumble story you would tell your team, three sentences: what you tried, what went wrong, what you do differently now. Say it out loud once. That story is now in your pocket for the next team meeting.

Section 04 · The Ethics Conversation

The ethics conversation needs a host.

Every team using AI is already making ethics calls: what gets pasted into a tool, what ships without review, what feels wrong even though no rule forbids it. The only question is whether those calls happen in the open. Hosting the conversation means your team sits down, together, and works out where AI is fine for your work, where it is not, and why. Policy documents set the floor; the conversation decides what the floor means here. Outsourcing it to a PDF teaches your team that ethics is someone else's job.

Disagreement is material

Mine it, gently

When two people disagree about whether an AI use is acceptable, you've found exactly where your team's line needs drawing. Treat the disagreement as the agenda, never as an obstruction to get past.

Escalate the real ones

Know what's yours to decide

Some questions belong to policy owners: legal exposure, student data, anything the university governs centrally. The host's job is sorting: settle what's local, route what isn't, and never improvise policy from the front of the room.

The floor under every conversation

Your team's ethics session starts from the traffic light, the data rule shared across all the AI courses. Whatever your team decides, it decides above this floor:

GreenPublic or generic information. Go.
YellowInternal work. Approved VU tools only.
RedPrivate information about people. Never.

The light outranks enthusiasm, deadlines, and every technique in this course. If a proposed use touches private information about people, the conversation is over before it starts.

Quick check: In the ethics session, someone proposes running advising notes about named students through an AI tool to save time. What's the call?

Go deeper

In practice

A workable first ethics session runs an hour and produces one page: the uses we're comfortable with, the uses we're not, the open questions we're escalating, and the date we'll revisit. Two hosting rules carry it. Ask before ruling: "where would you draw the line, and why?" gets you the team's real reasoning; announcing your line first gets you compliance and silence. And date the output: capabilities shift every few months, so a decision without a revisit date quietly becomes wrong. The revisit is where this connects to section 06, because the ethics container is one of the things you hold, for months, as the ground moves.

As a group · 5 min

Why this matters: hearing where other reasonable people draw the line recalibrates your own faster than any policy read-through.

  1. Pairs. One scenario: AI drafts your team's stakeholder updates, and a recipient asks whether a machine wrote what they received. What does your team say?
  2. Each person answers, then defends the answer for thirty seconds. Notice where you differ; that difference is what a real ethics session mines.
  3. Room round: collect the split. Then the harder question: who should decide this, your team or a policy owner? Sort it out loud.

Flying solo? Answer the disclosure question yourself, in writing: would your team tell recipients when AI drafted an update, and why? Then mark whether that call is local or belongs to a policy owner. You've just run the first two minutes of your ethics session.

Six months. One team. Your calls.

Your department is rolling out an AI tool that drafts the team's routine reports. You lead a team of six: two enthusiasts, three watchful, one openly worried. Go deeper

In practice

Notice what the lab rewards. Never enthusiasm, and never caution; always the container: fear named before it names itself, the job question answered with both honest parts, the fumble turned into the team's lesson instead of one person's shame, and the drift met with re-invitation instead of enforcement. Notice also what the weak outcomes share: the dashboard looks fine. Compliance theater is the signature failure of AI change because usage is easy to mandate and trust is not, so a team can perform adoption for months while quietly routing around the tool. If your rollout metrics look perfect and your meetings have gone quiet, the lab says you're in the weak timeline and don't know it yet.

As a group · 6 min

Why this matters: finding the moment you'd most likely fumble, before it arrives, is the cheapest leadership improvement available.

  1. Pairs. Compare your lab runs: where did your choices differ, and which moment cost you points?
  2. Each person names the real-life moment they're most likely to play weak, and says why: usually time pressure, discomfort, or wanting the meeting to end well.
  3. Swap counter-moves: your partner tells you what they'd want to hear from their manager in that exact moment. Write the line down.

Flying solo? Rerun the lab and deliberately play your honest self, the choices you'd actually make under pressure, then read what month six looks like. The gap between that run and your best run is your development edge, named.

Section 06 · Hold the Arc

The announcement was day one. The change takes months.

Most change leadership ends when the announcement does, and that is why most change fails politely, months later, where nobody is looking. Holding is the fourth move of the method and the least glamorous: keeping the container steady while capabilities shift, attention fades, and the fear comes back wearing new clothes. It runs on four habits, and on measuring the right thing: trust, alongside usage.

The revisit cadence

Reopen on purpose

A monthly pulse on the change: what's working, what isn't, what the tool can do now that it couldn't. Decisions get revisit dates, because a line drawn for last quarter's capabilities is quietly wrong by this one.

The quiet signals

Watch who stopped talking

Who has gone silent in meetings, who never mentions the tool, who brings you polish and never problems. Silence is the loudest data a team produces, and dashboards can't hear it.

Courage as a habit

Say the true thing early

The uncomfortable update, delivered before it's forced: what's behind schedule, what the tool still gets wrong, what you don't know yet. Every early truth makes the next one cheaper.

Connection as the multiplier

The 1:1 is the instrument

Real fears surface one-on-one, months after the meeting where everyone nodded. The fear question, asked plainly and heard without defensiveness, is your best sensor for how the change is actually going.

Go deeper

In practice

Measuring trust sounds soft until you operationalize it. Three countable signals: who speaks in team meetings (count it across a month; the trend is the data), what surfaces in 1:1s (fears and misses arriving means the channel works; polish means it doesn't), and whether problems reach you directly instead of through someone else. Pair those with usage and you can actually see the difference between adoption and the performance of adoption. And re-answer the job question every time it comes back. It will come back, because fear doesn't file your previous answers; the manager who answers it fresh in month five, both parts, as patiently as on day one, is doing the highest-leverage minute of change leadership there is.

As a group · 4 min

Why this matters: the quiet signals are easy to agree with and hard to actually watch; naming yours in front of a witness makes it real.

  1. Pairs. Each person names, as a role only, the team member they'd watch first: who would go quiet if this change frightened them?
  2. Say what "quiet" specifically looks like for that person: shorter answers, camera off, no questions.
  3. Trade first moves: what would you do in the next two weeks to open that channel? The 1:1 fear question is the default; find a better one if you can.

Flying solo? Write your two quietest people as roles, and next to each: what their silence would look like, and the date of your next 1:1 with them. If that date is more than two weeks out, move it.
