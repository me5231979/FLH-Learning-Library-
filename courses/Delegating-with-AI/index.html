Delegating, Rethought · Vanderbilt

Who gets the work is now
a design decision.

The same task can now go three places: to AI, to a team member who would grow on it, or it stays with you. Stanford GSB's research describes the shift plainly: leaders in the AI era redesign roles and processes rather than simply oversee execution. Route everything routine to AI and you hollow out the apprenticeship your juniors learn by. Route nothing and you burn your team's hours on work a machine finishes in minutes. This session teaches the routing decision, and the role redesign behind it.

By the end of this session, you will be able to

  1. Apply the Routing Test's three questions, growth, judgment, volume, to the real tasks crossing your desk
  2. Distinguish stretch work from grunt work before you route either one
  3. Protect the apprenticeship pipeline as AI absorbs the tasks juniors used to learn by
  4. Redesign a role around what grows, judgment reps, client contact, verification ownership, instead of just shrinking its task list
  5. Brief a delegation, to a person or to AI, so it lands with a clear outcome and named checks

The data rule · the same card in every AI course

Before anything goes into an AI tool, one three-second question: what color is this?

🟢
Green · Public or generic
Questions about public topics, generic drafts, brainstorming, "explain this to me." Any approved tool.
🟡
Yellow · Internal work
Your drafts, internal announcements, budgets, grant drafts, internal policies. Approved Vanderbilt tools only, signed in with your VU account. Never a personal account or a random free site.
🔴
Red · Private information about people
Names with grades, health details, personnel issues, passwords, anything confidential about a real person. No AI tool, full stop.

Inside the light: Vanderbilt classifies data in levels, and each approved tool has a ceiling. ChatGPT EDU (VU account) is approved up to Level 2. Amplify and Microsoft Copilot (VU account) are approved for Level 3 work. Children's names, photos, and records go in no AI tool.

The hard cases: ask "who is this about" before "how sensitive does it feel." A student's essay is about a student. Meeting notes naming colleagues are about colleagues until the names come out. A recommendation letter is about its subject.

Never, in any tool: Social Security numbers · Student records or grades (FERPA) · Health information (HIPAA) · Children's names, photos, and records · Salaries and personnel files · Passwords and credentials · Anything confidential about a real person.

This card is the working shorthand for Vanderbilt IT's data classification and approved-tool guidance: vanderbilt.edu/it/policies. When the two ever disagree, the policy wins.

Section 01 · The Triage

Every task has three destinations.

A task lands on your desk. The old question was who has capacity. The new question is where the task should live: with AI, with a person it would grow, or with you. Nobody trained managers for this triage, and both reflexes fail. Default everything routine to AI and the tasks your juniors learn by quietly disappear. Default everything to people and your team spends hours on work a machine finishes in minutes. Before the method, the evidence: play the pattern and see how fast this landed on your desk.

To AI

The mechanical layer

Repetition with catchable errors: pulls, formats, first-pass summaries. Fast and tireless, and it only works with a named human verifier behind it.

To a person, as stretch

The growth layer

Work that is hard because it is new. Slower today, and it builds capability that compounds. This is the layer default-to-AI managers quietly delete.

Keep it

The judgment layer

Context, relationships, accountability that is genuinely yours. Smaller than it feels: most managers keep far more than this layer actually holds.

Why this matters: a finding you guessed at, and missed, sticks far better than one you were shown. Four findings on how fast this triage arrived; call each one before the reveal.

Quick check: A task lands that AI could do in minutes and a junior could learn from in days. What does the default-to-AI reflex actually cost?

In practice

The numbers in the game come from Gallup's workplace AI research and Gallup, 2026 (employees whose manager actively supports AI use are 1.7x as likely to use it weekly): US employees using AI at work at least a few times a year nearly doubled in two years to about 40 percent, daily use doubled to about 8 percent, and only around 22 percent say their organization has communicated a clear plan for it. The management gap is the story: the routing is already happening on your team, mostly by reflex. Stanford GSB's insights on leading through the AI shift name where the fix lives: leaders redesign roles and processes rather than simply oversee execution. Which means the triage on this slide is not extra work on top of your job. Increasingly, it is the job.

As a group · 4 min

Why this matters: the hollow-team failure mode becomes real the moment you find a reflex-routing on your own team.

  1. In pairs: name one task that moved to AI on your team in the last few months, officially or quietly.
  2. Ask the question nobody asked at the time: could a junior have grown on it, and what would they have learned?
  3. Hands to the room: how many of those moves were decisions, and how many were reflexes? Count it out loud. Most rooms are nearly all reflexes, which is exactly the gap in Gallup's plan number.

Flying solo? List three tasks that moved to AI around you in the last few months. For each, write one line: what would a junior have learned by doing it? Any task with a real answer goes on your watch list for the rest of this session.

Section 02 · The Routing Test

Three questions, and the order matters.

Here is the method. For every task, three questions, always in this order: growth first, judgment second, volume last. The order is the whole trick. Volume is the loudest question and the least important, because speed is visible this week and growth is visible next year. Ask growth first and the meeting minutes and the junior's first memo stop looking identical.

1 · The growth question

Would doing this grow a person?

If yes, stretch beats speed: the task goes to a person even when AI is faster. Speed today is not worth a team that never learns. This is the question AI-era managers forget.

2 · The judgment question

Does it need context, relationships, or accountability?

Then it stays human, and sometimes stays yours. Relationships hear who shows up; accountability keeps a name on the outcome.

3 · The volume question

Is it mechanical repetition with catchable errors?

That is AI's lane, and it comes with a rule: a named verifier, every time. AI's lane is real; it is just the last lane you check, never the first.

Play · Route the task

Why this matters: the test is easy to recite and harder to run under time pressure. Five tasks from a manager's real week; run the three questions in order and route each one.

The data rule outranks the test

The Routing Test decides where a task goes. The traffic light from Start Smarter decides what AI may see, and it applies before any task enters the AI lane:

GreenPublic or generic information. Go.
YellowInternal work. Approved VU tools only.
RedPrivate information about people. Never.

If a task touches private information about people, performance notes, salaries, health, anything, the AI lane is closed, whatever the volume question says. The light outranks the test.

Go deeper

In practice

Run the questions out of order once and you'll see why the order is the method. Volume-first makes the meeting minutes and the junior analyst's first board memo look identical: both are mechanical enough for a machine to draft. Growth-first separates them in one breath: nobody grows on transcription, and the memo is a rep the junior needs to earn. The other trap is treating "keep it" as the safe default. Keeping is also a routing decision, and it has a cost: every familiar task you hold is either judgment or hoarding, and the test tells you which. When a task passes the growth question, the test is over; you do not get to keep asking until you hear the answer you wanted.

As a group · 5 min

Why this matters: the test only becomes a habit once you have run it aloud on your own work, disagreements included.

  1. Pairs. Each person picks two real tasks from their current week: one they were about to hand to AI, one they were planning to keep.
  2. Run each task through the three questions out loud, in order. Your partner holds you to the order.
  3. Flag any task where question one and question three disagree, and bring the sharpest one to the room. That disagreement is where managers earn their pay.

Flying solo? Take two tasks from your week: one headed for AI, one you planned to keep. Write the three answers for each, in order. If growth and volume disagree on either task, mark it: that task is your capstone candidate.

Section 03 · Stretch vs Grunt

Hard because it's new, or hard because it's long?

This distinction decides careers. Stretch work is hard because it is new: the first client call, the first memo with your name on it. Grunt work is hard because it is long: the fortieth identical data pull. The growth question runs on telling them apart, and there is a trap built into this decade: the tasks juniors used to learn the trade on, first drafts, research passes, are exactly the tasks AI absorbs first. Yesterday's apprenticeship is today's AI lane.

Stretch work

Hard because it's new

Scary in a useful way, feedback attached, and it leaves a capability behind. Slower than AI today, and it is how seniors get made.

Grunt work

Hard because it's long

Repetition with no lesson after the early reps. The honest test: what did rep 30 teach that rep 3 did not? If the answer is nothing, it is grunt.

The trap

Yesterday's stretch is today's AI lane

First drafts and research passes taught the trade for decades. AI now does them in minutes. Route them away without building new stretch and you get seniors with no successors.

Play · Stretch or grunt

Why this matters: managers grade tasks from the manager's chair, and the distinction only works from the junior's. Five tasks seen through a junior's eyes; call each one.

Go deeper

In practice

The junior-pipeline problem deserves an honest statement: the industry has not solved it. The tasks that made today's seniors, the first drafts, the research grind, the long apprenticeship of low-stakes attempts, are being absorbed by AI faster than anyone is building replacements. What a single manager can control is building new stretch deliberately, one role at a time. Three reliable sources of it: verification ownership, where checking the AI's output against sources becomes a judgment rep with a signature on it; earlier client exposure, because AI absorbing the prep frees calendar for the meetings themselves; and judgment reps with feedback, small real calls made by the junior, reasoning shown, correction heard. None of these existed on the old task list. All of them are better teachers than the fortieth data pull ever was.

As a group · 5 min

Why this matters: naming the reps you learned by, and noticing which ones AI now does, makes the pipeline problem concrete enough to fix.

  1. Pairs: each person names the two or three tasks they personally learned the most from, coming up in their field.
  2. Mark the ones AI now does in minutes. Most pairs mark more than half.
  3. For each marked task, invent its replacement rep: what could a junior own today that teaches the same judgment? Bring your best replacement to the room.

Flying solo? Write the three tasks you learned the most from early in your career, and mark any AI now does. For each mark, write the replacement rep you could hand a junior this month: verification ownership, a client seat, or a real call with feedback.

Section 04 · Role Math

Redesign the role, not just the task list.

As AI absorbs a role's routine layer, something has to fill the space. Weak redesign just shrinks the list, and the person hears the honest version of it fast: part of my job is gone. Role math is the stronger move: name what drains out, name what backfills, judgment reps, client contact, verification ownership, and have the conversation about the role's new shape, including the sentence managers keep skipping: what the freed capacity is for.

The role-math rule

For every routine chunk AI absorbs, name the backfill before you announce the change. What backfills is what grows: more judgment reps, more verification ownership, more human contact. Design it so what remains is more valuable than what left, and say out loud what the freed capacity is for. A role redesigned with its owner grows; a role shrunk from a distance quietly starts job hunting.

Quick check: AI just absorbed a third of a role's routine work. What is the role-math move?

Go deeper

In practice

The role-math conversation has three sentences, delivered together. What drains: here is the routine chunk AI is taking, with a named verifier from day one. What backfills: here is what you get back, tied to where you told me you want to grow. What the freed capacity is for: here is the work we can finally do. Skip the third sentence and the person invents a scarier version, because everyone knows freed hours go somewhere. One boundary keeps the conversation honest: do not promise what the organization decides above you. What you can promise is the part you control: that this role's redesign happens with its owner, that the backfill is real work with feedback attached, and that the person leaves the conversation more valuable than they entered it.

As a group · 5 min

Why this matters: the conversation is easy to plan and hard to say; hearing it out loud once is worth three plans.

  1. Pairs. One person is the manager and delivers the three sentences for the role they just sketched; the partner plays the role's owner hearing it for the first time.
  2. The partner answers honestly: which sentence did you believe least?
  3. Swap and repeat, then bring the room the sentence that was hardest to say convincingly. It is almost always the third.

Flying solo? Write the three sentences for the role you sketched: what drains, what backfills, what the freed capacity is for. Read them aloud once. Whichever sentence you stumbled on is the one to rewrite before the real conversation.

Section 05 · The Lab

Route the quarter.

Week one of the quarter, and four tasks land at once: the recurring data pull, the new client's kickoff deck, the process documentation nobody owns, and an escalation from an angry partner. You have AI, a junior analyst hungry for real work, a senior analyst ready for more, and yourself. Route all four, then see your team at quarter end.

Practice · The Routing Lab

Why this matters: every routing here is one you will make on real work this month, and the quarter-end outcomes are the ones that actually happen. Route each task, then run the quarter. Moves open one at a time, and your first move shapes what comes next.

Route all four tasks
Go deeper

In practice

Notice where the lab punishes you. Never for using AI too much or too little; always for routing by reflex: the comfort-keep, the capacity-dump, the AI handoff with no verifier. And notice how the two big failure modes sound at quarter end. The overloaded manager fails loudly: missed dates, visible bottlenecks, everyone can hear it. The hollowed team fails silently: everything ships, the junior is bored, nobody grew, and the cost only becomes audible a year later when there is no one ready for the next role. Silence is the more dangerous sound, which is why the growth question goes first.

As a group · 6 min

Why this matters: the hardest routing on your real plate is the one that feels too senior to share, and it deserves the room's eyes.

  1. Pairs. Each person names the task from their own plate most like the partner escalation: it feels like it must stay with you.
  2. Partner runs the test on it out loud: is it truly the judgment layer, or is a piece of it shareable, an observer seat, the follow-up plan, the next one like it?
  3. Bring the room the best split you found: what stays, what becomes someone's stretch.

Flying solo? Name the task on your plate that feels least routable. Write what makes it yours in one line, then write the piece of it someone could own: the observer seat, the follow-up, or the next one like it. If you cannot find any piece, you found the judgment layer; if you found one, you found a stretch assignment.

Section 06 · The Handoff

The routing is half. The handoff is the rest.

A right routing with a bad handoff still fails. Delegation to a person needs four things said out loud: the outcome, the context, the checkpoints, and the right to struggle productively without being rescued. Delegation to AI needs two: the draft assignment and the named verifier, because a delegation to AI is really a delegation to the verifier. And one thing never changes, whichever way you route: you own what ships.

Briefing a person

Four lines, said out loud

The outcome: what done looks like. The context: why it matters and where it fits. The checkpoints: when you will look, agreed up front. The struggle room: no rescue at the first wobble; the struggle is the lesson.

Briefing AI

A draft and a verifier

The draft assignment: what it produces, from which inputs, to what standard. The named verifier: who checks, against what source, with the power to reject. No verifier, no handoff.

What never changes

You own what ships

Delegation moves the work, never the accountability. The junior's memo, the AI's summary, the verifier's miss: when it reaches the world, it carries your name.

Quick check: What is a delegation to AI, really?

Play · Judge the handoff

Why this matters: your team can already tell these apart; this is your calibration. Five delegation briefs, to people and to AI; call each one.

Go deeper

In practice

Productive struggle has a calibration: space checkpoints by stakes, closer when a client is watching, wider when the cost of a wobble is private. And learn the tell that separates struggling from drowning: struggle produces attempts, drowning produces silence. Check for attempts, never for comfort; a stretch assignment is supposed to be uncomfortable. On the AI side, the verifier's craft, checking a draft against sources, sizing the check to the stakes, logging the misses, gets a full session of its own: the Workflow & Process Redesign course in this series teaches the draft, verify, decide pattern that runs the AI lane. It pairs well with this one.

As a group · 5 min

Why this matters: a brief that sounds complete in your head almost always has a missing line, and the room can hear which one.

  1. One volunteer drafts aloud the brief for the lab's kickoff deck, going to the senior analyst as stretch. Four lines: outcome, context, checkpoints, struggle room.
  2. The room checks the lines off as it hears them, then names the missing or weakest one.
  3. Rebuild the weak line together, then a second volunteer briefs the AI version of the data pull: draft assignment plus named verifier, sources and reject path included.

Flying solo? Write the four-line brief for the task you flagged in section 02: outcome, context, checkpoints, struggle room. If it is going to AI, write the two-line version instead: the draft assignment, then the verifier with their source and their power to reject.
