05 · When things go sideways

If it breaks, you have a move

If Projector or the site fails mid-session: Teach from the whiteboard: the two sides of the line, the traffic light, and the rubric skeleton all draw in seconds. The audit and anchor drills never needed a screen; the capstone moves to the printed card. The lab becomes a group walk-through: read each moment's three options aloud and have the room vote.
If Learner devices can't join (wifi or filters): Run facilitator-only: the trainers play well on the projector with the room voting A/B/C by hands; the private structure builder and capstone move to paper. You lose typing, not learning.
If You're 10+ minutes behind by the Hiring Lab (05): Declare the Core path silently: pair drills become solo passes, debriefs shrink to one voice, and the closing tightens to the fist-to-five plus commitment round. Never cut the lab, the structure builder, or the capstone; they carry the session's practice objectives.
If Someone names a real candidate or starts describing an identifiable person: Protect the norm warmly: 'Hold that. Roles and processes, never names; this session practices the exact discipline it teaches.' If it keeps happening, it usually means a live, painful search is in the room; offer a private follow-up and steer the room back to structures.
If Someone admits, publicly, that they have already pasted candidate materials into a chatbot: Thank them, genuinely; that honesty is the most useful moment the session can produce. No blame, one clean path: delete the conversations, don't repeat it, and know the escalation point if the material was sensitive. Then aim the room at the swap: every red-side urge has a green-side twin, and their case becomes the worked example.
If The room pushes back: 'AI screening is obviously coming; this line is temporary': Don't argue futures; argue jurisdiction. Vendor tools with audits and HR governance may well come, and that decision belongs to HR and University policy, never to an individual manager with a chatbot. The line in this course is about what managers do with general-purpose tools today, and that line is stable however the vendor market moves.
If The room is the opposite: so worried about AI they won't touch the green side either: Anchor on the structure dividend: rubrics and structured interviews were best practice before AI existed; AI just makes building them cheap. Run the Fix the Posting trainer next; watching proxies fall out of a posting usually converts the cautious, because the fairness gain is visible and no person was ever near the tool.
If A real, specific data-privacy incident surfaces mid-flight: Stop and honor it; this outranks the agenda. Restate the traffic light, confirm the case against it, and route it: their HR contact and VU policy own incidents, and you don't improvise remediation from the front of the room. Park it with a named follow-up and move on.

06 · Tough questions, ready answers

Say each answer out loud once before you teach

I already pasted resumes into a chatbot last month. How bad is this, and what do I do?

Thank you for saying it, and you are not alone; the tools arrived faster than the guidance did. Three moves: stop, so it doesn't become a habit; delete the conversations in the tool's history; and if the materials were sensitive or the paste shaped a real decision, tell your HR contact so the University hears it from you rather than from a complaint. Then take the energy that made the shortcut tempting and spend it on the green side: the rubric and the audit solve the same time problem without touching a person.

HR uses screening software already. Why is my chatbot different?

Governance is the difference. An enterprise applicant system runs under contracts, data protections, audits, and HR accountability, and even those tools are facing new bias-audit laws. A manager pasting resumes into a general-purpose chatbot has none of that: no data agreement covering candidate materials, no audit trail, no accountable owner, and a model that was never validated for employment decisions. Same-looking act, entirely different legal and ethical footing. When a vendor tool tempts you, the move is to route it to HR, which is exactly what this course asks.

If AI is biased, aren't humans more biased? At least the machine is consistent.

Consistency is the problem, not the defense. A biased human interviewer harms the candidates they touch, can be noticed, coached, and outvoted by a panel; a biased model applies the same skew to every candidate in the pile, silently, at scale, with no one watching a specific decision. Amazon's screener is the clean example: the bias came FROM the human hiring history it learned on, and automation made it systematic. The honest answer to human bias is the structure this course teaches, which is auditable at every step; automation of the verdict is bias with the lights off.

The rubric and structured interviews sound slow. My searches are already too long.

The structure is front-loaded, and that's the trick: thirty minutes before the posting opens, when nothing is urgent yet. It pays itself back inside the same search: screens go faster because the must-haves are written down, interviews stop meandering because the questions exist, and the debrief shortens because you're comparing evidence instead of relitigating impressions. What actually makes searches long is the second round you run because the first one produced a shortlist nobody could defend. And AI collapses the build cost: the drafting that used to take an afternoon now takes minutes, with your judgment on top.

Can I use AI on interview notes if I remove the names first?

Removing the name usually isn't removing the person. Notes carry employer, dates, projects, and specifics that identify someone in a small pool almost immediately, and a hiring pool is always small. The honest test: could anyone identify the person from this text plus context? For interview notes the answer is nearly always yes, so treat them as red. The legitimate version of the urge is upstream: use AI to build a better note-taking template before interview day, when nobody exists in it.

What about using AI to write the rejection and offer emails? That's about a person too.

The template is process; the person is red. Draft the generic rejection or offer template in an approved tool, with brackets where the specifics go, and make it as warm and clear as you like; that's legitimate yellow work. Then add the person's name and details yourself, outside the tool. The test from section 02 runs here unchanged: the moment a specific candidate is identifiable in the text, the paste is over the line, even for a kind purpose like softening a no.

Does the skills map risk labeling my current team? That's people data too.

Run it at the role level and it stays clean: what capabilities does the TEAM have, what does the work ahead need, where are the gaps. That's yellow, in approved tools. What you don't do is feed in performance reviews or assessments of named individuals; that crosses into red exactly like candidate materials do. If the map makes you want to develop a specific person toward a gap, wonderful, and that conversation happens with the person, not with the tool.

Section 01 · The Temptation

The shortcut that isn't yours to take.

Forty applications, a busy week, and a chatbot that reads faster than you do. The temptation makes perfect sense, and it fails three separate ways at once: the paste puts private information about people into a tool, the ranking is a screen nobody can audit or explain, and automated employment decisions are exactly where regulators and courts are moving. Here is the reframe the whole session runs on: the screening was never where AI's value lived anyway. The valuable work sits upstream and around the decision, and it is entirely yours to take.

1 · Privacy

Red data, full stop

A resume, an application, a reference: each one is private information about a person, which the traffic light marks red, never. There is no volume of applications that changes the color.

2 · Fairness

A screen nobody can audit

A chatbot's shortlist can't tell you why candidate twelve sank. Whatever patterns it absorbed from its training data just made an unexplainable call about a real person, under your name.

3 · Legal

Regulated territory

Automated employment decisions carry legal weight, and the rules are multiplying: bias audits, candidate notice, vendor scrutiny. The safest position is also the fairest one: the decision stays yours.

Section 02 · The Red Side

The never list, in full.

Two rules define the red side. First, the data rule: resumes, applications, references, interview notes about named candidates, anything that identifies a person in a hiring process never enters an AI tool. Second, the decision rule: AI never ranks, scores, or screens candidates, in any tool, approved or otherwise. The subtle cases are where good managers slip, so we'll take those one at a time.

The traffic light, applied to hiring
GreenPublic or generic information. Go. In hiring: the public posting, generic role material, question drafts for a role.
YellowInternal work. Approved VU tools only (ChatGPT EDU, Amplify, Copilot). In hiring: templates, rubrics, skills maps, nothing naming a person.
RedPrivate information about people. Never. In hiring: everything about a candidate or colleague as a person, in any tool, always.

This course touches people data constantly, so hear the rule plainly: the light outranks every technique taught today. When a use and the light disagree, the light wins.

Red

The reference call summary

A reference call is one person talking about another. Asking AI to summarize your notes puts both of them into the tool. Summarize it yourself; it's one call.

Red

Your gut notes on a candidate

Messy impressions about a named person are still information about a person. Fix your note template with AI before interview day, while nobody is in it.

Rewrite, then yellow

The rejection email tone check

"Just checking tone" on an email naming the candidate and their interview is a red paste. Strip the name and the specifics first; the generic draft is yellow work in approved tools.

Section 03 · The Wide Funnel

Write the post that invites.

Most job postings are accidental filters: recycled requirements, insider jargon, and a degree line nobody questioned, all quietly shrinking the pool before a single human judges anything. This is the first place AI genuinely earns its keep in hiring, because a posting is a process artifact with nobody in it. Green and yellow territory, high leverage, zero people data.

1 · The skills map

Know the gap first

Before writing the post, map the team: what it can do, what the work ahead needs, where the gaps are. AI structures the map fast, and the posting gets written to the gap instead of to the last person who held the job.

2 · The requirement audit

Interrogate every line

Ask of each requirement: does this describe the work, or the person we're picturing? "Digital native," "native English speaker," and unexamined degree lines are proxies that shrink pools for reasons unrelated to the job.

3 · The plain-language pass

Lower the reading wall

AI checks reading level, flags insider jargon, and rewrites in plain language. Strong candidates outside your bubble stop bouncing off a posting only insiders could parse.

Play · Fix the posting

Why this matters: the audit is a skill, and it calibrates fast. Five lines from real-world job postings; name each one's problem, or clear it.

Go deeper

In practice

The working sequence: skills map first, then the audit, then the rewrite. The map is yellow work, real internal detail with no named people, in approved VU tools, and Vanderbilt's talent-mobility push makes it doubly useful: sometimes the map shows the gap is growable from inside the team, and the posting you were about to write becomes a development conversation instead. Section 04 · The Structure

Decide how you'll decide, first.

Here is the structure dividend: same questions for every candidate, scores anchored to defined evidence, and a rubric built before any application is read. Decades of selection research back structure as hiring's most reliable fairness tool, and it doubles as your best AI move, because questions, anchors, and rubrics are process artifacts AI drafts brilliantly, with nobody in them.

The before-candidates rule

Everything structural gets built before the first application is read: the questions, the 1-to-5 anchors, the weights. Once candidates are in view, the structure freezes. The reason is human, and researchers see it constantly: structure built after you've met people bends toward the person you already like. The timing is also what keeps AI's help here green and yellow: a rubric written before candidates exist contains no candidate.

Quick check: Why does the rubric get built before any application is read?

Go deeper

In practice

The AI workflow for structure, start to finish, in approved VU tools with no people data: feed the role's real tasks and ask for behavioral questions tied to each one, in the "tell me about a time" shape, with follow-ups probing the candidate's specific part. Then ask for 1-to-5 anchor descriptions per question: what concrete evidence makes an answer a 2, what makes it a 5. Then turn AI against its own draft: which anchors are ambiguous, where would two raters score the same answer differently, which questions could a polished talker ace without the skill? Rewrite until a stranger could apply the rubric cold. That stranger test is the whole standard: anchors that need you present to interpret them are opinions wearing a rubric's clothes.

Section 06 · Bias, Watched

Bias doesn't knock. It drifts in.

Staying on the green side does not make the work bias-proof. AI-drafted questions inherit the stereotypes of everything written before them, rubric anchors can quietly encode one culture's communication style, and "culture fit" stands ready to launder "reminds me of us" into a criterion. The guardrails are cheap and specific, and they all work the same way: they force definition before judgment.

Door 1 · Inheritance

The stereotyped draft

Ask AI for leadership questions and watch every one assume taking charge in a room. The draft inherited a pattern, and the role might need quiet coordination instead.

Door 2 · The anchor

One culture's yardstick

"Polished, confident, commands the room" scores a communication style, and style tracks culture. Applied equally to everyone, it is still the wrong yardstick unless commanding rooms is the actual job.

Door 3 · The laundromat

"Culture fit"

Undefined, fit means "reminds me of us" wearing a professional word. Defined as specific observable behaviors, it can be legitimate. The definition is the whole difference.

The guardrails

Diverse review: a second reader from a different background reviews AI-drafted questions and anchors before use. The same-evidence test: identical facts get identical scrutiny, whoever they belong to. Why before rank: write the evidence-based reasons down before any ranking happens, never after. And the hard rule: anything automated that touches candidates goes through HR and VU policy first, always.

Quick check: "Culture fit" becomes a bias laundromat when...

Play · Spot the bias door

Why this matters: every one of these setups looks legitimate from a distance, which is exactly how bias travels. Five setups; find the open door in each.

Go deeper

In practice

Running the guardrails costs minutes. Diverse review means one colleague who differs from you in background or working style reads the AI-drafted questions and anchors before any candidate sees them, hunting for one thing: whose way of being good at this job do these assume? The same-evidence test runs in the debrief: for any fact that moved a score, a gap, a job hop, a credential, ask whether the identical fact got identical treatment on every candidate. Why-before-rank is a discipline of sequence: scores and their evidence get written per candidate, against the rubric, before anyone utters an ordering. And keep the escalation rule bright: the moment anything automated touches candidates, a vendor tool, a screening add-on, a scoring plugin, it goes to HR and VU policy before any pilot, because the legal landscape SHRM tracks is moving fast, and it is moving toward audits and accountability.

  4. Point at field 3, what you will NOT do, as the honest one: everyone in the room has one of these three temptations, and naming it is the defense.
  5. Push the build moment and have them read the final row to themselves: the line, standing, is the pledge they take out of the room.
Ask

“What's most likely to make you skip your first move, and what's your plan for that obstacle?”

Expect:

  • No time; the search will start before the structure exists
  • The posting is HR's template and feels untouchable
  • The team already uses AI shortcuts and pushing back feels awkward

Respond: No time: 'The structure session is 30 minutes against a search that runs for weeks; do it the day the requisition is approved.' The template: 'The requirements inside it are yours; audit those, and bring HR the rewrite rather than the complaint.' The shortcuts: 'Lead with the swap, never the scold: here's the green-side tool that solves the same problem. The lab's violation outcome is your visual aid if you need one.'

Debrief

One decision on your team now has structure coming before candidates, a held line, and a date. That is what fair looks like from the manager's chair.

Validate: Every learner builds a card (all four fields). The card plus the 7-day pulse plus the 30-day re-poll is the evidence chain; the count of first moves actually made is the headline metric.

Watch for: Vague decisions ('improve our hiring'). Push to ONE role or one live decision with a real date. And watch for the learner who picks 'take the pile out of AI today' quietly; find them after and thank them privately; that card is the session working exactly as designed.

Transition

“Reference shelf in one breath, and then the send-off.”

60-min core: Core: protect at full length; never cut the capstone.
