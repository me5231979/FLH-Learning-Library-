Hiring, Human · Vanderbilt

Let AI build the process.
Never the verdict.

The temptation sits in your inbox: a pile of applications and a chatbot that could read them in seconds. That move is off the table, and this session opens by drawing the line hard: candidate materials are private information about people, and no AI ever ranks, scores, or screens a person here. Then it teaches everything valuable on the right side of that line, because decades of selection research keep landing on the same answer: structure is the strongest fairness tool hiring has, and AI happens to be excellent at building structure and terrible at judging people.

By the end of this session, you will be able to

  1. Classify the materials in a hiring process with the traffic light and defend the red line, including the cases that feel harmless
  2. Use AI to draft job descriptions and structured interviews that widen the pool instead of quietly shrinking it
  3. Build a scoring rubric before any application is read, with anchors a stranger could apply
  4. Run a skills map of your team so the next posting names what the team actually lacks
  5. Recognize where bias enters AI-assisted hiring and apply the guardrails that catch it

Section 01 · The Temptation

The shortcut that isn't yours to take.

Forty applications, a busy week, and a chatbot that reads faster than you do. The temptation makes perfect sense, and it fails three separate ways at once: the paste puts private information about people into a tool, the ranking is a screen nobody can audit or explain, and automated employment decisions are exactly where regulators and courts are moving. Here is the reframe the whole session runs on: the screening was never where AI's value lived anyway. The valuable work sits upstream and around the decision, and it is entirely yours to take.

1 · Privacy

Red data, full stop

A resume, an application, a reference: each one is private information about a person, which the traffic light marks red, never. There is no volume of applications that changes the color.

2 · Fairness

A screen nobody can audit

A chatbot's shortlist can't tell you why candidate twelve sank. Whatever patterns it absorbed from its training data just made an unexplainable call about a real person, under your name.

3 · Legal

Regulated territory

Automated employment decisions carry legal weight, and the rules are multiplying: bias audits, candidate notice, vendor scrutiny. The safest position is also the fairest one: the decision stays yours.

In practice

The landscape behind the game: SHRM's AI research tracks steadily climbing AI use in HR, with recruiting the most common application, alongside the shifting legal landscape around automated employment decisions. The Stanford AI Index documents the other side: AI regulation rising sharply year over year, with decisions about people drawing scrutiny first. Amazon's scrapped resume screener remains the canonical case study: a top engineering organization trained a model on its own hiring history, watched it learn the bias in that history, and could not audit the bias out. The lesson for a manager is bracing: if they couldn't make automated screening fair, a chatbot with your applicant pile certainly won't. Meanwhile Vanderbilt's own talent-mobility work is making skills mapping a live manager activity, which is exactly the kind of upstream work AI does help with. That is the line this course walks.

As a group · 4 min

Why this matters: the temptation is only manageable once it's named out loud, and every manager in the room has felt some version of it.

  1. In pairs: name one moment where AI touching a hiring or talent decision was tempting, for you or someone you've seen. Roles and processes, no names.
  2. Call it together: which side of the line was it on? The person side, or the process side?
  3. Hands to the room: person side or process side? Most temptations land on the person side, which is exactly why the line has to be drawn before the busy week arrives.

Flying solo? Write down the AI-in-hiring move you've been most tempted by, then sort it: does it touch a person's materials, or does it build the process? If it touches a person, the next two sections give you the replacement.

Section 02 · The Red Side

The never list, in full.

Two rules define the red side. First, the data rule: resumes, applications, references, interview notes about named candidates, anything that identifies a person in a hiring process never enters an AI tool. Second, the decision rule: AI never ranks, scores, or screens candidates, in any tool, approved or otherwise. The subtle cases are where good managers slip, so we'll take those one at a time.

The traffic light, applied to hiring
GreenPublic or generic information. Go. In hiring: the public posting, generic role material, question drafts for a role.
YellowInternal work. Approved VU tools only (ChatGPT EDU, Amplify, Copilot). In hiring: templates, rubrics, skills maps, nothing naming a person.
RedPrivate information about people. Never. In hiring: everything about a candidate or colleague as a person, in any tool, always.

This course touches people data constantly, so hear the rule plainly: the light outranks every technique taught today. When a use and the light disagree, the light wins.

Red

The reference call summary

A reference call is one person talking about another. Asking AI to summarize your notes puts both of them into the tool. Summarize it yourself; it's one call.

Red

Your gut notes on a candidate

Messy impressions about a named person are still information about a person. Fix your note template with AI before interview day, while nobody is in it.

Rewrite, then yellow

The rejection email tone check

"Just checking tone" on an email naming the candidate and their interview is a red paste. Strip the name and the specifics first; the generic draft is yellow work in approved tools.

Yellow

The offer letter template

No names, no personal details, pure process artifact: yellow, in approved VU tools. The moment a person's details enter the text, the color changes.

Go deeper

In practice

The test that sorts every case in one breath: could a specific person be identified from what you are about to paste? If yes, it never goes in, whatever the deadline. And when you catch the temptation, use the swap: almost every red-side urge has a green-side twin that solves the same problem. Tempted to summarize interview notes? The real need is a better note template, which AI builds happily with nobody in it. Tempted to compare finalists in a chatbot? The real need is the rubric you should have built before you met them. The swap converts guilt into method. One more habit worth building now: when a tool, vendor, or well-meaning colleague proposes anything automated that touches candidates, the answer is a referral to HR and VU policy, every time, before any pilot. For the broader policy picture, the Guardrails & Responsible Use course in the Course Library is this course's general-purpose sibling; today is its hiring deep-dive.

As a group · 5 min

Why this matters: a never list your team wrote is a never list your team keeps.

  1. Pairs: draft your unit's hiring never list, five lines, starting from the two rules on this slide.
  2. Trade lists with another pair. Hunt for the gap: what subtle case did they miss? The tone check and the reference call are the usual escapees.
  3. Read the sharpest addition to the room. One sentence each; you're building the local law together.

Flying solo? Write your own five-line never list from this slide, then add the subtle case most likely to tempt you personally. Keep it where you'll see it during your next search.

Section 03 · The Wide Funnel

Write the post that invites.

Most job postings are accidental filters: recycled requirements, insider jargon, and a degree line nobody questioned, all quietly shrinking the pool before a single human judges anything. This is the first place AI genuinely earns its keep in hiring, because a posting is a process artifact with nobody in it. Green and yellow territory, high leverage, zero people data.

1 · The skills map

Know the gap first

Before writing the post, map the team: what it can do, what the work ahead needs, where the gaps are. AI structures the map fast, and the posting gets written to the gap instead of to the last person who held the job.

2 · The requirement audit

Interrogate every line

Ask of each requirement: does this describe the work, or the person we're picturing? "Digital native," "native English speaker," and unexamined degree lines are proxies that shrink pools for reasons unrelated to the job.

3 · The plain-language pass

Lower the reading wall

AI checks reading level, flags insider jargon, and rewrites in plain language. Strong candidates outside your bubble stop bouncing off a posting only insiders could parse.

Play · Fix the posting

Why this matters: the audit is a skill, and it calibrates fast. Five lines from real-world job postings; name each one's problem, or clear it.

Go deeper

In practice

The working sequence: skills map first, then the audit, then the rewrite. The map is yellow work, real internal detail with no named people, in approved VU tools, and Vanderbilt's talent-mobility push makes it doubly useful: sometimes the map shows the gap is growable from inside the team, and the posting you were about to write becomes a development conversation instead. For the audit, paste the posting draft and ask directly: which of these requirements are proxies, what could screen out capable people who took a nonstandard route, what does this read like at a ninth-grade reading level? Then judge the suggestions yourself; AI drafts the rewrite and you decide what the role truly requires. Keep one discipline: every requirement that survives should trace to a real task the role performs. If nobody can name the task, the requirement is a picture of a person, and it goes.

As a group · 5 min

Why this matters: auditing someone else's posting builds the eye faster than auditing your own, because you have no attachment to its lines.

  1. Pairs: each person recalls one requirement line from a posting they've written or worked under. Roles only, no names.
  2. Audit each other's line with the question: does this describe the work, or the person being pictured?
  3. Rewrite the weakest line together as a task the role actually performs, and read the before-and-after to the room.

Flying solo? Pull up the last posting for a role on your team. Audit its top three requirements: work, or picture? Rewrite the worst one as a real task, and keep the rewrite for section 04.

Section 04 · The Structure

Decide how you'll decide, first.

Here is the structure dividend: same questions for every candidate, scores anchored to defined evidence, and a rubric built before any application is read. Decades of selection research back structure as hiring's most reliable fairness tool, and it doubles as your best AI move, because questions, anchors, and rubrics are process artifacts AI drafts brilliantly, with nobody in them.

The before-candidates rule

Everything structural gets built before the first application is read: the questions, the 1-to-5 anchors, the weights. Once candidates are in view, the structure freezes. The reason is human, and researchers see it constantly: structure built after you've met people bends toward the person you already like. The timing is also what keeps AI's help here green and yellow: a rubric written before candidates exist contains no candidate.

Go deeper

In practice

The AI workflow for structure, start to finish, in approved VU tools with no people data: feed the role's real tasks and ask for behavioral questions tied to each one, in the "tell me about a time" shape, with follow-ups probing the candidate's specific part. Then ask for 1-to-5 anchor descriptions per question: what concrete evidence makes an answer a 2, what makes it a 5. Then turn AI against its own draft: which anchors are ambiguous, where would two raters score the same answer differently, which questions could a polished talker ace without the skill? Rewrite until a stranger could apply the rubric cold. That stranger test is the whole standard: anchors that need you present to interpret them are opinions wearing a rubric's clothes.

As a group · 6 min

Why this matters: ambiguous anchors feel precise to their author, and the fastest cure is a stranger's reading.

  1. Pairs: swap the structure plans you just built.
  2. Each partner drafts a 5-level anchor for the other's probe quality: what would a 2 sound like, what would a 5 sound like?
  3. Stress-test one anchor aloud: would two raters score the same answer the same way? If no, tighten the evidence words until they would.

Flying solo? Draft the 2 and the 5 anchor for your own probe quality, then apply the stranger test: could someone who has never met you score an answer with just your words? Rewrite until yes.

Go deeper

In practice

Notice what the lab punishes. Gut choices cost you quality: the recycled posting refills the pool you had, vibe interviews measure confidence, and the felt-right decision rebuilds the team you already have. The chatbot screen costs something categorically different: a privacy violation that no later step can repair, because every decision downstream inherits it. That asymmetry is the deepest thing this course teaches. Process quality is a dial you can improve next time; the red line is a door, and it only swings one way. When you weigh a shortcut on a real requisition, ask which kind of mistake it risks: a dial mistake, or a door mistake. Dials forgive. Doors don't.

As a group · 6 min

Why this matters: your real process almost certainly contains one of the lab's weak choices, and finding it here costs nothing.

  2. Find its weakest moment against the lab's strong options: where did structure give way to gut?

Section 06 · Bias, Watched

Bias doesn't knock. It drifts in.

Staying on the green side does not make the work bias-proof. AI-drafted questions inherit the stereotypes of everything written before them, rubric anchors can quietly encode one culture's communication style, and "culture fit" stands ready to launder "reminds me of us" into a criterion. The guardrails are cheap and specific, and they all work the same way: they force definition before judgment.

Door 1 · Inheritance

The stereotyped draft

Ask AI for leadership questions and watch every one assume taking charge in a room. The draft inherited a pattern, and the role might need quiet coordination instead.

Door 2 · The anchor

One culture's yardstick

"Polished, confident, commands the room" scores a communication style, and style tracks culture. Applied equally to everyone, it is still the wrong yardstick unless commanding rooms is the actual job.

Door 3 · The laundromat

"Culture fit"

Undefined, fit means "reminds me of us" wearing a professional word. Defined as specific observable behaviors, it can be legitimate. The definition is the whole difference.

The guardrails

Diverse review: a second reader from a different background reviews AI-drafted questions and anchors before use. The same-evidence test: identical facts get identical scrutiny, whoever they belong to. Why before rank: write the evidence-based reasons down before any ranking happens, never after. And the hard rule: anything automated that touches candidates goes through HR and VU policy first, always.

Go deeper

In practice

Running the guardrails costs minutes. Diverse review means one colleague who differs from you in background or working style reads the AI-drafted questions and anchors before any candidate sees them, hunting for one thing: whose way of being good at this job do these assume? The same-evidence test runs in the debrief: for any fact that moved a score, a gap, a job hop, a credential, ask whether the identical fact got identical treatment on every candidate. Why-before-rank is a discipline of sequence: scores and their evidence get written per candidate, against the rubric, before anyone utters an ordering. And keep the escalation rule bright: the moment anything automated touches candidates, a vendor tool, a screening add-on, a scoring plugin, it goes to HR and VU policy before any pilot, because the legal landscape SHRM tracks is moving fast, and it is moving toward audits and accountability.

As a group · 5 min

Why this matters: the guardrails become real the first time you run one on your own material.

  1. Pairs: take the anchor you drafted in section 04's activity.
  2. Run door 2 on it: does any word in it score a style rather than the job's real demand? Circle the suspects.
  3. Rewrite the worst word as observable evidence, and read the before-and-after to the room. "Confident" usually becomes something like "explains the finding clearly and answers challenges with data."

Flying solo? Audit your own section 04 anchor for style words: confident, polished, energetic, professional. Rewrite each as observable evidence of the job's real demand.
