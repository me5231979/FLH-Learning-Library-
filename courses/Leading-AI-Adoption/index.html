Making It Normal · Vanderbilt

Teams don't adopt tools.
They adopt behavior.

Gallup's workplace AI research keeps landing on the same two predictors of whether a team really uses AI: the manager uses it visibly, and it lives inside real work. The licenses are bought and the tools are everywhere; what most teams are missing is a leader who shows their own use out loud, says the rules explicitly, and keeps it on the agenda after the novelty fades. Only 22 percent of employees say their organization has communicated a clear plan for AI. Everyone else is watching you and guessing.

By the end of this session, you will be able to

  1. Explain why a manager's visible AI use predicts team adoption better than mandates, licenses, or memos
  2. Model your own AI use out loud, including the checks you ran and the misses you caught
  3. Give your team explicit permission and explicit limits, with the traffic light as the hard edge
  4. Install AI norms into the 1:1s and team meetings you already run, without adding a single meeting
  5. Sustain adoption past the novelty cliff with protected learning time and metrics that tell stories

Section 01 · The Modeling Effect

Your team adopts what it sees.

Most organizations pulled the obvious levers first: buy licenses, send a mandate, run an all-hands. Usage barely moved. Gallup's workplace research points somewhere less comfortable: the strongest predictors of a team actually using AI are a manager who visibly uses it and AI wired into real workflows. The tool budget matters less than what your team watches you do on a Tuesday. Before the method, the evidence; most rooms guess these numbers wrong.

What barely moves usage

Mandates and licenses

Tool access is nearly universal and adoption still stalls. A memo tells people AI exists; it gives nobody a reason, a rulebook, or an example to copy.

What moves it

A manager who shows their work

Opening the laptop and walking the team through a real prompt, a real draft, and a real fix. Visible use plus real-workflow integration outpredicts every other lever.

The permission gap

Silence reads two ways

Most employees have never been told what AI use is encouraged. Half your team reads that silence as prohibition; the other half improvises rules in the dark.

Go deeper

In practice

The numbers in the game come from Gallup's workplace AI research: about 40 percent of U.S. employees now use AI at work, roughly double the share of two years earlier, and 19 percent use it frequently. Only 22 percent say their organization has communicated a clear plan or strategy for it, and employees whose leadership has communicated one are 4.7 times as likely to feel comfortable using AI at work. Read those together and the management story writes itself: usage is spreading person by person, in silence, and comfort follows leadership behavior. The permission gap is not an HR abstraction. It is the specific people on your team who would use AI tomorrow if they were sure you wanted them to.

As a group · 4 min

Why this matters: the permission gap turns personal the moment you look for it on your own team.

  1. In pairs: has anyone on your team ever heard you say, in plain words, what AI use you encourage? Yes or no, honestly.
  2. Now the other side: could anyone on your team describe one way YOU use AI? If they couldn't, your use is invisible.
  3. Hands to the room on both questions. Count out loud. The distance between those counts and the room's headcount is the permission gap, sitting right here.

Flying solo? Answer both questions for yourself in writing: has your team heard your encouraged list, and could they describe one way you use AI? Every no you write down is a move this session will hand you.

The core idea

Your team is watching what you do with it, not what you say about it.

Section 02 · Show

Model it out loud, misses included.

The most contagious behavior a manager has is narrated use: "I drafted this with AI, here is the prompt, and here is what I fixed before it went out." Ninety seconds of that teaches more than a semester of encouragement, because it shows the tool, the standard, and the check in one pass. And the check is the point. If your team only ever sees you being fast, they will copy the speed and skip the verification, and the first shipped error will get blamed on the tool.

1 · Narrate the prompt

Show what you asked

The actual prompt, on the screen, with a sentence on why you wrote it that way. Prompts are invisible craft until somebody senior shows theirs.

2 · Narrate the fix

Show what you checked

The draft next to what you shipped, and the two things you corrected against the source. This is the behavior that matters most, so it's the one to model hardest.

3 · Narrate the miss

Show what it got wrong

Say the miss out loud and treat the catch as the win. A leader who admits AI misses makes imperfect use safe in public, which is where learning happens.

Play · Judge the model

Why this matters: your team can't tell you what your modeling looks like from the outside, but you can train your own ear. Five manager behaviors; call each one.

Go deeper

In practice

The narration is four sentences and you can script it tonight: here is the task, here is the prompt I used, here is what the draft got right and what I fixed, and here is why I checked it against the source instead of trusting how it read. Pick a task that's real and slightly imperfect; a flawless demo models nothing because nobody's Tuesday is flawless. Two traps to avoid: performing expertise, where the demo becomes a lecture on prompt wizardry and AI starts to look like a specialist skill, and performing speed, where the takeaway becomes "this is fast" instead of "this is checked". You are aiming for ordinary. The day AI use on your team is boring, you've won.

As a group · 5 min

Why this matters: narrating to one colleague today is the rehearsal for narrating to your team this week.

  1. Pairs. Each person narrates one real AI use from their last two weeks: the task, the prompt, the draft, the fix. No hypotheticals.
  2. The listener's job: catch whether the check appeared. If the story went straight from draft to done, ask "and what did you verify?"
  3. Swap. Then each pair reports one word to the room: was the check in the story, yes or no?

Flying solo? Write your four-sentence narration for one real AI use from your last two weeks: task, prompt, fix, and what you checked. Read it out loud once; that's the version your team hears this week.

Section 03 · Say

Nobody follows rules they've never heard.

Silence about AI is still a policy; it's just one you didn't choose. Half your team hears it as prohibition and quietly abstains. The other half hears it as a free-for-all and improvises rules you'd never approve. The fix is one explicit conversation, repeated until it's boring: what's encouraged, what's never OK, and who to ask when it's unclear. You'll draft yours in a minute.

The permission talk, three parts

1. The encouraged list: two or three uses you actively want, named specifically, with the check that goes with each. 2. The never list: the hard limits, stated as plainly as the encouragements. 3. The gray zone: who to ask when a case fits neither list, plus the promise that asking is applauded and never punished. Then say all three again next month, because a norm heard once is a rumor.

The never list starts here

Whatever else goes on your lists, the traffic light from the AI courses is the floor, and it outranks every technique in this course:

GreenPublic or generic information. Go.
YellowInternal work. Approved VU tools only (ChatGPT EDU, Amplify, Copilot).
RedPrivate information about people. Never.

If your team's work touches information about people, red is the first line of your never list, said out loud, in your voice, more than once.

Go deeper

In practice

Delivery decides whether the talk lands. Give it five minutes in a team meeting, in your own words, with your own examples; a forwarded policy PDF is silence wearing a costume. Expect two reactions and welcome both: the relieved ("finally, I've been using it for months") and the wary ("so it's really OK?"). The wary ones are your permission gap closing in real time, so answer them warmly and specifically. Then repeat the talk on a schedule: once at launch, once a month later, and every time someone new joins, because new hires arrive mid-silence and read it the same two wrong ways. When someone brings you a gray-zone case, thank them in public. The asking is the norm working.

As a group · 5 min

Why this matters: a limit that sounds clear in your head can sound ambiguous out loud, and it's better to find out here.

  1. Pairs. Read your partner your encouraged line and your never line, exactly as you typed them.
  2. The partner answers one question: "What would your team still be unsure about after hearing that?" Sharpen the fuzzy phrase they point at.
  3. Two or three volunteers read a never line to the room. Listen for the difference between a plain line and a hedge.

Flying solo? Read your three lines out loud and mark the one phrase a new hire could misread. Rewrite that phrase until it survives being heard once, in a hallway, by someone having a busy day.

Section 04 · Set

Norms live in rituals, not memos.

A norm is a behavior that happens on a schedule, which means it needs a home on the calendar you already have. The good news: your 1:1s and team meetings are already there, and installing AI into them costs minutes. One precondition sits under everything on this slide: psychological safety. Nobody shares a miss in a team that punishes misses, and every ritual below runs on people telling the truth about what went wrong.

1 · The 1:1 question

Two minutes, every week

"What did you try with AI this week, and what did it get wrong?" The second half is the clever part: it assumes misses exist and makes reporting them normal.

2 · The share-one round

One use, out loud

Two minutes of an existing team meeting: one person shares one real use, prompt and fix included. Rotate it so the quiet members present too, and misses count as shares.

3 · The miss log

Catches, without blame

A shared list of what AI got wrong and who caught it. No names on the miss, full credit on the catch. The log turns verification into a visible team sport.

4 · The pairing move

Enthusiast meets skeptic

Put your keenest user and your sharpest doubter on one real task together. The skeptic's standards become verification; the enthusiast's speed picks up a check.

Play · Fix the ritual

Why this matters: rituals fail in predictable ways, and the failure is usually visible in the setup. Five team setups; call what's missing, or call it working.

Quick check: Why does the miss log have to be blame-free to work at all?

Go deeper

In practice

Install one ritual at a time and let it run four weeks before adding the next; a team hit with four new norms at once hears an initiative, and initiatives end. Start with the 1:1 question, because it's private, cheap, and it feeds you intelligence: within a month you'll know who's experimenting, who's stuck, and who's sitting silent in the permission gap. Go first on everything. Log the first miss yourself, take the first share-one slot yourself, and let the team watch nothing bad happen to you. And keep the rituals small on purpose. Two minutes that happen every week beat a monthly AI hour that dies at the first busy season.

As a group · 6 min

Why this matters: the opener you script here is the one you'll actually say in your next team meeting.

  1. Pick your first ritual: the 1:1 question, the share-one round, or the miss log. One only.
  2. Script its opening line, the sentence that introduces it to your team, in writing. Include the safety promise out loud.
  3. Pairs: read your line; your partner plays the team's skeptic and reacts honestly. Adjust, then two volunteers read theirs to the room.

Flying solo? Pick your first ritual and script its opening line, including the safety promise. Say it out loud twice; the second time is noticeably better, and that's the one your team gets.

Section 05 · The Adoption Lab

Ninety days, six people, your call.

You lead a six-person team. The tools are licensed, leadership wants adoption, and nothing else is decided. Two of your people are curious, three are waiting to see what you do, and one is openly skeptical. Four leadership choices shape what day 90 looks like. Make them, then run the ninety days.

Choose all four moves
Go deeper

In practice

Notice what the lab never punishes you for: how much AI your team uses. Every penalty lands on leadership behavior: use nobody could see, rules nobody heard, rituals that lived nowhere, and a skeptic treated as a problem instead of a resource. That mirrors the research: adoption follows the manager's visible behavior and the team's real workflows, and it stalls in silence. The two-power-users outcome deserves special attention, because it's the most common one in the wild and it photographs like success in a status report. Two enthusiasts producing impressive demos while four colleagues quietly opt out is a divided team wearing an adoption costume, and month six is when the bill arrives.

As a group · 5 min

Why this matters: the lab's choices are diagnostic; where your real team sits today is written in which options felt familiar.

  1. Show of hands by tier: who reached whole-team practice, who got the two-speeds outcome, who stalled?
  2. One learner from the stalled tier reads their coach notes aloud; the room listens for the single cheapest fix.
  3. Pairs: name which of the four choices your REAL team is living right now, and what this week's version of the stronger option would be.

Flying solo? Rerun the lab as your real ninety days: pick the options closest to what you actually do today, read the coaching, then rerun with the one change you could make this week. The delta between the two runs is your plan.

Section 06 · Sustain

Month three is the real test.

Every adoption curve has the same dip. Novelty carries months one and two; then workload returns, the share-one round goes quiet, and usage slides back toward the two people who never needed encouragement. The dip is normal. Whether it becomes the end of the story depends on three things you control: protected time, what you celebrate, and how you measure.

Protect the time

An hour a week is a budget

Learning time treated as a perk dies at the first deadline. Treat it as a budget you defend: on the calendar, protected like a client meeting, spent on real tasks.

Celebrate the catch

Caught errors are wins

When someone catches an AI miss before it ships, that's the system working. Praise it publicly and the whole team relearns what the win actually is: the check.

Stories, never surveillance

Report adoption as stories

Uses shared, misses caught, hours returned to better work. Never leaderboard individuals; a usage ranking converts a learning norm into surveillance in one week.

Quick check: Usage on your team dips in month three. What's the most likely story?

Play · Month-three moves

Why this matters: the dip arrives on schedule, and managers who have rehearsed the sustaining move make it calmly instead of panicking. Four dip scenarios; pick the move.

Go deeper

In practice

Put the dip on your own calendar now: a note at week ten that says "the round will be quiet; seed it." That one reminder converts the cliff from a surprise into a scheduled maintenance task. Your month-three checklist is short: the 1:1 question still gets asked, the share-one round gets re-seeded with your own narration, the learning hour survives the busy season on at least most calendars, and the miss log gets reviewed as a team with the catches celebrated. For reporting upward, one line does it: how many people tried something new this month, what the team caught before it shipped, and where the recovered hours went. Team-level, story-shaped, and nobody ranked.

As a group · 4 min

Why this matters: naming your team's likeliest dip out loud today is what lets you catch it quietly in week ten.

  1. Solo, thirty seconds: which sustaining structure dies first on YOUR team: the protected hour, the round, or the log? Be honest about your busy season.
  2. Pairs: name it, then name the specific week it'll happen and the move you'll make that week.
  3. A few answers to the room. Listen for how many chose the same structure; that's the room's shared weak point.

Flying solo? Write one line: which sustaining structure dies first on your team, in which week, and the move you'll make that week. Put a calendar reminder on that week right now.
