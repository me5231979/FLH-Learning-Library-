Section 01 · The Case

Data work is AI's sweet spot. And its blind spot.

Both halves of that sentence are true, and you need both. AI reads a 5,000-row export in seconds, spots patterns you would find in an hour, and drafts a comparison while your coffee is still warm. It also invents totals, silently drops rows it failed to parse, and does confident arithmetic that is simply wrong. Evidence first: every number in this game comes from Gallup's workplace AI research, and most rooms miss at least one.

In practice

The source for the game is Gallup's workplace AI research, the long-running survey of how employees actually use these tools. The finding that matters most here: data and analysis work sits near the top of AI use cases by reported payoff, with about three quarters of the employees who use AI on it saying it clearly improves their productivity, and the gains concentrate heavily in the people who use it often rather than occasionally. Meanwhile most staff who live in spreadsheets still pull, total, and eyeball entirely by hand. That combination, high payoff and low adoption, is unusual, and it means the fastest available win in most offices is sitting in a file you already own. The blind spot is real too: AI fabricates numbers with total confidence, which is why this course welds a verification step into the loop instead of treating it as optional hygiene.

Section 02 · Safe Data In

The traffic light applies to files, too.

Before any technique, the rule that outranks every technique. Uploading a file is different from typing a question: one drag and drop can move thousands of rows about real people into a tool that was never approved to hold them. An enrollment sheet with names, a salary file, survey comments where a student is mentioned in passing: all of it is people data, and people data never goes into unapproved tools. The same light you learned in Start Smarter decides what a data file may see.

The light, read for datasets
GreenPublic or generic data: published statistics, federal datasets, made-up practice numbers. Go.
YellowInternal work data with no people in it, including de-identified or aggregated files. Approved VU tools only: ChatGPT EDU, Amplify, Copilot.
RedPrivate information about people: names, IDs, salaries, grades, health details. Never in unapproved tools.

One habit converts most red files to yellow: de-identification. Delete the name and ID columns, roll rows up into counts, and read every free-text column, because a single name in a comment field makes the whole file people data. Then, and only then, the approved tools can help.

Play · Green, yellow, or red

Why this matters: the light is easy to recite and harder to apply to a real file at 4:45 on a Friday. Five datasets; call the light on each.

Go deeper

In practice

De-identification is a five-minute ritual, and it goes like this. Make a copy of the file; the original never gets touched. Delete every column that identifies a person: names, initials, employee and student IDs, emails, phone numbers. Scan the free-text columns by eye, because comments are where names hide. If the analysis works on groups, aggregate before uploading: counts by month or by unit answer most questions and carry no individuals at all. And when a file cannot be cleanly de-identified, the answer is already written: it stays out of unapproved tools entirely, whatever the deadline. The light outranks the loop, the deadline, and every technique in this course.

On your own

Do this now: List two datasets you touch monthly and call the light on each. For any red one, write the one-line de-identification plan: which columns to delete, whether counts would do.

Section 03 · Ask the Sheet

Describe first. Then ask.

Here is the loop the whole session turns on: Describe, Ask, Check, Tell. This section is the first half. Most people paste a file into a chat and type "analyze this," and the AI, knowing nothing about what the columns mean, guesses. The fix costs thirty seconds: tell it what the data is before you ask anything, then ask one question at a time, climbing from summary to comparison to outlier to explanation.

1 · Describe

Tell it the column story

What each column holds, what period the rows cover, where the data came from, and any quirks: "July is missing," "totals include cancellations." The AI can only be as right as this paragraph.

2 · Ask, one rung at a time

Climb the question ladder

Summarize: what does a typical month look like? Compare: this term against last. Outliers: which rows break the pattern? Explain: what changed in September, and show me the rows behind it.

A worked example

The help-desk sheet

"Twelve months of help-desk tickets: Month, Category, Count, Avg days to close. Source: the ticket system export. Summarize the year, then compare fall to spring, then find the strangest month."

Go deeper

In practice

Three habits make the ladder work. Keep the describe paragraph honest about mess: if the data has gaps, renamed categories, or a month recorded twice, say so up front, because unspoken quirks come back later as confident wrong answers. Ask one question per message; a single prompt demanding summary, trends, outliers, and recommendations produces a wall of plausible mush, while the ladder produces answers you can check one at a time. And end any rung that surprises you with "show me which rows that came from"; a real pattern survives the question, an invented one dissolves on contact.

On your own

Do this now: Take your grind question and write its two-rung ladder: one honest describe paragraph for your dataset, then the exact first ask. Keep both; the lab and the capstone reuse them.

Section 04 · Check the Math

Trust is not a workflow. Checking is.

The loop's third step is the one that protects your name. AI-generated analysis fails in ways human work rarely does: a total that matches nothing, a category that was never in the file, an average computed after a month silently fell out of the read. Skipping the check does not even save time: SHRM's research on AI at work finds workers spending around four hours a week fixing AI output, which is what checking looks like when it happens too late, after the wrong number has traveled.

The check ritual, every run

1. Recompute one number by hand: pick the figure your reader will quote and add it up yourself. 2. Check the totals and counts: do the parts sum to the whole, and does the row count match the file? 3. Trace the surprise: any claim that would change a decision gets "show me the rows that came from" before you believe it. A generated number never enters a document without one of these checks behind it.

Play · Catch the error

Why this matters: AI's wrong answers look exactly like its right ones. Five outputs from a month-end analysis; decide what each one gets.

Quick check: What does the four-hours-a-week finding say about skipping verification?

Go deeper

In practice

Which number do you recompute? The one that will be quoted: the headline total, the percentage in the subject line, the figure a dean or director will repeat in a meeting. One hand-checked number catches most fabrications, because a model that invented one figure rarely invented just that one, and the mismatch surfaces immediately. The row-count check catches the quietest failure, dropped data: ask "how many rows did you read?" and compare the answer to the file. And keep the asymmetry in mind: a boring answer that matches your instincts can wait for the weekly pass, while a surprising answer gets traced to its rows today, because surprising numbers are the ones that travel fastest.

On your own

Do this now: Run the same ritual on that output yourself: recompute the average, name the totals check, and write the exact "show me the rows" question you would ask about the Facilities claim.

In practice

Notice what the lab punishes. Never using AI on the numbers; always the design around it: a file that went up with names attached, a dump-everything prompt that produced mush, a check that was really a vibe. The strongest run is the Data Loop verbatim: de-identify, describe, climb the ladder, recompute one number, and hand leadership two sentences with a chart. Notice also the trap on the check step: redoing the whole analysis by hand scores worse than the targeted ritual, because a check that costs as much as the work means the AI bought you nothing. Verification has a budget; spend it on the number that will be quoted.

On your own

Section 06 · Tell the Story

Nobody acts on a wall of numbers.

The loop's last step is the reason the other three exist. An analysis that ends as a screen of figures changes nothing; the same analysis, told as two plain sentences with one chart, moves a meeting. AI can help you draft the telling too, and this is also where the hardest boundary in the course lives: the machine narrates what the data shows, and the recommendation, what your unit should do about it, stays yours.

The two-sentence finding

What changed, why it matters

Sentence one: the change, with its number. Sentence two: why the reader should care, or what to look at next. "Tickets rose 18 percent this quarter, almost all password resets. A self-service reset link would remove most of the surge."

The chart brief

Tell it the chart you want

Give AI three things: the comparison ("monthly tickets, this year against last"), the audience ("directors, ten seconds of attention"), and the takeaway line you want printed under it. A chart without a takeaway line is decoration.

Never outsourced

The recommendation

AI has never sat in your budget meeting and owns nothing it suggests. Let it describe the pattern; the "so we should" sentence carries your name and your judgment, and writing it is the job.

Play · Judge the finding

Why this matters: you calibrate your own writing by calling someone else's. Five data narrations headed for a busy reader; judge each one.

Quick check: Which part of the telling never gets delegated to AI?

Go deeper

In practice

A telling that works answers the reader's next question before it is asked. Lead with the change, in units the reader already thinks in; "18 percent" beats "212 versus 180," and "two clinics drove it" beats a table of nine. Keep the number's check in your pocket, because someone will ask where the figure came from, and "I recomputed it against the export" ends that conversation in one sentence. And watch the editorializing line: the data can say enrollment dipped after the deadline moved; it cannot say whose fault that was. The moment your sentence assigns blame or motive, you have left the data and started spending your own credibility.

On your own

Do this now: Write the two-sentence finding for your lab run, then test it: does sentence one carry a number, does sentence two tell the reader why to care, and does neither sentence claim more than the data showed?
