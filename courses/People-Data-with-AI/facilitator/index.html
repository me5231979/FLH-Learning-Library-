05 · When things go sideways

If it breaks, you have a move

If Projector or the site fails mid-session: Teach from the whiteboard: the traffic light, the Coach's Loop (aggregate, interrogate, humanize, converse), and the rehearsal pattern all draw in seconds. The trainers become read-aloud room votes, the builder and capstone move to the printed card, and the lab becomes a group walk-through: read each stage's three options aloud and have the room vote.
If Learner devices can't join (wifi or filters): Run facilitator-only: the trainers play well on the projector with the room voting A/B/C by hands; the private builder and capstone move to paper. You lose typing, not learning.
If You're 10+ minutes behind by the Review Prep Lab (05): Declare the Core path silently: pair drills become solo passes, debriefs shrink to one voice, and the closing tightens to the fist-to-five plus commitment round. Never cut the lab, the builder, or the capstone; they carry the session's practice objectives.
If Someone names a colleague or starts reading a real survey comment aloud: Protect the norm warmly and immediately: 'Hold that. Patterns and roles, never names, and real comments stay off the air; they can identify their authors.' If it keeps happening, a real conflict is usually underneath; offer a private follow-up and point them at Coaching for Performance for the conversation itself.
If Someone announces they already run 1:1 notes or survey comments through a personal AI account: Thank them for the honesty, because half the room is quietly doing something similar, then draw the line without shaming: named people in personal tools is the exact red case, and today gives them the compliant version that gets them the same value (de-identified pattern lists, approved tools). Follow up privately if it sounded like an active habit involving identifiable people.
If The room is skeptical: 'AI has no place near people decisions, full stop': Agree with the core of it, then sharpen: the course bans AI from exactly the places they're worried about (verdicts, named people, individual records) and uses it only where a fast reader helps (aggregated patterns, rehearsal). The Judge the Draft trainer usually converts this room: they can hear the difference between rehearsal and outsourced judgment.
If The room is the opposite: enthusiasts who want AI to draft the actual reviews: Aim the enthusiasm at the lab: let them pick the AI-writes-everything option and read the grievance outcome aloud. The weak tier does the sobering for you. Then hand them the strong version: AI on structure and recall is faster anyway, and it survives an HR appeal.
If A real, live privacy incident surfaces ('I think this already happened on my team'): Stop and honor it; this outranks the agenda. Restate the Aggregate Rule, confirm the case against it without collecting details in the room, and route it: this goes to HR or the privacy office today, not to a workshop. Offer to stay after and help them frame the report.

06 · Tough questions, ready answers

Say each answer out loud once before you teach

Is my survey data actually anonymous? Can managers see who said what?

Answer for your context honestly: at Vanderbilt, engagement platforms report at aggregate levels with minimum group sizes precisely so individuals cannot be identified, and this course adds the manager-side duty: never try to reverse it. The moment a manager speculates about authorship, even warmly, anonymity is dead retroactively and every future survey collects fiction. If someone needs the exact reporting thresholds for a specific platform, take it offline and get the real answer from HR rather than guessing from the front of the room.

Culture Amp's AI Coach is built into the tool and reads individual comments. If the vendor does it, why can't I?

Because the vendor's AI runs inside a contracted, access-controlled platform that the institution vetted, with the survey's anonymity model intact. The rule this course teaches governs what YOU paste into tools: taking people data out of its governed home and putting it into an AI tool is a different act from the platform processing data inside its own walls. Use the built-in features of approved platforms freely; the Aggregate Rule is about what leaves them.

My 1:1 notes are mine. Why is it red to ask AI about my own notes?

The notes are yours; the information in them is about someone else, and that is what the red light protects. 'My notes about Jordan' is a person's performance record wearing your handwriting. The compliant version costs you five minutes: build a de-identified pattern list, roles and recurring topics, no names, no identifying events, and that list is yellow in an approved tool. You keep the value of theme-spotting; Jordan keeps the dignity of not being in a prompt.

If AI can't write the verdict, what is it actually saving me? This sounds like the same work plus extra steps.

The hours in people work were never in the judging; they were in the reading, recalling, structuring, and dreading. AI collapses those: a hundred comments become themes in a minute, a year of de-identified notes becomes a structured evidence file, and the conversation you dreaded gets rehearsed before you have it once. The judgment was always minutes of work resting on hours of preparation. You keep the minutes; the machine eats the hours.

What if the AI's theme is wrong and I act on it?

That is exactly what the ground truth test exists to catch: no theme gets acted on until you can name a concrete example you have seen yourself, and a theme without one goes to the team as a question, which is safe even when the theme is wrong, because asking a genuine question never damaged a team. The failure mode to fear is the confident wrong theme announced as fact, and the test blocks precisely that. Data proposes; the manager disposes.

Small teams are my whole life; everything I manage is under ten people. Is this course useless for me?

The rule bites hardest for you, and the techniques still work. Under five respondents, skip the aggregate interrogation of scores and use what you legitimately have: your own de-identified pattern list across time rather than across people, published benchmarks as green context, and the rehearsal pattern, which needs no team data at all. Small-team managers actually get the most from sections 04 and 06; the conversation techniques scale down perfectly even where the statistics do not.

Doesn't HR or my HCM system already forbid all of this? Why is a course drawing these lines?

Policy sets the floor and moves slower than the tools; this course trains the judgment for the cases policy has not enumerated yet. Where Vanderbilt policy or your platform's rules are stricter than today's material, they win, always. What the course adds is the manager-level instinct: the head count before the paste, the ground truth test before the announcement, the roles-not-names habit at the keyboard. Those keep you safe in the gap between what tools make possible and what policy has caught up to.

Section 02 · The Rule

Interpret patterns. Never feed it people.

Every AI course in this series carries one traffic light, and people data is where it has the most teeth. The Aggregate Rule is that light applied to your team's data: you may use AI to interpret patterns; you may never feed it people. Before any people data goes near a tool, it passes exactly one test: could this point at a person?

The Aggregate Rule
GreenPublished benchmarks and generic information. No one on your team is in it. Go.
YellowInternal work, including aggregated, de-identified team-level results. Approved VU tools only: ChatGPT EDU, Amplify, Copilot.
RedPrivate information about people: names, individual scores, identifiable comments, your notes about a person. Never.

The light outranks every technique in this course. If a case is red, no theme, no deadline, and no efficiency argument changes the answer.

The small-team trap

A "team-level" average from a team of three identifies its people; the label says aggregated and the math says disclosure. Count heads before you call anything aggregate: under five respondents, treat it as red. De-identification works the same way: it is a verb, done and checked, never a checkbox on someone else's export.

Play · Red, yellow, or green

Why this matters: the rule is easy to recite and the real cases arrive in disguise. Five people-data situations; give each one its light.

Go deeper

In practice

De-identifying for real takes about four moves. Names out, obviously. Identifying roles out: "the only designer on the team" is a name with extra steps. Identifying events out: "after the March escalation" narrows the cast fast. Then the head count: under five respondents in any slice, the slice stays red however clean it looks. One more habit worth building: do the de-identification pass yourself, in a plain document, before anything opens an AI tool. The pass takes minutes, it is where you catch the near-misses, and it means the first thing the tool ever sees is already safe. When a case stays genuinely ambiguous after all that, the answer is a person: ask HR or your privacy office before a tool sees anything.

As a group · 5 min

Why this matters: the dangerous cases are the ones that look yellow, and inventing them is the fastest way to learn to spot them.

  1. Pairs: invent the sneakiest case you can that LOOKS yellow but is actually red. Small teams, identifying roles, and quotable comments are fair game.
  2. Pairs trade cases and call the light, with the reason.
  3. The two best traps go to the room. Call each one together, out loud, and name the tell that gives it away.

Flying solo? Take the most recent people-data artifact you actually have, survey export, retro board, your own notes, and sort each part of it: green, yellow, or red. Write the red parts down; that list is what never touches a tool.

Section 03 · The Themes

Ask the data a better question.

Once your data is safely at team level, AI becomes a genuinely useful interrogator. It reads a hundred de-identified comments in seconds and proposes patterns you would have missed at 5 pm on a Friday. The craft is in the questions you ask it, and there are three that pay every time.

1 · Themes

What patterns are here?

"What are the strongest themes in these team-level results, and what in the data supports each one?" Forces volume into shape, with receipts.

2 · Contrasts

What changed?

"Compare with last cycle: what moved, what stayed stuck?" A score means little on its own; movement is where the story lives.

3 · The question behind the question

What explains both?

"What would explain both the high autonomy score and the low recognition score?" The contradiction in your data is usually the finding.

Humanize: the ground truth test

Every theme the AI proposes gets tested against what you have actually seen: can you name one concrete example from your own experience of this team? A theme with an example is a finding worth acting on. A theme without one is a hypothesis, and hypotheses go to the team as questions, never as announcements. Data proposes; the manager disposes.

Play · Build your interrogation plan, privately

Why this matters: the three questions only earn their keep pointed at your own data, and you leave this section with the exact plan to run this week. Nothing you type is saved or sent anywhere.

Fill in all three
Go deeper

In practice

Two habits keep the interrogation honest. First, ask for evidence with every theme: "and quote the de-identified data that supports each one." An AI asked only for themes will find some in random noise; an AI asked for support has to show its work, and weak themes collapse on contact. Second, treat your suspicion as a hypothesis in the same queue as the AI's. Managers tend to interrogate data until it confirms what they already believed and then stop; putting your own theory through the same ground truth test is what separates reading the data from decorating your hunch. And the rule from section 02 rides along the whole way: aggregated, de-identified, approved VU tools, head count checked.

As a group · 5 min

Why this matters: another manager's read on your mystery is the cheapest second opinion you will ever get.

  1. Pairs: trade your "mystery" lines from the builder, the part of your data you cannot explain.
  2. Your partner proposes two explanations for yours; you propose two for theirs. No names, patterns and roles only.
  3. Each of you picks the one explanation you could actually test in a 1:1 this week, and says the question you would ask.

Flying solo? Write two candidate explanations for your own mystery line, then write the 1:1 question that would test the more likely one. Put that question at the top of next week's 1:1 agenda.

Section 04 · The Rehearsal

AI rehearses. You judge.

Hard feedback gets postponed, and postponed feedback arrives late, vague, or never. Here AI genuinely helps, as a rehearsal partner: describe the situation with roles instead of names, draft against a real framework, stress-test the tone, practice the pushback. One line never moves: AI rehearses the conversation; it never generates the judgment. Your assessment of a person comes from your evidence, in your words.

The rehearsal pattern

1. Roles, never names: "a senior analyst on my team," never anyone a tool could identify. 2. Framework first: draft against SBI (situation, behavior, impact) or feedforward (name the pattern, design the next attempt together). 3. Stress-test: "Where is this vague? What will they hear that I didn't say?" 4. Anticipate: "Give me the three hardest reasonable responses, and let me practice mine."

Play · Judge the draft

Why this matters: AI-assisted feedback fails in recognizable ways, and your ear learns them fastest by judging. Five lines from managers using AI on feedback; call each one.

Quick check: What may AI contribute to a hard feedback conversation?

Go deeper

In practice

The rehearsal prompt pattern in full: "A [role] on my team [the observable pattern, no names, no identifying detail]. I want to give this feedback using [SBI or feedforward]. Here is my draft. Stress-test it: where is it vague, what could be heard as blame, and what are the three hardest reasonable responses I should be ready for?" Notice what the pattern assumes: you already have the evidence. If you cannot write the situation and the behavior yourself, the problem is upstream of wording, and no tool fixes thin evidence. This course pairs with Coaching for Performance, which teaches the human method, GROW and feedforward; today covers what happens at the keyboard before that conversation starts.

  2. Everyone drafts ONE feedback line for it, SBI or feedforward, two minutes.
  3. A few volunteers read theirs; the room calls each: specific and owned, generic mush, or crosses the line. Rewrite the mushiest one together.

Section 06 · The Conversation

Close the loop.

Everything so far produced themes. This section is where they earn their keep, because a theme that never becomes a conversation is a chart. Three moves close the loop: the share-back, the question bank, and measuring the right thing.

1 · The share-back

What we heard

Three parts, said in public: what we heard, what we will try, and what we will not change and why. The third part builds the most trust, because it proves you actually decided something.

2 · The question bank

Themes become questions

Turn each theme into one question you will actually ask in 1:1s. Recognition low? "What did you do last month that nobody noticed?" The theme sets the agenda; the person fills it in.

3 · The measure

Count conversations

Track conversations held and experiments started, never dashboard views. If every theme has a conversation or an experiment attached within a month, the process works.

Quick check: Which measure tells you your team's survey process is actually working?

Play · Judge the share-back

Why this matters: the scenario is real: results are in, your team is watching how you handle them. Five ways a manager presents survey themes; call each one.

Go deeper

In practice

The share-back has one non-negotiable and one secret weapon. The non-negotiable: never speculate about who said what, even warmly, even once. The moment a team suspects comments get traced, every future survey collects fiction, and the data side of this entire course stops working. The secret weapon is the third line, "what we will not change and why." Teams do not expect managers to fix everything; they expect to be leveled with. A manager who says "the release pace stays, here is why, and here is what I can move instead" earns more trust than one who promises six improvements and delivers one. AI can help draft the share-back the same way it helps with feedback: structure and tone, on team-level themes, with the decisions already yours.

As a group · 4 min

Why this matters: drafting the share-back in the room, against your real signal, means the loop starts closing before the session ends.

  1. Solo, two minutes: draft the opening of YOUR share-back for the signal from your section 03 builder: what we heard, one thing to try, one thing that will not change and why.
  2. Pairs: read them to each other. The listener calls it: closes the loop, breeds distrust, or says nothing.
  3. One or two volunteers read theirs to the room. Tighten the vaguest line together.

Flying solo? Draft your three-line share-back for your real signal, then test it: does it name what was heard, commit to one visible try, and level with the team about one thing that stays? If yes, schedule the meeting where you will say it.
