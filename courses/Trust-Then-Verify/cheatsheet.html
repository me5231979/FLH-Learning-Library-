Trust, then verify, the cheat sheet

The Three Reads, in order

  1. The claim read: underline every checkable claim: names, numbers, dates, quotes, citations. Those are the fault lines.
  2. The source read: trace the riskiest claims to a source you trust, and open it. A claim with no source is a guess wearing a suit.
  3. The slant read: what is this assuming, who is missing, and what would the strongest opposite case say?

Riskiest claim: the one that travels farthest and costs most if wrong.

The five failure modes, with tells

  • Hallucination: precise details you can find nowhere else. Orphan facts.
  • Staleness: a moving number or policy stated with no date attached.
  • Slant: adjectives doing the arguing; "concerns" versus "complaints."
  • Omission: the summary reads smoother than the source ever did.
  • Faked math: any arithmetic the model did itself. Redo it, every time.

The stop rules

  • Three misses in a row on the same task: the model is telling you it cannot do this one. Take the task back.
  • The source does not exist: one invented citation makes every citation suspect. Start over without the tool.
  • Judgment you cannot check: if you could not tell right from wrong here, no read will save you.

The traffic light (data rule)

  • Green: public or generic information. Go.
  • Yellow: internal work. Approved VU tools only.
  • Red: private information about people. Never.

The light outranks every read. No check makes a forbidden input acceptable.

The verification budget, sized to the stakes

No check: brainstorms and drafts for your own eyes; there are no claims to be wrong yet. Claim read (90 seconds): internal summaries and notes you will act on. All three reads: anything that travels: emails to groups, briefings, reports, pages others will trust. Human expert, regardless: legal, medical, financial, or people consequences. Decide the size before you read the output; a fluent draft will talk you down.

The daily habit

  1. Trigger: the moment output lands, the claim read starts.
  2. Trace the one claim that would hurt most if wrong.
  3. Slant question on anything that will influence a decision.
  4. Tally misses by task; the tally runs the stop rules.
  5. Say "caught one" out loud. Catches are wins.

The numbers, for the skeptics

  • Workers using AI spend around 4 hours a week fixing its output (SHRM): about five full work weeks a year.
  • Publicly reported AI incidents keep hitting record highs even as models improve (Stanford AI Index).
  • Decades of decision-aid research: when a system is usually right, human checking fades. That drift is automation bias.

The 90-second check, fill in the blanks

Output: [what it is and where it goes]. Fault lines underlined: [names, numbers, dates, quotes, citations]. First trace: [the riskiest claim] against [the source I will open]. Slant question asked: [what it assumes / who is missing / the opposite case]. Verdict: ship / fix and ship / stop. Misses on this task so far: [tally].
