05 · When things go sideways

If it breaks, you have a move

If Projector or the site fails mid-session: Teach from the whiteboard: the five failure modes, the Three Reads, and the stop rules all draw in seconds. Read the worked example aloud from your printed backup paragraph and have the room call the catches. The trainers become room votes on scenarios you read out; the capstone moves to the printed card. You lose polish, not learning.
If Learner devices can't join (wifi or filters): Run facilitator-only: the trainers play well on the projector with the room voting A/B/C by hands; the ritual builder and capstone move to paper. The lab works as a walk-through: read each move's three options aloud and have the room vote before you reveal the coaching.
If You're 10+ minutes behind by the Verification Lab (05): Declare the Core path silently: pair drills become solo passes, debriefs shrink to one voice, and the closing tightens to the fist-to-five plus commitment round. Never cut the lab, the ritual builder, or the capstone; they carry the session's practice objectives.
If Someone starts reading out a real output that names a person or contains private details: Protect the norm warmly: 'Hold that. Outputs and tasks, never private information about people. Describe the kind of output; skip the contents.' If it keeps happening, it usually means a real red-light practice is running on their team; restate the traffic light and move the specifics to a private follow-up.
If The room is skeptical: 'if I have to check everything, the AI saves me nothing': Don't argue; do the arithmetic out loud. The claim read on a page is ninety seconds; the SHRM cleanup tax is four hours a week. The budget section is the whole answer: most outputs need a small check or none, and the expensive misses cluster in the few that travel. Verification is how the time savings survive contact with reality.
If The room is the opposite: enthusiasts who insist their favorite model no longer hallucinates: Aim the enthusiasm at the data: incident counts keep hitting record highs even as models improve, because trust grows faster than accuracy. Then aim them at the lab; the invented-benchmark slot usually does the sobering for you. Better models make verification cheaper, and automation bias makes it rarer; the skill is holding the first without the second.
If Someone raises a real data-privacy concern mid-flight: Stop and honor it; this is the one interruption that outranks the agenda. Restate the traffic light (green public, yellow internal in approved VU tools only, red private information about people, never), confirm their case against it, and if it's genuinely ambiguous, park it with a promise to route it to the right owner rather than improvising policy from the front of the room.
If A learner disputes one of the game's numbers or asks for the exact study: Point at the sources by name: SHRM's workplace AI research for the four-hours figure, the Stanford AI Index for incident counts, both linked in the deck's Go deeper. Model the course's own method: 'Don't take my word; the links are in section 01, open them.' A facilitator who invites the source read is teaching the source read.

06 · Tough questions, ready answers

Say each answer out loud once before you teach

If I have to verify everything, doesn't that cancel the time AI saves?

Only if every check is a full check, which is exactly what the budget prevents. A brainstorm gets no check; an internal note gets ninety seconds; only the outputs that travel get all three reads, and those are the ones that were always worth minutes of care. The SHRM finding is the other side of the ledger: people who don't build the checking habit pay around four hours a week in cleanup anyway, at the worst possible time, after the error has traveled. Verification isn't the tax; it's the cheaper way to pay it.

Models are improving fast. Won't hallucination be solved soon, making this course obsolete?

The error rate falls and the exposure rises, and the second is winning: the AI Index keeps recording more incidents, not fewer, because better models get trusted with more consequential work and checked less. And notice that only one of the five failure modes is hallucination. Staleness, slant, omission, and faked math are structural: any system summarizing the world can drop a caveat or frame a fact. The reads also work on human writing, which is why the skill outlives any model generation.

My manager sends me AI-drafted documents. Am I supposed to verify my boss's output?

Verify claims, whoever drafted them, and frame it as protecting the document rather than auditing the person. 'I traced the enrollment figure before we send it up' is diligence in anyone's book, and the sign-off line from the lab works upward too: tell people what you checked. If your team adopts the miss log by task rather than by person, this stops being awkward at all; the log makes checking a shared routine instead of a judgment.

How do I check output on a topic where I'm not an expert?

The claim read and the source read need no domain expertise: anyone can underline a date and open a citation. What needs expertise is the judgment call, and that's stop rule three saying something honest: if you couldn't tell a right answer from a wrong one, you are the wrong verifier for that output, and the check belongs to someone who could. Route it, the way you'd route a legal question to counsel. Knowing which outputs you can't verify is itself a verification skill.

Isn't the slant read just politics? Facts are facts.

The slant read exists precisely because facts can all be true while the output still misleads. The worked example shows it: every surviving fact checked out, and 'long-overdue' was still the summary arguing a position its source never took. The three questions are mechanical, not political: what is this assuming, who is missing, what would the strongest opposite case say. They're the same questions a good editor asks, and putting them on a checklist means they get asked by every reviewer, whatever their temperament.

What if my whole team already trusts AI output and I'm the only one checking?

Start with the ritual, not the lecture: verify your own outputs, use the sign-off line, and say 'caught one' out loud when a check fires. Catches are contagious in a way policy memos aren't; the first time your claim read stops a wrong number from reaching a dean, the team notices. Then propose the miss log as an experiment, framed on tasks, never people. And if you genuinely see red-light data going into unapproved tools, that's not a culture project; raise it with your supervisor, because the light is policy.

Section 01 · The Cost

Errors don't look like errors. They look right.

A human who is unsure sounds unsure. A model that is wrong sounds exactly like a model that is right: specific dates, plausible names, clean prose, total confidence. That texture is why AI errors travel so far before anyone catches them, and why the cleanup bill lands hours or weeks later. Before the method, the evidence. Call each number before it's revealed; most rooms miss at least one, and the miss is what makes it stick.

In practice

The four-hours figure comes from SHRM's workplace AI research, which keeps finding the same pattern: adoption runs fast, checking skills lag behind, and workers absorb the difference as cleanup time. The Stanford AI Index supplies the other half of the picture: publicly reported AI incidents keep setting records even as models improve, because better models earn more trust, get used in more places, and get checked less. Hold both facts at once and the conclusion writes itself: what protects you is a checking habit, sized to the stakes, that survives your busiest week. That habit is this session.

Section 02 · The Failure Modes

Five ways to be wrong, each with a tell.

"The AI made a mistake" is too vague to act on. Name the species of error and you know exactly where to look, how to check, and how worried to be. Five failure modes cover nearly everything that goes wrong in working AI output, and each one leaves a fingerprint.

1 · Hallucination

Invented facts

Studies, citations, quotes, dates, and people that never existed. The tell: precise details you cannot find anywhere else. Real facts leave trails; invented ones are orphans.

2 · Staleness

True then, wrong now

A rate, policy, name, or figure that was correct when the model learned it. The tell: a moving number stated with no date attached, as if the world stopped changing.

3 · Slant

Real facts, loaded frame

Everything checks out and the framing still argues a side. The tell: one group gets "concerns" while the other gets "complaints." The adjectives are doing the arguing.

4 · Omission

What it left out

The summary that quietly dropped the objection, the caveat, the losing quarter. The tell: the output reads smoother than the source. Reality has friction; a summary that lost it lost something else too.

5 · Faked math

Numbers by vibes

Totals, averages, and percent changes the model "calculated" instead of copied. The tell: any arithmetic it did itself. Models predict plausible-looking numbers; they do not compute.

Play · Name the failure

Why this matters: each mode has a different fix, so the diagnosis decides your next move. Five real-looking outputs; name what's wrong with each.

Go deeper

In practice

The taxonomy earns its keep because each mode maps to a different check. Hallucination falls to the source read: open the citation, find the study, locate the quote. Staleness falls to a date check: when was this true, and is it still? Slant falls to the slant read: strip the adjectives and see what's left. Omission falls to comparison: hold the summary against the source and look for what vanished. And faked math falls to a calculator: redo any arithmetic the model did, every time, because computing is the one thing it only appears to do. Notice what this means for speed: you rarely need every check. Diagnose the likely mode for this kind of output and spend your minutes there.

As a group · 5 min

Why this matters: building a convincing fake error teaches you more about spotting real ones than any list of tells.

  1. Pairs. Each person writes one plausible work sentence with one planted failure mode: an invented citation, a stale figure, a loaded frame, a missing caveat, or a wrong average.
  2. Swap sentences. Name your partner's mode and point at the tell that gave it away.
  3. Best plant goes to the room: read it aloud and let everyone call the mode.

Flying solo? Open the last AI output you actually used. Hunt it for each of the five modes in turn, one minute per mode. Finding nothing is a fine result; now you know what looking feels like.

Section 03 · The Three Reads

Three short reads catch what belief misses.

Here is the whole method. Any AI output, three passes, each one fast and each one looking for something different. Run them in order, because the first read makes the other two cheap.

Read 1 · The claim read

Underline the checkable

Mark every claim that could be checked: names, numbers, dates, quotes, citations. Those are the fault lines, the places where the output can actually break. Everything else is upholstery.

Read 2 · The source read

Trace the riskiest claims

Take the fault lines that carry the most weight and trace each to a source you trust. Open it. Confirm it says what the output says. A claim with no source is a guess wearing a suit.

Read 3 · The slant read

Question the frame

Ask three things: what is this output assuming, who is missing from it, and what would the strongest opposite case say? Facts can all be true while the frame does the lying.

Worked example · An AI summary of a policy document

"The updated flexible work policy takes effect June 30 and asks staff for three days on campus. Since the 2023 survey found 82 percent of staff prefer hybrid schedules, this long-overdue change finally brings the University in line with its peers."

Claim readFive fault lines surface: June 30, three days, the 2023 survey, 82 percent, and "in line with its peers." Now you know exactly what to check.
Source readThe policy document names no effective date; June 30 is invented. The survey is real, ran in 2021, and found 64 percent; the figure is stale. Two of five claims failed.
Slant read"Long-overdue" and "finally" are the summary arguing for the policy, which the source document never does. Strip the frame before this travels anywhere.

Play · Find the fault line

Why this matters: checking time is scarce, so the skill is knowing which claim to check first: the one that is checkable and carries the most weight. Five sentences; pick the first check.

Go deeper

In practice

The order is the trick. The claim read costs about ninety seconds on a page and turns a wall of prose into a short list of checkable points, which is what makes the source read affordable: you trace the two or three claims that carry real weight instead of everything. "Riskiest" has a working definition: the claim that travels farthest and would cost the most if wrong. A date people will act on outranks a percentage in a background paragraph. And if you took Answers, Faster, this should feel familiar: ask-anchor-check is the Three Reads specialized for research questions. What you're learning here is the general case, ready for any output the model hands you.

  3. Land the definition together: checkable plus load-bearing. The claim that travels farthest wins the first check.

Section 04 · Size the Check

Size the check to the stakes.

Running every read on every output would make AI slower than no AI, and skipping every read is how the four-hour tax gets paid. The skill in the middle is a verification budget: the check grows with how far the output travels and who it can hurt.

The verification budget
No checkA brainstorm, a rough draft for your own eyes, a list of ideas. There are no claims to be wrong yet.
Claim readAn internal summary or note you will act on. Ninety seconds: underline the fault lines, fix what you know is off.
All threeAnything that travels: an email to a department, a briefing, a report, a page others will trust. Claim, source, slant.
ExpertLegal, medical, financial, or people consequences. A qualified human reviews, no matter how clean it reads. AI can help draft; it cannot carry the accountability.
The stop rules

Sometimes the right size for the check is abandoning the tool for the task. 1. Three misses in a row: the model has failed the same task three straight times; it is telling you it cannot do this one. Take the task back. 2. The source does not exist: one invented citation means every citation in the output is now suspect, and re-verifying them all costs more than starting over. 3. Judgment you cannot check: if you could not tell a right answer from a wrong one, no read will save you. Verification needs a verifier who knows what wrong looks like.

The data rule still runs

The budget sizes the check after the output exists. The traffic light from Start Smarter governs what the tool may see in the first place, and it outranks everything in this course:

GreenPublic or generic information. Go.
YellowInternal work. Approved VU tools only.
RedPrivate information about people. Never.

Red data about people never goes into unapproved tools, whatever you plan to verify afterward. No read makes a forbidden input acceptable.

Quick check: An AI draft discussing a named employee's performance concern reads perfectly. What actually sizes this check?

Play · Check, ship, or stop

Why this matters: the budget only helps if you can size a check in the moment, at speed. Five real situations; make the call for each.

Go deeper

In practice

Two habits keep the budget honest. First, decide the check size before you read the output, because a fluent draft will talk you down; the stakes were set the moment you knew where the output was going, and nothing in the prose changes them. Second, keep a private tally of misses per task. The stop rules run on that tally: three misses in a row on the same task is a fact you can only notice if you counted. The tally also runs the other way, and that matters just as much: a task with months of clean checks has earned a smaller budget. Verification is a dial you keep adjusting to the evidence, in both directions.

  2. The room votes each one, hands up: ship as is, run the reads, or stop and do it yourself.
  3. On any split vote, stop and ask what stake or audience the two sides saw differently. The disagreement is the lesson.

Flying solo? List your three most common AI tasks and write the budget line for each: no check, claim read, all three reads, or expert. Keep the list; it becomes your default settings.

In practice

Notice what the hour was actually enough for. The claim read on two pages costs five minutes. Tracing the three load-bearing claims costs fifteen. Section 06 · The Habit

Make the check a reflex.

A method you have to remember is a method you'll skip on busy days, and busy days are when the expensive miss ships. The fix is attaching a small check to the moment output arrives, and giving your team a way to talk about misses that makes catching one a win.

The 90-second version

For every output that leaves your hands

Underline the fault lines as you read. Trace the one claim that would hurt most if wrong. Ask the slant question when the output will influence a decision. Ninety seconds, most days, and it turns automatic in about two weeks.

The team ritual

Misses logged, catches celebrated

Keep a shared miss log that records the task and the failure mode, with no blame attached, and say "caught one" out loud when a check fires. A team that celebrates catches keeps checking; a team that punishes misses learns to stop reporting them.

Everyone's job

Bias review by checklist

The slant read belongs to whoever holds the document, as a standing question on every review: what is this assuming, and who is missing? Put it on the checklist and every reviewer runs it as routine, whatever their temperament.

Go deeper

In practice

Habits attach to triggers, so pick the trigger deliberately: the moment AI output lands in front of you, before you do anything else with it, the claim read starts. Two weeks of that and the underlining happens without deciding to. Three staleness misses on the same monthly report is a stop-rule conversation about that report. And the "caught one" ritual does quiet cultural work: it tells everyone the checks are running, makes the checker look sharp rather than slow, and keeps the log honest.

Facilitator scriptFull 5 min · Core 1

Convert cards into spoken commitments, capture the Level 1 reaction check, and point at the follow-up chain.

Say

“The whole session in one breath: the errors arrive fluent, the five modes have tells, the Three Reads catch them, the budget sizes the check, the stop rules know when to quit, and the ritual keeps it alive on your busiest day. You have a card. Before you go: your output, and your day. Say it out loud; spoken commitments stick.”

Do
  1. Read the featured card: the one next step is the next-output test, the full Three Reads on the very next AI output, timed, with the catch and the clock written down.
  2. Run the fist-to-five (Level 1): 'On a fist to five, how confident are you that you'd catch a wrong claim before it ships?' Scan and note the number; the 30-day re-poll asks it again.
  3. Run the commitment round, popcorn style: output and day, out loud or in chat.
  4. Point at the cheat sheet and verification card buttons; the cheat sheet is built to sit next to the keyboard.
  5. Tell them the 7-day pulse is coming, then land the last line and stop on time.
Ask

“Around the room, one line each: your output, and the day the first verified one ships.”

Expect:

  • 'The Friday meeting summary, this Friday'
  • 'The monthly report draft, Thursday'
  • Someone commits to tracing one citation in something already sitting in their inbox

Respond: Thank each one, no commentary. For the citation-tracer, warmly: 'Good instinct. Open it before you leave the building; a traced citation in the next hour beats a planned one next week.'

Debrief

The session's success metric isn't in this room; it's the 7-day pulse: how many first verified outputs shipped, what the reads caught, and how long the catching took.

Validate: Fist-to-five mostly 3+ (Level 1); a majority state an output and a day out loud or in chat. Count both; with the pulse and the 30-day re-poll, that's your evidence chain.

Watch for: Cards that quietly skipped the date. The commitment round catches them: no day, no commitment; help them pick one on the spot.

Transition

“Go read the next one like it matters. And when you catch something, and you will, say it out loud: caught one.”

60-min core: Core: fist-to-five plus a fast commitment round; the ending survives every cut.
