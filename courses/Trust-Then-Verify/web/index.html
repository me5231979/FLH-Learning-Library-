Trust, Then Verify · Vanderbilt

Fluent is not the same
as true.

SHRM's workplace research puts a number on what unverified AI costs: workers who use it spend around four hours a week fixing its output. The reason errors get that far is how they arrive: specific, confident, and in the same polished voice as everything the model gets right. Verification is the trainable skill that closes the gap. The people who check well get AI's speed without the cleanup tax, and this session teaches the checking as a discipline of its own.

The data rule · the same card in every AI course

Before anything goes into an AI tool, one three-second question: what color is this?

🟢
Green · Public or generic
Questions about public topics, generic drafts, brainstorming, "explain this to me." Any approved tool.
🟡
Yellow · Internal work
Your drafts, internal announcements, budgets, grant drafts, internal policies. Approved Vanderbilt tools only, signed in with your VU account. Never a personal account or a random free site.
🔴
Red · Private information about people
Names with grades, health details, personnel issues, passwords, anything confidential about a real person. No AI tool, full stop.

Inside the light: Vanderbilt classifies data in levels, and each approved tool has a ceiling. ChatGPT EDU (VU account) is approved up to Level 2. Amplify and Microsoft Copilot (VU account) are approved for Level 3 work. Children's names, photos, and records go in no AI tool.

The hard cases: ask "who is this about" before "how sensitive does it feel." A student's essay is about a student. Meeting notes naming colleagues are about colleagues until the names come out. A recommendation letter is about its subject.

Never, in any tool: Social Security numbers · Student records or grades (FERPA) · Health information (HIPAA) · Children's names, photos, and records · Salaries and personnel files · Passwords and credentials · Anything confidential about a real person.

This card is the working shorthand for Vanderbilt IT's data classification and approved-tool guidance: vanderbilt.edu/it/policies. When the two ever disagree, the policy wins.

Section 01 · The Cost

Errors don't look like errors. They look right.

A human who is unsure sounds unsure. A model that is wrong sounds exactly like a model that is right: specific dates, plausible names, clean prose, total confidence. That texture is why AI errors travel so far before anyone catches them, and why the cleanup bill lands hours or weeks later. Before the method, the evidence. Call each number before it's revealed; most rooms miss at least one, and the miss is what makes it stick.

In practice

The four-hours figure comes from SHRM's workplace AI research, which keeps finding the same pattern: adoption runs fast, checking skills lag behind, and workers absorb the difference as cleanup time. The Stanford AI Index supplies the other half of the picture: publicly reported AI incidents keep setting records even as models improve, because better models earn more trust, get used in more places, and get checked less. Hold both facts at once and the conclusion writes itself: what protects you is a checking habit, sized to the stakes, that survives your busiest week. That habit is this session.

Section 02 · The Failure Modes

Five ways to be wrong, each with a tell.

"The AI made a mistake" is too vague to act on. Name the species of error and you know exactly where to look, how to check, and how worried to be. Five failure modes cover nearly everything that goes wrong in working AI output, and each one leaves a fingerprint.

1 · Hallucination

Invented facts

Studies, citations, quotes, dates, and people that never existed. The tell: precise details you cannot find anywhere else. Real facts leave trails; invented ones are orphans.

2 · Staleness

True then, wrong now

A rate, policy, name, or figure that was correct when the model learned it. The tell: a moving number stated with no date attached, as if the world stopped changing.

3 · Slant

Real facts, loaded frame

Everything checks out and the framing still argues a side. The tell: one group gets "concerns" while the other gets "complaints." The adjectives are doing the arguing.

4 · Omission

What it left out

The summary that quietly dropped the objection, the caveat, the losing quarter. The tell: the output reads smoother than the source. Reality has friction; a summary that lost it lost something else too.

5 · Faked math

Numbers by vibes

Totals, averages, and percent changes the model "calculated" instead of copied. The tell: any arithmetic it did itself. Models predict plausible-looking numbers; they do not compute.

Play · Name the failure

Why this matters: each mode has a different fix, so the diagnosis decides your next move. Five real-looking outputs; name what's wrong with each.

Go deeper

In practice

The taxonomy earns its keep because each mode maps to a different check. Hallucination falls to the source read: open the citation, find the study, locate the quote. Staleness falls to a date check: when was this true, and is it still? Slant falls to the slant read: strip the adjectives and see what's left. Omission falls to comparison: hold the summary against the source and look for what vanished. And faked math falls to a calculator: redo any arithmetic the model did, every time, because computing is the one thing it only appears to do. Notice what this means for speed: you rarely need every check. Diagnose the likely mode for this kind of output and spend your minutes there.

On your own

Do this now: Open the last AI output you actually used. Hunt it for each of the five modes in turn, one minute per mode. Finding nothing is a fine result; now you know what looking feels like.

Section 03 · The Three Reads

Three short reads catch what belief misses.

Here is the whole method. Any AI output, three passes, each one fast and each one looking for something different. Run them in order, because the first read makes the other two cheap.

Read 1 · The claim read

Underline the checkable

Mark every claim that could be checked: names, numbers, dates, quotes, citations. Those are the fault lines, the places where the output can actually break. Everything else is upholstery.

Read 2 · The source read

Trace the riskiest claims

Take the fault lines that carry the most weight and trace each to a source you trust. Open it. Confirm it says what the output says. A claim with no source is a guess wearing a suit.

Read 3 · The slant read

Question the frame

Ask three things: what is this output assuming, who is missing from it, and what would the strongest opposite case say? Facts can all be true while the frame does the lying.

Worked example · An AI summary of a policy document

"The updated flexible work policy takes effect June 30 and asks staff for three days on campus. Since the 2023 survey found 82 percent of staff prefer hybrid schedules, this long-overdue change finally brings the University in line with its peers."

Claim readFive fault lines surface: June 30, three days, the 2023 survey, 82 percent, and "in line with its peers." Now you know exactly what to check.
Source readThe policy document names no effective date; June 30 is invented. The survey is real, ran in 2021, and found 64 percent; the figure is stale. Two of five claims failed.
Slant read"Long-overdue" and "finally" are the summary arguing for the policy, which the source document never does. Strip the frame before this travels anywhere.

In practice

The order is the trick. The claim read costs about ninety seconds on a page and turns a wall of prose into a short list of checkable points, which is what makes the source read affordable: you trace the two or three claims that carry real weight instead of everything. "Riskiest" has a working definition: the claim that travels farthest and would cost the most if wrong. A date people will act on outranks a percentage in a background paragraph. Section 04 · Size the Check

Size the check to the stakes.

Running every read on every output would make AI slower than no AI, and skipping every read is how the four-hour tax gets paid. The skill in the middle is a verification budget: the check grows with how far the output travels and who it can hurt.

The verification budget
No checkA brainstorm, a rough draft for your own eyes, a list of ideas. There are no claims to be wrong yet.
Claim readAn internal summary or note you will act on. Ninety seconds: underline the fault lines, fix what you know is off.
All threeAnything that travels: an email to a department, a briefing, a report, a page others will trust. Claim, source, slant.
ExpertLegal, medical, financial, or people consequences. A qualified human reviews, no matter how clean it reads. AI can help draft; it cannot carry the accountability.
The stop rules

Sometimes the right size for the check is abandoning the tool for the task. 1. Three misses in a row: the model has failed the same task three straight times; it is telling you it cannot do this one. Take the task back. 2. The source does not exist: one invented citation means every citation in the output is now suspect, and re-verifying them all costs more than starting over. 3. Judgment you cannot check: if you could not tell a right answer from a wrong one, no read will save you. Verification needs a verifier who knows what wrong looks like.

The data rule still runs

The budget sizes the check after the output exists. The traffic light from Start Smarter governs what the tool may see in the first place, and it outranks everything in this course:

GreenPublic or generic information. Go.
YellowInternal work. Approved VU tools only.
RedPrivate information about people. Never.

Red data about people never goes into unapproved tools, whatever you plan to verify afterward. No read makes a forbidden input acceptable.

Quick check: An AI draft discussing a named employee's performance concern reads perfectly. What actually sizes this check?

Go deeper

In practice

Two habits keep the budget honest. First, decide the check size before you read the output, because a fluent draft will talk you down; the stakes were set the moment you knew where the output was going, and nothing in the prose changes them. Second, keep a private tally of misses per task. The stop rules run on that tally: three misses in a row on the same task is a fact you can only notice if you counted. The tally also runs the other way, and that matters just as much: a task with months of clean checks has earned a smaller budget. Verification is a dial you keep adjusting to the evidence, in both directions.

On your own

Do this now: List your three most common AI tasks and write the budget line for each: no check, claim read, all three reads, or expert. Keep the list; it becomes your default settings.

In practice

Notice what the hour was actually enough for. The claim read on two pages costs five minutes. Tracing the three load-bearing claims costs fifteen. The slant pass costs five more, and you still had half the hour left. The lab's bad endings never come from lack of time; they come from spending the hour polishing sentences instead of checking claims, because polishing feels like diligence and reads like progress. The other lesson hides in the sign-off slot: what you tell the dean about your checking is itself a claim, and it shapes how much the dean re-verifies. An honest signal ("numbers verified against the enrollment system, benchmark confirmed") lets the reader spend their attention where you didn't.

Section 06 · The Habit

Make the check a reflex.

A method you have to remember is a method you'll skip on busy days, and busy days are when the expensive miss ships. The fix is attaching a small check to the moment output arrives, and giving your team a way to talk about misses that makes catching one a win.

The 90-second version

For every output that leaves your hands

Underline the fault lines as you read. Trace the one claim that would hurt most if wrong. Ask the slant question when the output will influence a decision. Ninety seconds, most days, and it turns automatic in about two weeks.

The team ritual

Misses logged, catches celebrated

Keep a shared miss log that records the task and the failure mode, with no blame attached, and say "caught one" out loud when a check fires. A team that celebrates catches keeps checking; a team that punishes misses learns to stop reporting them.

Everyone's job

Bias review by checklist

The slant read belongs to whoever holds the document, as a standing question on every review: what is this assuming, and who is missing? Put it on the checklist and every reviewer runs it as routine, whatever their temperament.

Go deeper

In practice

Habits attach to triggers, so pick the trigger deliberately: the moment AI output lands in front of you, before you do anything else with it, the claim read starts. Two weeks of that and the underlining happens without deciding to. The team layer needs one more ingredient: the miss log records the task and the mode, never the person who missed it, because the log's job is pattern-finding. Three staleness misses on the same monthly report is a stop-rule conversation about that report. And the "caught one" ritual does quiet cultural work: it tells everyone the checks are running, makes the checker look sharp rather than slow, and keeps the log honest.

On your own

Do this now: Set the trigger now: name the exact moment your chosen output arrives each week, and put a two-minute calendar block or reminder on it labeled with your check. The reminder does the remembering so the busy day doesn't have to.
