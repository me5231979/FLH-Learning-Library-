06 · Tough questions, ready answers

Say each answer out loud once before you teach

If RAG grounds the answers in our documents, can I finally trust them without checking?

Trust more, verify still. RAG fixes the raw material, not the reader: the model can misquote or blend what it retrieved. The upgrade is that verification gets fast, because a grounded answer hands you the exact source to check. Click the citation; that's the whole check.

Is fine-tuning something our team could even do?

Technically yes, practically almost never first. It needs training data you probably haven't curated, compute you'd have to pay for, evaluation to prove it worked, and a redo every time the base model updates. The honest sequence is: exhaust prompting, add RAG, and if you still have evidence fine-tuning is needed, bring in central IT rather than going alone.

Are agents actually safe to use at work right now?

Scoped, limited, and checkpointed: yes, for low-stakes drafting and compiling. Unsupervised with broad tool access: no. The failure modes are documented and repeatable, which is good news; it means the guardrails are designable. The workflow card you build today IS the safe pattern.

Do I need to memorize the NIST framework?

No. You need the four questions it encodes: who decides, what are we running, how do we know it works, what do we do about it. If you can ask those about any AI tool your team uses, you're doing risk management; NIST just gives everyone the same vocabulary.

Our vendor says their model doesn't hallucinate. True?

No model is hallucination-free; grounding and guardrails reduce the rate, sometimes dramatically. The professional response to that claim is: 'show me the evaluation results and red-team findings.' A vendor who has them will love the question, and a vendor who doesn't just told you something important.

Does this session change the data rules from AI Basics?

It extends them, same colors, same rules. Green, yellow, red still govern what goes INTO a tool. AI 201 adds the workflow view: pattern, checkpoint, and tier travel together, so the rule follows the task instead of living on a poster.

Section 01 · The Window

Every answer has a window.

A model doesn't remember your conversation; it re-reads it, every single turn, through a finite context window. Attention decides which parts of that window matter for the next word. Anything outside the window doesn't exist for that answer. That's the whole mystery of "it forgot what I said."

Message 8 of a conversation

You ask: "Quick check: what did I ask you to always do at the start?"

Quick check: Twenty messages in, the model ignores an instruction you gave at the start. What most likely happened?

Go deeper

In practice

Three habits fall straight out of the mechanism. Restate, don't assume: in long chats, repeat the key instruction instead of trusting message three. Put the ask near the end: "important instruction last" isn't superstition; recent tokens compete better for attention. Start fresh on purpose: a new chat with a clean, complete prompt often beats message 40 of a cluttered one. The window is why.

Section 02 · Grounding

Give the model receipts.

A model's training data is frozen in the past, so it guesses at anything current or internal. Retrieval-Augmented Generation fixes the raw material: before answering, the system retrieves your real, current documents and hands them to the model. RAG doesn't make the model smarter, and it doesn't make sources trustworthy on its own. It grounds answers in the documents you give it; the answers are only as reliable as that library.

The question: "How many unused PTO days carry over into next year?"

Prefer a new tab? Open on YouTube.

Why this video: the standard explainer the industry itself uses, from the researcher who studies it.

Quick check: What does RAG actually fix?

Go deeper

In practice

You already use RAG: chat tools that "search the web first," assistants plugged into your document library, and Amplify workflows grounded in uploaded files are all retrieval in front of a model. The tell of a grounded answer is the citation you can click. Section 03 · The Decision

Start cheap. Escalate on evidence.

Prompting, RAG, and fine-tuning solve different problems at wildly different costs. The framework is three questions, asked in order, and most teams should stop at the first yes.

1 · Prompting

Can a better prompt fix it?

Cheapest, fastest, zero infrastructure. Exhaust this first; the four patterns in the next section are the toolkit.

2 · RAG

Need current or internal facts?

Add retrieval when the model needs facts it wasn't trained on: your policies, this quarter's data, anything that changes.

3 · Fine-tuning

Need a permanent behavior change?

Reserve for a lasting shift in tone, format, or skill at real scale. Budget for real data, real compute, and re-doing it every model update.

Play · Pick the method

Why this matters: the expensive mistake isn't picking wrong once; it's defaulting to the heavy solution before exhausting the cheap one. Five real requests; choose the lightest method that works.

Go deeper

In practice

The hidden cost people underestimate is maintenance. A prompt costs minutes and changes freely. A RAG library needs an owner who keeps the documents current, or the answers quietly rot. Fine-tuning needs training data, compute, evaluation, and a redo every time the underlying model updates, which is often. That's why the framework runs cheapest-first: escalate on evidence, not enthusiasm. IBM's side-by-side comparison video is a solid share-out for your team; find it linked from the cheat sheet.

Section 04 · The Patterns

Four moves past the basic prompt.

AI Basics gave you the four-part prompt. These four patterns, from the same academic catalog behind the Persona Pattern, are the next gear: each one solves a specific, recognizable problem.

Few-Shot

Show, don't tell

Give two or three examples of the input and output you want before the real ask. The model learns the pattern on the spot, no retraining.

Chain-of-Thought

Think out loud

"Reason through this step by step before answering." Measurably improves accuracy on multi-step and numeric tasks, and lets you audit the steps.

Template

Fill in my form

Hand the model your exact output structure: fields, headers, order. The move for anything you'll ask for repeatedly.

Flipped Interaction

Interview me first

"Ask me clarifying questions before you attempt this." The move for when you don't yet know what you need.

Play · Match the pattern

Why this matters: the patterns only pay off if you reach for the right one without thinking. Five real tasks; pick the pattern that fits.

Go deeper

In practice

The research behind each: Few-Shot comes from the original GPT-3 paper (Brown et al.), which showed models learn tasks from in-prompt examples alone. Chain-of-Thought comes from Wei et al., who measured the accuracy jump from step-by-step reasoning. Template and Flipped Interaction come from White et al.'s prompt pattern catalog, the same Vanderbilt-led work behind the Persona Pattern you learned in AI Basics. The patterns stack: a Template prompt with Few-Shot examples is a workhorse.

  2. Pick the ONE pattern that would improve it most, and rewrite the prompt in that pattern.

Section 05 · Autonomy

Goals in. Actions out.
Who's watching?

Multimodal AI chains steps across text, image, audio, and video into real workflows. Agentic AI goes further: you give the model a goal instead of an instruction, and it runs a loop, plan, act, observe, until the goal is met. Or until it breaks, and it breaks in specific, documented ways.

Failure 1

Wrong tool call

The agent picks the wrong tool for the step: searching the calendar for a file, emailing when it should file a ticket.

Failure 2

Misread input or output

The tool works; the agent misunderstands it. "0 results found" becomes "the report shows zero incidents."

Failure 3

Infinite loop

No exit condition. The same failing step retries forever, burning time and budget without escalating.

The fix is never "trust it more"

Scoped tools (only what the job needs), step limits (a loop that must end), and a human checkpoint before anything ships. Where that checkpoint goes is a judgment call you'll make in the capstone.

Prefer a new tab? Open on YouTube.

Why this video: the plan-act-observe loop drawn clearly. Play the first five minutes; the failure modes below are what the diagrams leave out.

Play · Babysit the agent

Why this matters: reading an agent's trace and spotting the failure is exactly the skill the human checkpoint exists to apply. Five traces; name what broke, if anything.

Go deeper

In practice

The checkpoint question has three honest answers, and your capstone will ask you to pick one. Spot-check: a human samples some outputs; right for low-stakes volume. Full review: a human reads everything before it ships; right for external or consequential output. Named approval: a specific accountable person signs off; right for anything touching money, people, or commitments. The pattern to avoid is the silent fourth option: no checkpoint at all, discovered only after the incident.

As a group · 4 min

Why this matters: checkpoint placement is a judgment your team should agree on BEFORE an agent ships something embarrassing, not after.

  1. Name one place in your unit's work where an agent could genuinely help (drafting, filing, compiling).
  2. Decide together: spot-check, full review, or named approval? Defend the choice in one sentence.
  3. Name the failure mode from this slide you'd most expect there, and the tell you'd watch for.

Flying solo? Answer all three for your own workflow; it's a first draft of your capstone card.

Section 06 · Evaluation

Fluency is not accuracy.

A hallucinated answer sounds exactly as confident as a correct one; that's why it's dangerous, and why organizations red-team AI systems: structured testing to find the flaws before users do. Time to calibrate your own detector.

Play · Spot the hallucination

Why this matters: you can't verify everything, so you need a calibrated sense of WHERE models fail. Six AI answers; call each one accurate or hallucinated.

The tells, collected

Suspiciously specific numbers and citations you can't find. Details slightly adjacent to the truth. Absolute rules where reality has exceptions. And no tell at all, sometimes, which is why the check is a habit, not a vibe: fact, date, number, name, citation: verify against the source.

Go deeper

In practice

What you just did by hand, organizations do as a discipline. Red-teaming, in NIST's definition, is a structured testing effort to find flaws and vulnerabilities in an AI system, often with the developers in the loop. Evaluation is its steady-state sibling: measuring accuracy on known test sets before and after every change. When a vendor says "our model doesn't hallucinate," the right response is: "show me the eval results and the red-team findings."

As a group · 3 min

Why this matters: naming where a wrong answer would hurt most turns abstract risk into a checkable list.

  1. As a table: name the ONE place in your work where a confident wrong answer would cost the most.
  2. Agree on the verification that catches it, and how long that check actually takes.
  3. Compare: is the check faster than the incident? It almost always is; say so out loud.

Flying solo? Write your highest-stakes wrong-answer scenario and its check. It feeds the checkpoint field of your capstone.

Section 07 · Governance

Four functions. Three tiers.

The NIST AI Risk Management Framework is voluntary, but it's fast becoming the shared vocabulary regulators, auditors, and vendors use for AI risk. Four functions, no code required, and one of them you can practice right now.

Govern

Who decides?

Policies, roles, and accountability for AI across the organization. Culture, not just documents.

Map

What are we running?

Inventory the AI in use, its context, and who it affects. You can't manage what you haven't mapped.

Measure

How well does it work?

Evaluate and red-team: accuracy, bias, failure modes, tracked over time, not assumed.

Manage

What do we do about it?

Act on what you measured: prioritize risks, add checkpoints, retire what can't be fixed.

Play · Tier the information

Why this matters: this single habit, tiering information BEFORE it goes into an AI tool, prevents most of the real incidents that make the news. Five items; classify each.

Go deeper

In practice

The tiers here are the same green, yellow, red rules from AI Basics, now wearing their governance name. Green: public or generic, any approved tool. Yellow: internal content, company-managed tools only (Amplify, VU-signed Copilot). Red: sensitive data, named approval required, or no AI tool at all: FERPA, HIPAA, personnel, donors, credentials, unpublished research, contracts. The NIST framing adds the org-level view: someone should be able to answer "what AI are we running, and how do we know it works?" For your team, that someone might be you.

As a group · 4 min

Why this matters: running one real tool through the four functions makes NIST a checklist instead of an acronym.

  1. Pick one AI tool your team already uses, even informally.
  2. Run the four functions as questions: who owns it, is it inventoried anywhere, has anyone measured it, what would we do if it misbehaved?
  3. Note the first unanswered question. That's your team's governance gap, found in four minutes.

Flying solo? Same four questions, your most-used tool. The unanswered one goes in your notes.
