Vanderbilt will define the great university of the 21st Century and be it.

That is Chancellor Diermeier's institutional vision, one sentence with no hedge in it. The how is equally blunt: operate at uncommon speed, agility, and scale to address society's challenges through education and research, and help reclaim the essential role of leading research universities in driving American prosperity. Our motto has always been the dare, Crescere aude, dare to grow, and none of it runs on silence.

Exceptional Core Operations

With speed, agility and scale, Vanderbilt will distinguish itself with innovative and impactful capabilities to ensure that we have the talent, resources and reputation necessary to deliver on our vision.

2

Bold Strategic Initiatives

With “exceptional core operations” as our fuel, Vanderbilt will pursue increasingly bold initiatives that enable and enhance the reach and impact of our education and research mission.

Through strategic advocacy efforts and modeling the role of an essential research university, Vanderbilt will stimulate industry change and combat existential threats to our education and research mission.

How this course delivers

Building Brave Teams underwrites all three Areas of Focus: teams that surface mistakes quickly keep core operations exceptional, and bold initiatives need people who feel safe enough to question them before they ship.

The university spins that flywheel with execution with ambition, values-driven leadership, and athletics momentum. Before the what · The why

Section 01 · The Construct

Safe to risk.

Psychological safety is the shared belief that this team is safe for interpersonal risk-taking, for saying "I don't know," "I made a mistake," "I disagree," or "I have an idea that might be bad." That's Amy Edmondson's definition, and every word is load-bearing: it's a shared belief, about risk, held by a team. It is not a personality trait, and it is not a mood.

Myth 1

It's being nice

Niceness avoids the hard thing. Safety makes the hard thing sayable. Candid teams argue more, not less, without casualties.

Myth 2

It lowers the bar

Safety without standards is the comfort zone. The target is both dials high: hard on the work, soft on the people.

Myth 3

It means consensus

Safety protects dissent; it doesn't require agreement. A safe team can disagree, decide, and commit, in that order.

Play · Safety, or something else?

Why this matters: most "psychological safety" efforts fail because they're actually niceness campaigns or standards cuts wearing the label. Five team snapshots; name what you're really looking at.

How to play: read the snapshot, make the call, then read the why; the explanations carry the teaching, so a miss you understand beats a guess you don't. You're solid at 4 of 5.

Go deeper

In practice

The definition earns its keep as a test. Before you call a team "safe," ask: when was the last time someone here said I was wrong, out loud, and nothing bad happened to them? When did someone junior disagree with someone senior, in the room, before the decision? If you can't name a recent example, you have politeness, not safety, and politeness is what silence sounds like from the inside.

As a group · 4 min

Why this matters: defining the construct in your own words, against a real memory, is what makes it diagnostic instead of decorative.

  1. In pairs: recall one meeting where something true went unsaid. No names, stories stay, lessons leave.
  2. Diagnose it: which interpersonal risk felt too expensive, looking ignorant, incompetent, negative, or disruptive?
  3. Write a one-sentence definition of psychological safety in your own words. Check it: does it mention risk, and a shared belief, not niceness?

Flying solo? Recall your own unsaid-thing moment and name the risk that priced it out. That memory is your material for the whole session.

The core idea

High-performing teams are not comfortable teams. They are candid ones.

Section 02 · The Ladder

Four stages. One ladder.

Timothy Clark maps safety as a progression: people need to feel included before they'll learn out loud, learn before they'll contribute fully, and contribute before they'll challenge the way things are done. Teams get stuck at a stage, and the stuck stage is diagnosable.

Stage 1 · Inclusion

Can I belong here?

Accepted as a person, before any performance. The test: does the newest voice get invited in, by name?

Stage 2 · Learner

Can I ask and err?

Safe to ask questions, try, and miss. The test: what happened to the last person who said "I don't know"?

Stage 3 · Contributor

Can I add my work?

Trusted with real work, and credited for it. The test: do ideas travel with their author's name attached?

Stage 4 · Challenger

Can I question how?

Safe to challenge the status quo, upward. The test: when was dissent last thanked, publicly?

Play · The 7-item diagnostic

Why this matters: these seven items are Edmondson's actual research instrument, the same one used in the studies. Rate your current team honestly; nothing you tap is saved or sent anywhere.

How to rate: hold ONE specific team in mind, your closest one, and answer for how it is, not how you wish it were. 1 = strongly disagree, 5 = strongly agree. Your band and your lowest item appear after item 7; the lowest item, not the total, is what you'll bring to the table discussion.

0 of 7 answered

Quick check: A team scores 33/35 on the survey, and still misses every deadline. What would Edmondson say?

Go deeper

In practice

Read your survey result as a location, not a grade. A low score on the mistake item points at Learner safety; a low score on "unique skills valued" points at Contributor; if everything is fine until someone questions how things are done, the ceiling is Challenger. Tuckman's forming, storming, norming, performing runs underneath this: a team that never storms, never has the safe fight, usually isn't performing, it's just quiet. The ladder tells you which rung to build next, and the next two sections are the tools for it.

As a group · 5 min

Why this matters: hearing which item scored lowest across the table, without sharing totals, surfaces the team's real work in five minutes.

  1. At your table: share only your lowest-scoring item, and what you noticed while rating it. Totals stay private; passing is fine.
  2. Listen for the pattern: is the table's low item about mistakes, help, difference, or risk?
  3. Place your team on the ladder: which of the four stages is your current ceiling, and what's your one-line evidence?

Flying solo? Write your lowest item and your stage placement with one line of evidence. Both feed your commitment card at the end.

Section 03 · The Evidence

The quiet team is the risky team.

This isn't a soft idea with a hard sell. Three bodies of evidence, from hospitals, cockpits, and the largest team study ever run, say the same thing: silence is expensive, and the teams that speak up outperform the teams that keep the peace.

Hospitals · 1996

More errors reported

Edmondson's nursing-unit studies found the better units reported more medication errors, not because they made more, but because they felt safe reporting them. The quiet units were hiding theirs.

Cockpits · 1977

Silence at Tenerife

A flight engineer's doubt, voiced too gently to a captain too senior to question, preceded the deadliest crash in aviation history, 583 people. Crew Resource Management exists so that doubt now gets said plainly.

Google · 2012 to 2015

Project Aristotle

Across 180+ teams, who was on the team mattered far less than how the team ran. Psychological safety was the #1 predictor of effectiveness, above talent, seniority, and everything else measured.

The working equation

Safety = f(Leader Invitation × Consistent Response × Normalized Fallibility)

Invitation: the leader explicitly asks for input. Response: what happens next is predictable and non-punishing, every time. Fallibility: the leader goes first on "I don't know" and "I was wrong." Multiply, don't add: a zero in any factor zeroes the product.

Quick check: The higher-performing nursing units reported MORE medication errors. The best explanation?

Go deeper

In practice

Run the equation on your own team, factor by factor. Invitation: when did you last ask "what am I missing?" and wait for the answer? Response: think of the last person who brought bad news, what actually happened to them, and who watched it happen? Fallibility: when did the most senior person in your meetings last admit an error out loud? The audience for each factor is everyone watching, not just the person speaking. That's why one shot messenger silences a whole team for a year.

As a group · 4 min

Why this matters: the case only transfers when you locate the same physics in your own meetings.

  1. At your table, pick one case, hospital, cockpit, or Google, and answer: what would have to be true for a person to speak up in that moment?
  2. Then flip it: what would have to be true for the leader to invite the speaking up?
  3. Name the nearest equivalent moment in your own work, where doubt exists but stays gentle. One table shares.

Flying solo? Answer all three for your highest-stakes recurring meeting. Keep the moment you named; Section 06 gives you the words for it.

Section 04 · The Window

Expand the Open quadrant.

The Johari Window (Luft & Ingham, 1955) maps what's known about you against who knows it. Team effectiveness is largely a function of the Open pane, and there are exactly two ways to grow it: disclosure shrinks what you hide, and feedback shrinks what you can't see.

Known to self Not known to self Known to others

Open area · The Arena

Known to you, known to them. Where trust, speed, and real collaboration live. The pane you're here to grow, and there are exactly two ways to grow it.

Blind spot

They see it; you don't. Only feedback shrinks this pane, which is why soliciting it is a skill, not a courtesy.

Not known to others

Hidden area · The Façade

You know it; they don't. Every item kept here costs energy to maintain. Disclosure moves it to Open, on your terms, at your pace.

Unknown

Nobody knows yet. Shrinks over time through new challenges and shared experience. Leave it room.

Play · Build your Open quadrant

Why this matters: the window only moves two ways, disclosure and feedback, and this is your disclosure rep, the same decision you'll make with paper and stickies in the room. Nothing you tap leaves your screen.

  1. Tap a word once (solid gold) to claim it for your Open pane, pick 5 to 6 you would stand behind in front of your team.
  2. Tap it again (dashed outline) to mark it true of you, but held back; it files under Hidden, up to 3. A third tap clears it.
  3. Build the window. You're done when Open holds 5 to 6 words you'd actually say out loud, and anything you knowingly held back sits in Hidden.

Open: 0 of 5 to 6 claimed · Hidden: 0 of 3 held back

Claim at least 5 words first

Play · Sort the sticky

Why this matters: the mapping step is where the insight happens, and people get it wrong under time pressure. Jordan chose warm, organized, logical, dependable, quiet. The stickies come back; sort each into its pane.

How to play: for each sticky, ask two questions in order, did Jordan claim it? can others see it?, and the two answers name the pane. You're ready for the in-room version at 4 of 5.

Go deeper

In practice

The in-room protocol is deliberately bounded, and the bounds are the safety design. Teammates may only write adjectives from the list, no commentary, no explanations, which caps the emotional risk of both giving and receiving. Mapping is silent and private. The reflection is by choice: you answer one of three prompts, your pick, because forced disclosure destroys the very thing this teaches. And what's on your sheet never gets shared aloud. Autonomy isn't a courtesy here; it's the mechanism.

As a group · 25 min in-room

Why this matters: this is the keystone experience of the workshop, a structured, low-stakes rehearsal of disclosure and feedback with real people.

  1. Finalize your 5 to 6 adjectives on your sheet (silent, 5 min). Pass the sheet clockwise; each teammate adds 1 to 2 adjectives from the list on a sticky. No commentary.
  2. When your sheet returns: silently sort each sticky, matches your pick → Open; you didn't pick it → Blind; you knew but didn't share → stays Hidden.
  3. At your table, answer ONE prompt, your choice: What surprised me in Blind? What did I keep Hidden, and what would moving it cost or gain? What word do I wish someone had written?

Flying solo? Send your 5 to 6 words to two colleagues and ask each for two words back, from the same list. Sort what returns. That's the whole exercise, asynchronously.

Section 05 · The Behaviors

Every move expands or contracts.

Safety isn't declared; it's transacted, one behavior at a time. Interrupting, crediting, eye-rolling, thanking dissent, each move expands the space for risk or contracts it, and everyone watching updates their belief about what's safe here. The skill is seeing the transaction as it happens.

Expanders

Inclusion: use names; invite the newest voice first. Learner: "What am I missing?"; model "I don't know." Contributor: credit specifically, publicly. Challenger: steel-man the opposing view; thank the dissenter in the room.

Contractors

Inclusion: interrupting; inside jokes; "as we all know…" Learner: eye-rolls at questions; "we tried that already." Contributor: taking credit; gatekeeping information. Challenger: shooting the messenger; HiPPO decisions.

Play · Expand or contract?

Why this matters: in the meeting, these go by in two seconds, and the contracting ones often sound harmless. Eight real moves; call each one, fast.

How to play: go gut-first, two-second calls, because that's meeting speed, then let the why name the stage each move touches. 6 of 8 means you'll see it live.

Go deeper

In practice

The in-room version of this is a wall your team builds: a grid, four rows for the four stages, two columns, behaviors that expand, behaviors that contract, filled from your own experience on stickies, then gallery-walked so every table reads every grid. Photograph it. That artifact becomes your team's 30-day retro agenda: which contracting behavior did we retire, and which expander did we practice? The grid your team writes beats any list an instructor hands you, because every line on it has a witness.

As a group · 12 min in-room

Why this matters: naming behaviors from lived experience converts insight into a checklist your team actually recognizes.

  1. As a table: fill the 4×2 grid (stage × expand/contract) with stickies from your own experience, what you've seen, done, or received.
  2. Gallery walk: rotate to the next table's grid, read it, add one sticky of your own to theirs.
  3. Whole room: name the pattern you saw on every grid. That's your organization's habit, for better or worse.

Flying solo? Draw the 4×2 grid for your team from memory: two behaviors per cell. The contract column is your stop-doing shortlist for the capstone.

Section 06 · The Rehearsal

Candor is a skill, not a trait.

The highest rung on the ladder, Challenger safety, needs words, and winging them is how feedback turns into a fight. SBI + Ask, adapted from the Center for Creative Leadership, is the frame: anchor the Situation, describe the Behavior you observed, name the Impact you felt, end with an Ask that opens a conversation.

S · Situation

Anchor it

"In yesterday's staff meeting, when the budget question came up…" Specific time and place. No "always," no "lately."

B · Behavior

Describe, don't judge

"…you cut off Priya mid-sentence…" What a camera would have recorded. "Dismissive" is a verdict; an interruption is a fact.

I · Impact

Name what you saw and felt

"…she didn't speak again the rest of the meeting, and I'm worried we lost her input." Your observation, owned with "I."

+ Ask

Open the door

"Can we talk about how to handle those moments?" The ask converts a verdict into a conversation, it's what makes candor survivable.

Play · Fix the feedback

Why this matters: feedback usually fails at one specific joint, and hearing where trains your ear faster than any template. Five attempts; name what's missing or broken in each.

How to play: listen for the four joints in order, Situation, Behavior, Impact, Ask, and name the first one that's missing or broken. At 4 of 5 your ear is tuned; the triad rehearsal is where your mouth catches up.

Go deeper

In practice

Notice what your body does when you're the receiver: dry mouth, defensive posture, the rebuttal forming before the sentence ends. That reaction is the data, it's what your teammates feel every time you're the giver, and it's why the receiver's only job in rehearsal is to listen and ask clarifying questions. No defense, no explanation. Practicing the receiving is half the skill, and it's the half nobody drills. One cultural note: in high-power-distance contexts, rehearse peer-to-peer first; upward candor is a later, carefully staged step, adapt the practice, never delete it.

As a group · 14 min in-room

Why this matters: skills transfer only if practiced out loud, with a witness, before the stakes are real.

  1. Triads, roles rotating every 4 minutes: Speaker delivers a real (or realistic) SBI + Ask. Receiver listens; clarifying questions only, no defense. Observer checks all four elements and tone, then gives 60 seconds of feedback.
  2. Rotate until each person has played each role once.
  3. Debrief the room: what was hardest, and what would need to be true on your team for this to happen without a workshop?

Flying solo? Write one real SBI + Ask, out loud, to an empty room. Then say the receiver's line: "Tell me more." You'll use both within 14 days.
