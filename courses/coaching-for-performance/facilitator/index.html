05 · When things go sideways

If it breaks, you have a move

If Projector or the site fails mid-session: Teach from the printed cheat sheet: GROW's question bank, the three powerful-question tests, and the Feedforward rules all work on a whiteboard. The pair and triad practice needs no screen at all.
If Learner devices can't join (wifi/filters): Run facilitator-only: the GROW simulator plays brilliantly on the projector with the room voting A/B/C by hands; private work (ratio, capstone) moves to the printed worksheet. You lose typing, not learning.
If You're 10+ minutes behind by Feedforward (Section 05): Declare the Core path silently: cut remaining videos and group debriefs, run one Feedforward rotation instead of three. Never cut the GROW pair roleplay, the recap, or the capstone; they carry objectives 2 and 5.
If The GROW roleplay coaches keep giving advice: Expected; it's the whole point. Interrupt once, kindly and globally: 'Coaches, if you've said anything that isn't a question, restart from the bank.' Then let pairs self-police. Name it in the debrief as evidence of the default.
If Someone dominates, or dismisses coaching as 'too slow for the real world': For dominators: 'Hold that; parking lot.' For the skeptic, don't argue; route to Section 07: 'You're right that directing is sometimes correct, and there's a whole section on when. The question is the ratio.' The skeptic usually becomes your best Section 07 voice.
If A coachee brings a heavy or personal topic into the roleplay: The brief says low-stakes work challenges; re-anchor gently if a pair drifts deep. Coaching practice isn't therapy, and this room isn't private. If someone is genuinely struggling, connect them with EAP resources after, privately.

06 · Tough questions, ready answers

Say each answer out loud once before you teach

I don't have time to coach. Directing is faster.

Directing IS faster, once. The same problem returning to your desk every week is the slow path. The math of the session: a ten-minute GROW conversation that builds capability beats sixty one-minute answers that build dependence. Coaching is how a manager buys their calendar back.

What if they come up with a worse plan than mine?

Two honest answers. First, check the stakes: if the cost of their B-minus plan is small, their owned B-minus beats your imposed A, because they'll execute it harder and learn from it. Second, coaching isn't withholding: after their options are on the table, 'may I add one?' is allowed. Yours becomes an option, not the answer.

Isn't this just therapy? I'm not qualified for that.

Coaching is about work goals, forward motion, and their own thinking; it stays on professional ground. If a conversation moves into mental health, personal crisis, or anything clinical, that's your cue to connect them with EAP or HR resources, not to coach harder. Knowing that line IS part of the skill.

My own manager never coaches me. Why should I go first?

Because the research says it's the top behavior of great managers, and you'd rather be one than wait for one. Also: coaching works upward. 'What would make this most useful?' is a question you can ask your boss about your own one-on-ones. Someone starts the culture; managers in this room are the likeliest candidates.

Can I use these questions in performance or disciplinary conversations?

The skills transfer, but the frame changes. A formal performance process has requirements that come first; talk to HR before improvising coaching moves inside one. For everyday performance conversations short of that line, GROW plus honest expectations is exactly the right tool.

How is coaching different from mentoring?

A mentor shares their path: 'here's what worked for me.' A coach helps someone find their own: 'what might work for you?' Both are valuable; the failure mode is mentoring when you meant to coach, because your story quietly becomes their instruction. Notice which one you're doing on purpose.

Section 02 · The Default

You were promoted for answers.

That's the trap. Excellent doers become managers, and under pressure they do what made them excellent: solve it themselves, fast. Daniel Goleman's research names the styles; the coaching style, developing people for the future, is the one great managers reach for most and stressed managers reach for least.

Directing

"Do it this way."

You hand over the answer. Fast, and right for a crisis. As a habit, it teaches your team to bring you every problem, forever.

Pacesetting

"Watch me do it."

You take it back and do it yourself, brilliantly. The work ships, the person shrinks, and your calendar becomes the bottleneck.

Coaching

"What do you think?"

You ask, they discover, they own it. Slower today. Compounding forever. This is the style Project Oxygen found on top.

Play · Catch the style

Why this matters: you can't change a default you can't hear. Five manager lines; name what each one is really doing.

Go deeper

In practice

McKinsey calls the shift "from controller to coach": from the leader who provides certainty to the one who leads with discovery. The tell that you're in controller mode is physical: you're talking more than they are, and you feel the itch to interrupt with the answer. The fix costs one breath: swallow the answer, and ask "what have you tried?" For the full styles model, watch Goleman on the coaching style (13 min) this week.

Section 03 · The Model

Four letters run the conversation.

GROW is the most widely used coaching structure in the world, and the one Google teaches its own managers. Four stages, each powered by questions, ending in commitment.

G · Goal

Where to?

"What would make this conversation useful?" Their goal, in their words, before anything else.

R · Reality

Where now?

"What's actually happening? What have you tried?" Facts before options; no diagnosis from you.

O · Options

What could you do?

"What else?" asked three times. The fourth option is usually better than the first.

W · Will

What will you do?

"What, by when, how committed, 1 to 10?" Anything under 8 means the plan isn't theirs yet.

Why this video: hear the questions asked with a real voice before you choose your own below.

Play · Coach Jordan

Why this matters: the fastest way to learn coaching is to feel a conversation open up, or shut down, based on what you say. Jordan, a capable analyst on your team, just said they're overwhelmed. Coach them through one full GROW conversation.

Go deeper

The question bank, yours to keep

Goal: "What would you like to focus on?" · "What does success look like?" Reality: "What's happening right now?" · "What have you already tried?" · "Where are things, 1 to 10?" Options: "What could you do?" · "What else?" (three times) · "What would you try if nothing stood in the way?" Will: "What will you do, by when?" · "What might get in the way?" · "How committed are you, 1 to 10?"

As a group · 8 min

Why this matters: coaching a real human, with the no-advice rule on, is the rep that changes behavior. The simulator warmed you up; this is the workout.

  1. Pair up. Coachee: name a real, low-stakes work challenge. Coach: run GROW using ONLY questions from the bank above.
  2. The one rule: the coach may not give advice or share what they would do. Questions only.
  3. Four minutes, then the coachee gives one line: "you coached me" or "you managed me," and why.
  4. Swap roles if time allows.

Flying solo? Self-coach in writing: answer the bank's questions about your own current challenge, one stage at a time. GROW works on paper too.

Section 04 · The Craft

Advice wearing a question mark.

"Have you tried delegating more?" isn't a question; it's your answer with punctuation. The International Coaching Federation calls the real thing a powerful question: open, curious, and aimed at their thinking, not your solution. Most manager questions fail the test.

Why this matters: hearing the difference between advice-in-disguise and real inquiry is the core coaching skill. Five manager lines; pick the rewrite that actually opens thinking.

The six-second rule

A powerful question deserves silence to work in. Ask it, then count six seconds in your head before you say anything else. The discomfort you feel is them thinking. Whoever breaks the silence first buys the problem.

Go deeper

In practice

Three tests for a powerful question, from the ICF's core competencies. Open: it can't be answered yes or no. Clean: it doesn't smuggle your answer in ("don't you think…", "have you tried…"). Theirs: it aims at their thinking, not your curiosity, so "what matters most to you here?" beats "what did your director say exactly?" And the most reliably powerful question in coaching is also the shortest: "What else?"

As a group · 5 min

Why this matters: converting your OWN advice into questions, out loud, is where the skill transfers from the slide to your mouth.

  1. Solo, one minute: write the advice you most recently gave someone on your team, word for word.
  2. Convert it into one open, clean question using the three tests above.
  3. Pairs: read your before and after aloud. Partner verdict: does the rewrite pass all three tests?
  4. Two or three best conversions get read to the room.

Flying solo? Do steps 1 and 2, then test your rewrite: could it be answered yes/no? Does it contain your answer? Aim it again if so.

Facilitator scriptFull 9 min · Core 6

Install the ICF's three tests (open, clean, theirs) and train the ear to catch advice wearing a question mark.

Say

“The International Coaching Federation calls the real thing a powerful question, and it has three tests. Open: can't be answered yes or no. Clean: your answer isn't hiding inside it. Theirs: it aims at their thinking, not your curiosity. Most manager questions fail test two. 'Have you tried delegating more?' isn't a question; it's advice with a question mark, and everyone in the room hears the difference. Then, after you ask a real one: silence. Six seconds. The discomfort you feel is them thinking.”

Do
  1. Open with the slide's line: 'have you tried delegating more?' is your answer with punctuation.
  2. Send them to the converter: five manager lines, pick the rewrite that actually opens thinking.
  3. Teach the six-second rule from the gold card, and model it: ask the room a question and visibly wait.
  4. Run the group activity: convert your OWN most recent advice into one clean question, pairs verdict, best two to the room.
  5. Write the room's best conversion on the whiteboard; it stays up for the rest of the session.
Ask

“Why is the silence after a good question so hard for managers specifically?”

Expect:

  • We're used to having the answer ready
  • Silence feels like failure / losing control of the meeting
  • We assume they're stuck and need rescuing

Respond: Name the reframe: 'Silence isn't the conversation breaking; it's the conversation working. Whoever fills it first buys the problem, and for years that's been you.'

Debrief

Three tests and six seconds. Your questions just got more powerful than most professional interviewers'.

Validate: 4+ of 5 on the converter; each learner has one before/after conversion of their own advice.

Watch for: Questions that pass 'open' but fail 'clean': 'what do you think about talking to them directly?' is still smuggling. Use the room's examples to show it.

Transition

“Questions coach the present. The next tool coaches the future, and it removes the single biggest source of defensiveness: the past.”

Section 05 · Feedforward

Skip the past. Coach the future.

Marshall Goldsmith's Feedforward flips development input on its head: instead of feedback about what went wrong, you ask for suggestions about what could go right next time. People can't change the past, so conversations about it breed defense. The future is all options.

Rule 1

Name the behavior

"I want to get better at running meetings." One behavior, stated by the person who wants to change it.

Rule 2

Future only

Suggestions must be about next time. Any sentence starting with "well, last week you…" is against the rules.

Rule 3

Only "thank you"

The receiver may not explain, defend, or even agree. Two words. That's what makes it safe to be honest.

Prefer a new tab? Open on YouTube.

Why this video: watch the inventor run the exercise you're about to do, in less time than the exercise takes.

Play · Past, future, or advice?

Why this matters: feedforward fails the moment the past sneaks back in. Train your ear on five real-sounding lines.

Go deeper

In practice

Goldsmith runs this with rooms of executives and the energy is always the same: fast, generous, and oddly fun, because nobody is being judged. The receiver ends up with four to six concrete ideas in ten minutes, more usable input than most annual reviews deliver. It also works one-on-one: "I'm working on interrupting less. Two ideas for next week?" is a complete feedforward request.

As a group · 10 min

Why this matters: this is the highest-energy exercise in the session and the one people take back to their teams verbatim. Run it by the rules and it sells itself.

  1. Form triads. Each person writes one behavior they genuinely want to improve.
  2. Round one: person A states their behavior; B and C each give two future-focused suggestions; A says only "thank you."
  3. Rotate until everyone has received. Enforce the rules kindly: no past, no defense.
  4. Debrief one question: how did it feel to receive suggestions with no right of reply?

Flying solo? Send the one-line request above to two colleagues today: your behavior, two ideas for next week. Reply "thank you," nothing else.

Facilitator scriptFull 12 min · Core 8

No explaining, no defending, no 'that wouldn't work here.' You are about to feel how different input lands when nobody is judging history.”

Watch for: Triads negotiating instead of receiving ('yeah but the reason I do that is...'). The 'thank you' rule is the exercise; guard it. Also watch the leftover pair; give them the two-suggestion variant.

Section 06 · Your Structure

One size fits most.

GROW is the default for a reason, but it isn't the only structure. Two respected alternatives cover the situations GROW handles less well. The best model is the one you'll actually use.

GROW

The all-rounder

Goal, Reality, Options, Will. Best for everyday coaching conversations. Watch for: rushing Options; "what else?" is the whole trick.

CLEAR

The relationship

Contract, Listen, Explore, Action, Review. Adds an explicit up-front agreement and a review loop. Best for ongoing coaching relationships.

OSKAR

The un-stucker

Outcome, Scaling, Know-how, Affirm & Action, Review. Solution-focused: "you're at a 4; what's already working?" Best for coachees stuck in problem-analysis.

Play · Match the model

Why this matters: choosing a structure on purpose, instead of defaulting, is what turns a model from trivia into a tool. Four coachees; pick the best-fit model for each.

Go deeper

In practice

Don't overthink the choice. GROW for most conversations; CLEAR when the relationship needs an explicit contract ("what do you want from me in these sessions?"); OSKAR when someone has analyzed the problem to death and needs to look at what's already working. The models share one spine: their goal, their reality, their plan. Short explainers if you want them: Peter Hawkins on CLEAR (4 min) and an OSKAR walkthrough (5 min).

As a group · 3 min

Why this matters: saying your choice out loud, with a reason, is a rehearsal for the capstone plan in ten minutes.

  1. In pairs: which model fits how you naturally talk, and why?
  2. Name one person on your team, role only, for whom a DIFFERENT model would work better.

Flying solo? Answer both questions in your notes. Your capstone will ask for the choice in writing.

Facilitator scriptFull 7 min · Core 3

Say

For the person who has analyzed the problem to death, 'you're at a four; what's keeping it from being a two?' points them at what's already working. Pick the one that sounds like your voice.”

Do
  1. Walk the three cards: GROW the all-rounder, CLEAR for ongoing relationships, OSKAR for the stuck.
  2. Keep it light: the models share one spine, and the best one is the one they'll use.
  3. Send them to the matcher: four coachees, pick the best-fit model.
  4. Run the pair activity: which model fits how you naturally talk, and who on your team needs a different one?

Expect:

  • GROW, it's simple and I'll remember it
  • CLEAR, I like setting the contract up front
  • OSKAR, I have exactly that over-analyzer on my team

Section 07 · Judgment

Sometimes, just tell them.

Coaching is a tool, not a religion. Harvard's leadership research calls the skill situational coaching: knowing when to ask and when to direct. A manager who coaches during a fire alarm is as broken as one who directs everything.

Direct when

  • There's a genuine emergency or a hard deadline in hours
  • Safety, policy, or compliance leaves no discretion
  • The person is brand new and needs instruction, not exploration
  • They've asked for a decision you genuinely own

Coach when

  • They're capable of solving it and would grow from doing so
  • The same problem keeps coming back to your desk
  • There's more than one right answer
  • It's a development conversation, not a status update

Quick check: A new hire, day three, asks how to submit a payroll correction before today's cutoff. You…

Go deeper

In practice

The working heuristic: match the mode to the moment, then default to coaching. Most managers have the ratio backwards, directing most of the time and coaching only when there's spare time, which is never. The research argument for flipping the ratio is Ibarra and Scoular's "The Leader as Coach" (HBR); their situational grid is worth the read this week.

On your own · 3 min

Why this matters: your telling moment from Section 02 gets its verdict here, with the framework instead of guilt.

  1. Take the moment you wrote in Section 02, where you solved it for them.
  2. Run it against the two lists above: was it honestly a directing moment, or a coaching moment you directed?
  3. If it was a coaching moment, write the opening question you WISH you'd asked. You'll want it for the capstone.
Facilitator scriptFull 6 min · Core 4

Install situational judgment (Ibarra & Scoular) so the new coaching habit doesn't overcorrect into coaching fire alarms.

Say

“Now the guardrail. Coaching is a tool, not a religion. When there's a fire, you point at the door; you don't ask 'what options do you see?' Direct when there's a genuine emergency, when compliance leaves no discretion, when someone is brand new, or when the decision is honestly yours. Coach when they're capable and would grow, when the same problem keeps returning to your desk, when there's more than one right answer. The skill is matching the mode to the moment, and then defaulting to coach, because your ratio is currently backwards.”

Do
  1. Walk the two lists: direct when (emergency, compliance, novice, your decision) and coach when (capable, recurring, multiple answers, development).

  4. Run the solo activity: their Section 02 telling moment gets its verdict against the lists.

  • A brand-new team member's first week
  • An escalation the manager genuinely owned

Respond: Validate them: 'Right, and notice how specific those are. The trap isn't directing in those moments; it's letting those moments become the excuse for directing in all the others.'

Watch for: The convenient conclusion that most of their directing was justified. Push gently: 'If the same problem has come back three times, it wasn't a directing moment.'
