Section 01 · The Case

Google measured it.
Coaching came first.

Project Oxygen, Google's decade-long study of its own managers, ranked the behaviors of the highest-rated ones. Number one wasn't technical skill or decisiveness. It was "is a good coach." McKinsey describes the same shift: from controller, who has the answers, to coach, who operates with a mindset of discovery.

Section 02 · The Default

You were promoted for answers.

That's the trap. Excellent doers become managers, and under pressure they do what made them excellent: solve it themselves, fast. Daniel Goleman's research names the styles; the coaching style, developing people for the future, is the one great managers reach for most and stressed managers reach for least.

Directing

"Do it this way."

You hand over the answer. Fast, and right for a crisis. As a habit, it teaches your team to bring you every problem, forever.

Pacesetting

"Watch me do it."

You take it back and do it yourself, brilliantly. The work ships, the person shrinks, and your calendar becomes the bottleneck.

Coaching

"What do you think?"

You ask, they discover, they own it. Slower today. Compounding forever. This is the style Project Oxygen found on top.

Play · Catch the style

Why this matters: you can't change a default you can't hear. Five manager lines; name what each one is really doing.

Go deeper

In practice

McKinsey calls the shift "from controller to coach": from the leader who provides certainty to the one who leads with discovery. The tell that you're in controller mode is physical: you're talking more than they are, and you feel the itch to interrupt with the answer. The fix costs one breath: swallow the answer, and ask "what have you tried?" For the full styles model, watch Goleman on the coaching style (13 min) this week.

Section 03 · The Model

Four letters run the conversation.

GROW is the most widely used coaching structure in the world, and the one Google teaches its own managers. Four stages, each powered by questions, ending in commitment.

G · Goal

Where to?

"What would make this conversation useful?" Their goal, in their words, before anything else.

R · Reality

Where now?

"What's actually happening? What have you tried?" Facts before options; no diagnosis from you.

O · Options

What could you do?

"What else?" asked three times. The fourth option is usually better than the first.

W · Will

What will you do?

"What, by when, how committed, 1 to 10?" Anything under 8 means the plan isn't theirs yet.

Section 04 · The Craft

Advice wearing a question mark.

"Have you tried delegating more?" isn't a question; it's your answer with punctuation. The International Coaching Federation calls the real thing a powerful question: open, curious, and aimed at their thinking, not your solution. Most manager questions fail the test.

Play · The converter

Why this matters: hearing the difference between advice-in-disguise and real inquiry is the core coaching skill. Five manager lines; pick the rewrite that actually opens thinking.

The six-second rule

A powerful question deserves silence to work in. Ask it, then count six seconds in your head before you say anything else. The discomfort you feel is them thinking. Whoever breaks the silence first buys the problem.

Go deeper

In practice

Three tests for a powerful question, from the ICF's core competencies. Open: it can't be answered yes or no. Clean: it doesn't smuggle your answer in ("don't you think…", "have you tried…"). Theirs: it aims at their thinking, not your curiosity, so "what matters most to you here?" beats "what did your director say exactly?" And the most reliably powerful question in coaching is also the shortest: "What else?"

On your own

Do this now: Do steps 1 and 2, then test your rewrite: could it be answered yes/no? Does it contain your answer? Aim it again if so.

Section 05 · Feedforward

Skip the past. Coach the future.

Marshall Goldsmith's Feedforward flips development input on its head: instead of feedback about what went wrong, you ask for suggestions about what could go right next time. People can't change the past, so conversations about it breed defense. The future is all options.

Rule 1

Name the behavior

"I want to get better at running meetings." One behavior, stated by the person who wants to change it.

Rule 2

Future only

Suggestions must be about next time. Any sentence starting with "well, last week you…" is against the rules.

Rule 3

Only "thank you"

The receiver may not explain, defend, or even agree. Two words. That's what makes it safe to be honest.

Section 06 · Your Structure

One size fits most.

GROW is the default for a reason, but it isn't the only structure. Two respected alternatives cover the situations GROW handles less well. The best model is the one you'll actually use.

GROW

The all-rounder

Goal, Reality, Options, Will. Best for everyday coaching conversations. Watch for: rushing Options; "what else?" is the whole trick.

CLEAR

The relationship

Contract, Listen, Explore, Action, Review. Adds an explicit up-front agreement and a review loop. Best for ongoing coaching relationships.

OSKAR

The un-stucker

Outcome, Scaling, Know-how, Affirm & Action, Review. Solution-focused: "you're at a 4; what's already working?" Best for coachees stuck in problem-analysis.

Play · Match the model

Why this matters: choosing a structure on purpose, instead of defaulting, is what turns a model from trivia into a tool. Four coachees; pick the best-fit model for each.

Go deeper

In practice

Don't overthink the choice. GROW for most conversations; CLEAR when the relationship needs an explicit contract ("what do you want from me in these sessions?"); OSKAR when someone has analyzed the problem to death and needs to look at what's already working. The models share one spine: their goal, their reality, their plan. Short explainers if you want them: Peter Hawkins on CLEAR (4 min) and an OSKAR walkthrough (5 min).

Section 07 · Judgment

Sometimes, just tell them.

Coaching is a tool, not a religion. Harvard's leadership research calls the skill situational coaching: knowing when to ask and when to direct. A manager who coaches during a fire alarm is as broken as one who directs everything.

Direct when

  • There's a genuine emergency or a hard deadline in hours
  • Safety, policy, or compliance leaves no discretion
  • The person is brand new and needs instruction, not exploration
  • They've asked for a decision you genuinely own

Coach when

  • They're capable of solving it and would grow from doing so
  • The same problem keeps coming back to your desk
  • There's more than one right answer
  • It's a development conversation, not a status update

Quick check: A new hire, day three, asks how to submit a payroll correction before today's cutoff. You…

Go deeper

In practice

The working heuristic: match the mode to the moment, then default to coaching. Most managers have the ratio backwards, directing most of the time and coaching only when there's spare time, which is never. The research argument for flipping the ratio is Ibarra and Scoular's "The Leader as Coach" (HBR); their situational grid is worth the read this week.
