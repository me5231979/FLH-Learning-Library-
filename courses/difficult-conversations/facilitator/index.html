05 · When things go sideways

If it breaks, you have a move

If Projector or the site fails mid-session: Teach from the printed cheat sheet: SBI, STATE, the three conversations, and the quadrant grid all work on a whiteboard. The pair practice needs no screen at all.
If Learner devices can't join (wifi/filters): Run facilitator-only: interactions happen on the projector with volunteers calling choices; private exercises (avoidance slider, BRAVING, the capstone) move to the printed capstone worksheet or sticky notes. You lose typing, not learning.
If You're 10+ minutes behind by Radical Candor (Section 06): Declare the Core path silently: cut remaining group debriefs and the in-class videos. Never cut the STATE pair rep, the recap, or the capstone; they carry objectives 3 and 5.
If Silence after you ask a question: Count seven full seconds before rescuing; in this course especially, silence is people deciding whether to be honest. If it holds, narrow it: 'This table: Ruinous Empathy or Obnoxious Aggression?' Never answer your own question first.
If One voice dominates, or someone starts naming real colleagues: For dominators: 'Hold that; parking lot,' then invite two unheard voices. For naming names: step in immediately and kindly: 'Roles, not names, protects everyone in this room, including you.' Repeat the no-names norm; it is load-bearing.
If Someone gets emotional or discloses a live, serious conflict: Do not process it in the room. Acknowledge briefly ('That sounds genuinely hard, and I want to do it justice; find me at the break'), continue, and follow up privately. If it involves policy violations, harassment, or safety, route them to HR, not to this toolkit.

06 · Tough questions, ready answers

Say each answer out loud once before you teach

What if the difficult conversation I need is with my own boss?

The tools point up as well as down. STATE was built for high-stakes talks with people who outrank you: facts first, story owned tentatively, a genuine ask. Pick your timing, be extra generous in interpretation, and remember McKinsey's frame: surfacing a real problem is part of your job, not insubordination.

I've let it slide for a year. Isn't it too late and too awkward now?

It's more awkward, and still worth it. Name the delay honestly as yours: 'I should have raised this months ago, and that's on me.' That one sentence takes the ambush out of it and models the accountability you're about to ask for.

What about the person who cries or blows up every single time?

A predictable reaction doesn't erase the standard; it changes your preparation. Plan the pause, have your contrast statement ready, pick a private time with slack after it. If the pattern repeats across many conversations, bring in your HR partner; that's a support structure, not an escalation.

Isn't scripting a conversation manipulative?

You script yourself, not them. The opener and contrast statement exist because adrenaline makes everyone a worse version of themselves in the first 30 seconds. Preparing to be clear and kind under stress is care, not control. The other person still gets to change your mind; that's what the Ask step is for.

What if it turns out I'm the one who's wrong?

Then the conversation worked. You traded a resentment for information. That's why STATE ends with 'encourage testing'; if you weren't willing to be wrong, it was a lecture with a question mark.

When does this stop being a conversation and become an HR matter?

The toolkit covers performance, behavior, and working-relationship conversations. Policy violations, anything touching protected classes, safety concerns, or formal performance processes belong with HR from the start. When in doubt, consult HR before the conversation, not after it goes wrong.

Staying quiet is not neutral; it's a vote for things staying exactly as they are.

Section 02 · The Model

Every hard talk is three talks.

The Harvard Negotiation Project found that inside every difficult conversation, three conversations run at once. "Just stick to the facts" fails because the facts are only one of them.

Conversation 1

What Happened

The battle over the facts: who said what, who's right, who's to blame. Each of you holds a different, sincere story about the same events.

Conversation 2

Feelings

The emotions underneath: frustration, hurt, anxiety. Unspoken, they leak into tone and word choice anyway. Named, they lose their grip.

Conversation 3

Identity

The quiet one you have with yourself: "What does this say about me?" Am I competent? Am I a good manager? This one drives the avoidance.

Why this matters: in the room, these three layers blur together. Learning to tell them apart is what lets you respond to the right one.

Go deeper

In practice

The escape from the What Happened battle is the third story: the version a neutral observer would tell, where both views are real. "I think we see the deadline differently, and I'd like to understand your side" opens a door. "You missed the deadline" closes one. And when a facts argument keeps going in circles, it's usually an identity argument in disguise. The circle is the tell.

Section 03 · SBI Feedback

Specific beats vague.

"You're always late" starts a fight. The Center for Creative Leadership's SBI model turns the same concern into something a person can actually hear and act on. Hover or tap each part to find it in a real example.

In Monday's client review, you presented the draft numbers before we'd agreed on the final version, and the client saw two different totals. It cost us credibility in the room, and I spent the afternoon repairing it.

Quick check: Which of these is a Behavior statement, the way SBI means it?

Go deeper

In practice

Two upgrades once SBI is habit. First, SBI-I: after the impact, ask about intent: "What was going on for you?" It replaces your guess about their motive with their answer. Second, ATD's three Rs: feedback is reinforcing (do more of this), redirecting (change this), or reprimanding (this crossed a line). Most managers underuse the first and blur the last two. Prefer video to prose? SBI with worked examples (8 min).

On your own · 4 min

Why this matters: Your avoided conversation almost certainly contains a vague accusation. This turns it into something hearable before you're in the room.

  1. Take the conversation you named in Section 01 and write the concern as you'd blurt it ("they keep missing deadlines").
  2. Rewrite it as SBI: one specific situation, the observable behavior, the concrete impact.
  3. Read both versions. Notice which one you could actually say to their face.
Facilitator scriptFull 7 min · Core 5

Replace vague accusation with the Situation-Behavior-Impact structure, and install the camera test for behavior.

Say

“'You're always late' starts a fight. Not because it's too honest, but because it's not honest enough: no time, no place, no specifics, just a verdict. The Center for Creative Leadership's fix has three parts. Situation: when and where, so it's a fact, not a pattern-accusation. Behavior: what a camera would have seen. Impact: what it cost, concretely. Watch what happens to the same complaint when it goes through that machine.”

Do
  1. Walk the anatomy on the projector: hover Situation, then Behavior, then Impact, reading each highlighted piece.
  2. Teach the camera test out loud: if a camera couldn't have recorded it, it's a judgment, not a behavior.
  3. Run the quick check; option C ('you clearly don't respect the team's time') is the useful wrong answer, it's a mind-read.
  4. Send them to the solo activity: rewrite their own avoided concern as SBI, next to their note from Section 01.
Ask

“What's the actual difference between 'you're unreliable' and 'the report went out with last quarter's numbers'?”

Expect:

  • One is opinion, one is fact
  • You can argue with a label but not with the numbers
  • One attacks the person, one describes the work

Respond: Exactly: one is contestable and one is checkable. People defend themselves against verdicts; they solve problems. SBI keeps the problem the problem.

Debrief

The camera test is the whole slide: if a camera couldn't have seen it, don't say it as if it's a fact.

Validate: Quick check correct; learners have a written SBI version of their own concern.

Watch for: SBI statements that smuggle the verdict back in ('...and it was really disappointing'). Flag it kindly: impact is a cost, not a grade.

“Structure alone isn't enough; you can deliver a perfect SBI and still watch it land like an attack. Let's feel that difference.”

Practice · Feedback Lab

Build one that lands.

Your task: a teammate has come to your last two planning meetings unprepared, and you need to say so. Choose one option for each part of the feedback, then deliver it and see how it lands.

Why this matters: feeling the reaction change as each part sharpens is the fastest way to internalize SBI before you write your own. The graded reactions are illustrative, not a simulation of any real person.

Choose all three parts to deliver
Facilitator scriptFull 8 min · Core 6

Let learners feel how the reaction changes as each SBI part sharpens; the reaction is the teacher.

Say

“Same concern, three ways to say each part. Your job: assemble the feedback and watch how it lands. I'll go first, and I'm going to do it badly on purpose, because you've all heard this version.”

Do
  1. Frame the scenario: a teammate came unprepared to the last two planning meetings, and you're saying so.
  2. On the projector, deliberately build the WORST version (A-A-A) and deliver it. Let the defensive reaction land on the room.
  3. Send them to their devices: build and deliver their own, iterate until it lands.
  4. Circulate. Ask people showing a mid-tier result which part is costing them.
  5. Pull the room back and ask who got the strong reaction, and what they changed to get it.
Ask

“When I delivered the worst version, what did the reply defend: the work, or the person's character?”

Expect:

  • Their character, they brought up staying late
  • They never addressed the actual problem
  • They counter-attacked

Respond: Right. Attack character, and character is what gets defended. The status doc never came up. Vague feedback isn't gentler; it just changes the subject from the problem to the person.

Debrief

The reaction you get is diagnostic: defending the work means your feedback was about the work. Defending themselves means it wasn't.

Validate: Most of the room reaches the strong (8-9/9) outcome within the time box.

Watch for: Option-B-everywhere players getting a mediocre result and shrugging: push them to find WHICH slot is weakest, that's the diagnostic habit.

Section 04 · The Opening

Candid, not combative.

SBI gives you the content. The Crucial Conversations STATE model gives you the delivery: a way to say something honest while keeping the other person safe enough to hear it. Hover or tap each move to see it in the opener.

I might be missing part of the picture. How does it look from where you sit? If you see it differently, I genuinely want to hear it.

Pair with a neighbor. One of you reads the opener on this slide aloud; the other listens for one thing only: did it feel safe, or did it feel like an accusation? Then swap. The listener's ear, not the speaker's intent, is the measure. You'll use the same test on your own opener in the capstone.

As a group · 6 min

Why this matters: Saying a STATE opener out loud once, to a real face, is worth ten silent rehearsals. This is the practice rep the research says drives behavior change.

  1. In pairs: take the SBI statement you wrote in Section 03 and open it with STATE, out loud, to your partner.
  2. Partner gives exactly one line of feedback: "that felt safe" or "that felt like an accusation," and why.
  3. Swap roles. Then each of you adjusts one word or phrase based on what you heard.

Flying solo? Say your opener out loud to the mirror or record it on your phone, then listen as the receiver. Same test: safe, or accusation?

Facilitator scriptFull 8 min · Core 4

You're sure about the dates; you're guessing about the why.”

  • First one is about them, second is about the situation

Section 05 · Trust

Feedback lands on trust.

Brené Brown's research says trust isn't one big thing; it's seven small behaviors, built or broken in tiny moments. Simon Sinek adds the leadership frame: people can only hear hard feedback inside a circle of safety. Rate yourself, privately, on each behavior below.

Why this matters: your lowest score is usually the reason your hardest conversation feels risky. Strengthen the behavior and the conversation gets easier before you say a word. Nothing here is saved or shared.

Go deeper

In practice

The inventory works in both directions: it's also the checklist for how trust gets broken. Sharing what wasn't yours to share (Vault), promising what you can't deliver (Reliability), judging someone for asking for help (Nonjudgment). Before a hard conversation, ask: which of these have I banked with this person, and which have I broken? Go to the source: Brené Brown, "The Anatomy of Trust" (9 min) and Sinek, "Why good leaders make you feel safe" (12 min).

As a group · 4 min

Why this matters: Trust talk gets abstract fast. Anchoring it to one behavior makes it a to-do, not a virtue.

  1. Pair up. Without sharing scores, each person names the ONE behavior they'd strengthen this quarter.
  2. Ask each other: what would strengthening it look like this week, concretely?
  3. Around the room, popcorn style: behaviors only, no stories. Listen for the room's pattern.

Flying solo? Circle your lowest score and write one sentence: the first small thing you'll do about it this week.

Facilitator scriptFull 8 min · Core 4

Shift trust from an abstraction to seven rateable behaviors, and have each learner privately find their growth edge.

Say

“Why does the same sentence land fine from one manager and like a knife from another? The difference isn't the sentence; it's the account it's drawn on. Your lowest score is probably the reason your hardest conversation feels dangerous.”

Do
  1. Frame with Sinek: people hear hard feedback only inside a circle of safety, and the circle is built before the conversation, not during.
  2. Introduce BRAVING as Brené Brown's anatomy of trust: seven behaviors, built or broken in small moments.

  4. When the growth edge appears, give a quiet minute to read their behavior's suggestion.
  5. Run the pair activity: name ONE behavior to strengthen this quarter, no scores, no stories, then popcorn behaviors to the room and name the pattern you hear.

“Without sharing scores: which of the seven behaviors, when a MANAGER breaks it, kills trust fastest?”

Expect:

  • Vault, they repeat what you told them in confidence
  • Reliability, they promise and don't deliver
  • Nonjudgment, asking for help gets held against you

Respond: Vault usually wins the vote, and notice why: one leak reclassifies every future conversation as unsafe. Whatever the room picks, the lesson is the same: trust breaks by behavior, so it rebuilds by behavior too.

Debrief

Trust isn't a vibe, it's seven behaviors, which means it's trainable. Pick one; that's your quarter.

Validate: All seven rows rated (the tool won't show a result otherwise); each learner can name their one behavior.

Watch for: This is where someone may go quiet or emotional; trust inventories touch real relationships. Don't spotlight anyone; the exercise is private by design.

Transition

“With trust as the ground, here's the map for the feedback itself: two axes, four quadrants, one worth aiming at.”

Section 06 · Radical Candor

Care personally.
Challenge directly.

Kim Scott's two axes sort every piece of feedback a manager gives. Do both and you get Radical Candor. Drop one and you land in a failure quadrant, and each has a name.

Care personally ↑

Ruinous Empathy

Care without challenge. You spare their feelings today and their career pays tomorrow. The most common manager failure.

Radical Candor

Care and challenge together. "I'm telling you because I want you to succeed." Kind and clear.

Manipulative Insincerity

Neither care nor challenge. Praise to the face, complaints behind the back. Poison for trust.

Obnoxious Aggression

Challenge without care. Technically honest, delivered like a verdict, often in public. It's heard as an attack.

Challenge directly →

Why this video: Scott tells the story the whole framework grew from, in the time it takes to pour a coffee.

Play · Sort the quadrant

Why this matters: you'll never label your own feedback in the moment unless you've practiced labeling someone else's first.

Go deeper

In practice

Scott's sharpest line: candor is measured at the listener's ear, not the speaker's mouth. "I was just being honest" is not a defense if it landed as an attack. And the fix for Ruinous Empathy isn't harshness; it's remembering that withholding the feedback is the unkind option. The kindest thing you can do for someone's career is tell them the truth while there's still time to act on it.

As a group · 5 min

Why this matters: Knowing your default failure quadrant under stress is the single most useful piece of self-knowledge this framework offers.

  1. Private vote, hands or chat: under stress, do you drift toward Ruinous Empathy (soften too much) or Obnoxious Aggression (push too hard)?
  2. In pairs: what triggers your drift? Deadline pressure, seniority of the other person, being tired?
  3. Trade one counter-move each: the thing you do to pull yourself back to candor.

Flying solo? Name your drift and its trigger, then write the counter-move on the same note as your conversation from Section 01.

Section 07 · The Opening Line

The first 30 seconds set the course.

SHRM's field guidance gives the conversation its spine: three questions, in order. Answer them before you walk in and the talk has a direction. Skip them and it becomes a feelings spiral with no exit.

Question 1

Where are we now?

The current situation, in SBI terms: what's happening, observably, and what it's costing. This is your opening line's raw material.

Question 2

Where are we going?

The outcome you both want: the standard met, the relationship intact. If you can't name it, you're not ready to start.

Question 3

How do we get there?

Built together, not delivered. The other person owns the plan they helped write. Yours alone, they comply at best.

The opener, assembled

"I want to talk about [the topic], because I want [the shared outcome]. Here's what I've noticed: [situation, behavior, impact]. I might not have the full picture. How do you see it?"

Purpose first, facts second, their view invited before yours hardens. Every framework today is inside that one paragraph.

Quick check: Which first sentence gives the conversation the best chance?

Go deeper

In practice

Two delivery details that outweigh any script. Timing: hard conversations go better scheduled and private, never ambushed and never in front of an audience. "Do you have 20 minutes tomorrow morning? I want to talk through the project timeline" is respect in one sentence. Feedforward: McKinsey's research favors spending most of the talk on the future ("what would make the next one land on time?") over relitigating the past. The past gets two minutes; the future gets the rest.

On your own · 4 min

Why this matters: An opener written now, calm, is what your mouth reaches for later, stressed. This is the single highest-leverage artifact of the session.

  1. Take the assembled opener above and fill in the brackets for YOUR conversation from Section 01.
  2. Answer SHRM's three questions in one line each: where are we, where are we going, how do we get there.
  3. Read your opener out loud once, quietly. If a word makes you wince, change it now.

Section 08 · Recovery

Plan for the reaction.

Even a perfect opener can meet tears, anger, or silence. None of those mean you were wrong to raise it. They mean the other person stopped feeling safe, and safety is the thing you restore first, before any more content.

Tears / distress

Pause. Don't retract.

Acknowledge the emotion, offer a moment or a break, and stay kind. But don't take the message back to stop the tears; that trades their growth for your comfort.

Anger / defensiveness

Contrast, then continue.

Use a contrast statement: "I don't mean you're failing at this job. I do mean these two deadlines slipped and we need a plan." Kill the wrong conclusion, keep the real one.

Silence

Ask, then wait.

Ask a real question and count to six in your head. Silence is often processing, not stonewalling. Whoever fills the silence first learns nothing.

The permission slip

If safety collapses, you can pause the conversation without dropping the standard: "Let's take a break and pick this up tomorrow at 10." A paused conversation resumed is a success. An abandoned one is Ruinous Empathy with extra steps.

Then it's their turn · Listen like it matters

You asked how they see it. What you do with the answer decides whether they ever answer honestly again. Three moves:

  1. Paraphrase before you reply. "So what I'm hearing is…" Prove you heard it before you answer it.
  2. Ask one real follow-up. "What else should I know?" The second answer is usually the real one.
  3. Acknowledge before you counter. You don't have to agree to say "that makes sense from where you sit." It costs nothing and buys the rest of the conversation.

Quick check: They snap back, "So you're saying I'm bad at my job?" Best response?

Go deeper

In practice

Simon Sinek's framing is the one to carry out of the room: your job in the hard moment is to keep the person inside the circle of safety while the problem stands outside it. "You and me against the problem" survives tears, anger, and silence. "Me against you" survives nothing. And the whole meeting has an arc; ATD's CLEAR model names it: Connect (purpose first), Listen (the moves above), Explain (your SBI), Act (build the plan together), Review (agree what happens next and when you'll check in). Most managers over-invest in Explain and skip Listen and Review.

As a group · 6 min

Why this matters: You can't improvise a contrast statement mid-adrenaline, and you can't fake listening. Both get one rep here, while it's cheap.

  1. For your Section 01 conversation, predict the most likely hard reaction: tears, anger, or silence.
  2. Draft your contrast statement: "I don't mean ___. I do mean ___." Say it to a partner: safety, or lawyer's disclaimer?
  3. Now the listening rep: partner talks for 60 seconds about a mild work frustration of their own.
  4. You may respond ONLY with a paraphrase and one follow-up question. No advice, no rebuttal. Then swap.

Flying solo? Draft the contrast statement, then write the paraphrase opener you'll use: "So what I'm hearing is ___." You now have an opener, a recovery line, and a listening plan.
