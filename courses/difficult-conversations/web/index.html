The institutional why · Crescere aude

Define the great university. Then be it.

Chancellor Diermeier's institutional vision is one sentence with no hedge in it: Vanderbilt will define the great university of the 21st century, and be it. The how is equally blunt: operate at uncommon speed, agility, and scale to address society's challenges through education and research, and help reclaim the essential role of leading research universities in driving American prosperity. Our motto has always been the dare, Crescere aude, dare to grow, and the conversation you have been putting off is exactly that kind of dare.

Focus 1 · Exceptional core operations

Speed, agility, scale

Innovative, impactful capabilities that secure the talent, resources, and reputation the vision needs. The team translation: problems move at the speed they are spoken, and SBI feedback gets a concern to the person who can fix it while it is still small.

Focus 2 · Bold strategic initiatives

Core ops as fuel

Increasingly bold bets that extend the reach and impact of education and research. The team translation: a bold bet needs the truth early, and a manager who can open a hard conversation with STATE is how the truth arrives before the deadline does.

Focus 3 · Industry leadership

Model the essential university

Stimulate industry change by modeling what a research university should be. The team translation: a team whose manager pairs candor with care, and keeps trust through the hard conversations, is the working model other institutions get shown.

The reputation flywheel

Reputation and resources feed each other in a loop, reputation attracts talent, funding, partnerships, and access, which build the reputation. The university spins that flywheel with execution with ambition, values-driven leadership, and athletics momentum. Here's your place in it: talent stays where trust is real, and trust is built or spent in exactly the conversations this course teaches. Every hard thing said early, with SBI's fairness and STATE's care, is a problem that never grows big enough to reach the reputation.

Section 01 · The Cost

Silence is a decision.

Think of one difficult conversation you're avoiding right now. Almost everyone in this room has one. McKinsey's research on courageous conversations reframes the choice: speaking up isn't a personality trait, it's an obligation to dissent. Staying quiet is not neutral; it's a vote for things staying exactly as they are.

Section 02 · The Model

Every hard talk is three talks.

The Harvard Negotiation Project found that inside every difficult conversation, three conversations run at once. "Just stick to the facts" fails because the facts are only one of them.

Conversation 1

What Happened

The battle over the facts: who said what, who's right, who's to blame. Each of you holds a different, sincere story about the same events.

Conversation 2

Feelings

The emotions underneath: frustration, hurt, anxiety. Unspoken, they leak into tone and word choice anyway. Named, they lose their grip.

Conversation 3

Identity

The quiet one you have with yourself: "What does this say about me?" Am I competent? Am I a good manager? This one drives the avoidance.

In practice

The escape from the What Happened battle is the third story: the version a neutral observer would tell, where both views are real. "I think we see the deadline differently, and I'd like to understand your side" opens a door. "You missed the deadline" closes one. And when a facts argument keeps going in circles, it's usually an identity argument in disguise. The circle is the tell.

Section 03 · SBI Feedback

Specific beats vague.

"You're always late" starts a fight. The Center for Creative Leadership's SBI model turns the same concern into something a person can actually hear and act on. Hover or tap each part to find it in a real example.

In Monday's client review, you presented the draft numbers before we'd agreed on the final version, and the client saw two different totals. It cost us credibility in the room, and I spent the afternoon repairing it.

Quick check: Which of these is a Behavior statement, the way SBI means it?

Go deeper

In practice

Two upgrades once SBI is habit. First, SBI-I: after the impact, ask about intent: "What was going on for you?" It replaces your guess about their motive with their answer. Second, ATD's three Rs: feedback is reinforcing (do more of this), redirecting (change this), or reprimanding (this crossed a line). Most managers underuse the first and blur the last two. Section 04 · The Opening

Candid, not combative.

SBI gives you the content. The Crucial Conversations STATE model gives you the delivery: a way to say something honest while keeping the other person safe enough to hear it. Hover or tap each move to see it in the opener.

The last two releases went out four days after the date we committed to. I'm starting to worry that we're overcommitting, and that it's putting us in a bad spot with the client. I might be missing part of the picture. How does it look from where you sit? If you see it differently, I genuinely want to hear it.

Section 05 · Trust

Feedback lands on trust.

Brené Brown's research says trust isn't one big thing; it's seven small behaviors, built or broken in tiny moments. Simon Sinek adds the leadership frame: people can only hear hard feedback inside a circle of safety. Rate yourself, privately, on each behavior below.

Why this matters: your lowest score is usually the reason your hardest conversation feels risky. Strengthen the behavior and the conversation gets easier before you say a word. Nothing here is saved or shared.

Go deeper

In practice

The inventory works in both directions: it's also the checklist for how trust gets broken. Sharing what wasn't yours to share (Vault), promising what you can't deliver (Reliability), judging someone for asking for help (Nonjudgment). Before a hard conversation, ask: which of these have I banked with this person, and which have I broken? Section 06 · Radical Candor

Care personally.
Challenge directly.

Kim Scott's two axes sort every piece of feedback a manager gives. Do both and you get Radical Candor. Drop one and you land in a failure quadrant, and each has a name.

Care personally ↑

Ruinous Empathy

Care without challenge. You spare their feelings today and their career pays tomorrow. The most common manager failure.

Radical Candor

Care and challenge together. "I'm telling you because I want you to succeed." Kind and clear.

Manipulative Insincerity

Neither care nor challenge. Praise to the face, complaints behind the back. Poison for trust.

Obnoxious Aggression

Challenge without care. Technically honest, delivered like a verdict, often in public. It's heard as an attack.

In practice

Scott's sharpest line: candor is measured at the listener's ear, not the speaker's mouth. "I was just being honest" is not a defense if it landed as an attack. And the fix for Ruinous Empathy isn't harshness; it's remembering that withholding the feedback is the unkind option. The kindest thing you can do for someone's career is tell them the truth while there's still time to act on it.

Section 07 · The Opening Line

The first 30 seconds set the course.

SHRM's field guidance gives the conversation its spine: three questions, in order. Answer them before you walk in and the talk has a direction. Skip them and it becomes a feelings spiral with no exit.

Question 1

Where are we now?

The current situation, in SBI terms: what's happening, observably, and what it's costing. This is your opening line's raw material.

Question 2

Where are we going?

The outcome you both want: the standard met, the relationship intact. If you can't name it, you're not ready to start.

Question 3

How do we get there?

Built together, not delivered. The other person owns the plan they helped write. Yours alone, they comply at best.

The opener, assembled

"I want to talk about [the topic], because I want [the shared outcome]. Here's what I've noticed: [situation, behavior, impact]. I might not have the full picture. How do you see it?"

Purpose first, facts second, their view invited before yours hardens. Every framework today is inside that one paragraph.

Quick check: Which first sentence gives the conversation the best chance?

Go deeper

In practice

Two delivery details that outweigh any script. Timing: hard conversations go better scheduled and private, never ambushed and never in front of an audience. "Do you have 20 minutes tomorrow morning? I want to talk through the project timeline" is respect in one sentence. Feedforward: McKinsey's research favors spending most of the talk on the future ("what would make the next one land on time?") over relitigating the past. The past gets two minutes; the future gets the rest.

  2. Answer SHRM's three questions in one line each: where are we, where are we going, how do we get there.

Section 08 · Recovery

Plan for the reaction.

Even a perfect opener can meet tears, anger, or silence. None of those mean you were wrong to raise it. They mean the other person stopped feeling safe, and safety is the thing you restore first, before any more content.

Tears / distress

Pause. Don't retract.

Acknowledge the emotion, offer a moment or a break, and stay kind. But don't take the message back to stop the tears; that trades their growth for your comfort.

Anger / defensiveness

Contrast, then continue.

Use a contrast statement: "I don't mean you're failing at this job. I do mean these two deadlines slipped and we need a plan." Kill the wrong conclusion, keep the real one.

Silence

Ask, then wait.

Ask a real question and count to six in your head. Silence is often processing, not stonewalling. Whoever fills the silence first learns nothing.

The permission slip

If safety collapses, you can pause the conversation without dropping the standard: "Let's take a break and pick this up tomorrow at 10." A paused conversation resumed is a success. An abandoned one is Ruinous Empathy with extra steps.

Then it's their turn · Listen like it matters

You asked how they see it. What you do with the answer decides whether they ever answer honestly again. Three moves:

  1. Paraphrase before you reply. "So what I'm hearing is…" Prove you heard it before you answer it.
  2. Ask one real follow-up. "What else should I know?" The second answer is usually the real one.
  3. Acknowledge before you counter. You don't have to agree to say "that makes sense from where you sit." It costs nothing and buys the rest of the conversation.

Quick check: They snap back, "So you're saying I'm bad at my job?" Best response?

Go deeper

In practice

Simon Sinek's framing is the one to carry out of the room: your job in the hard moment is to keep the person inside the circle of safety while the problem stands outside it. "You and me against the problem" survives tears, anger, and silence. "Me against you" survives nothing. And the whole meeting has an arc; ATD's CLEAR model names it: Connect (purpose first), Listen (the moves above), Explain (your SBI), Act (build the plan together), Review (agree what happens next and when you'll check in). Most managers over-invest in Explain and skip Listen and Review.
