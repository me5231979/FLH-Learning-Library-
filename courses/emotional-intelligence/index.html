Lead yourself.
Then lead others.

The "soft" skill with hard numbers.

Go deeper

In practice

The sources behind the game: Goleman's "What Makes a Leader?" (Harvard Business Review) on EI versus IQ; McKinsey's skill-shift research projecting demand for social and emotional skills to grow roughly 26 percent in the U.S. by 2030, and its wage analysis of 10.2 million job postings showing hard skills still paying about double; Deloitte's high-performing-teams research, where members ranked emotional and social intelligence the top success factor; and SHRM's finding that respectful treatment at work outranks even pay as a satisfaction driver. Section 02 · The Map

Four domains, one sequence.

Goleman's applied model runs in order: you can't manage what you haven't noticed, and you can't manage a relationship before you can read a room. Underneath it sits the Mayer-Salovey ability model, the peer-reviewed foundation: perceiving, using, understanding, and managing emotion.

1 · Self-awareness

Notice it

Knowing what you're feeling as it happens, and what it does to your judgment. The foundation everything else stands on.

2 · Self-management

Steer it

Regulating the reaction between stimulus and response: the pause, the label, the chosen reply instead of the reflex.

3 · Social awareness

Read the room

Empathy as a skill: picking up what others feel from tone, face, and what isn't said. DDI found empathetic leaders perform 40 percent higher at coaching and engaging.

4 · Relationship management

Move together

Where the first three pay off: influence, feedback, conflict, and trust, built conversation by conversation.

Prefer a new tab? Open on YouTube.

Why this video: the researcher himself, in five minutes, including why EI keeps growing when IQ has plateaued.

Play · Name the domain

Why this matters: the model only helps if you can see it in real moments. Five workplace scenes; name the domain at work (or missing).

Go deeper

In practice

Why two models? Mayer, Salovey, and Caruso built the ability model that made EI measurable science: perceiving emotion, using it to aid thinking, understanding how emotions evolve, and managing them. Goleman translated the science into the leadership language organizations use. Knowing both keeps you honest: EI is a testable ability, not a horoscope, and the four domains are its workplace application. If a vendor sells you an "EQ assessment," ask which model it's built on; the good ones can answer.

On your own · 3 min

Why this matters: ranking your own domains, honestly, tells you which half of today matters most for you.

  1. Rank the four domains for yourself, strongest to weakest. Gut call, ten seconds each.
  2. Write one recent moment your weakest domain cost you something.
  3. Keep the note; the capstone will ask you to aim a practice at exactly that spot.

Section 03 · Self-Awareness

95 percent think they're self-aware.
10 to 15 percent are.

That gap is organizational psychologist Tasha Eurich's research (Eurich, 2018), and it's why self-awareness can't be self-certified. The Johari Window maps the problem: what's known to you and others (Open), known only to you (Hidden), known only to others (your Blind spot), and known to no one yet. Feedback is the only tool that shrinks the blind quadrant.

Play · Map your window, privately

Why this matters: naming one suspected blind spot, in writing, is the first honest act of self-awareness most people have done all year. Nothing you type is saved or sent anywhere.

Fill in all three

Quick check: In the Johari Window, what actually shrinks your Blind quadrant?

Go deeper

In practice

Eurich's research adds a twist worth knowing: there are two self-awareness types, internal (knowing yourself) and external (knowing how others experience you), and they don't correlate. Power makes it worse; senior people get less honest feedback every year. Her practical fix is one question asked of people who see you weekly: "What's one thing I do that gets in my own way?" Her short talk, "Increase your self-awareness with one simple fix" (TEDx, 17 min), is this week's watch.

As a group · 5 min

Why this matters: testing one Open-quadrant item with a real person is a low-stakes rehearsal for the blind-spot question that actually matters.

  1. Pairs, trust permitting: share ONE item from your Open quadrant ("I think I'm known for X").
  2. Partner confirms or gently adjusts: "that matches" or "here's what I'd add."
  3. Optional brave move: ask your partner the Eurich question: "what's one thing people like me tend to miss about ourselves?"

Flying solo? Text a trusted colleague the Eurich question this week. The answer goes in your Blind quadrant, with gratitude.

Section 04 · Self-Management

Name it to tame it.

UCLA neuroscientists scanned brains while people labeled emotions and found something useful: putting a feeling into words reduces activity in the amygdala, the brain's alarm system, and engages the prefrontal cortex that does deliberate thinking. Labeling doesn't switch the feeling off; it turns the volume down enough to choose your response. And precision matters: "frustrated because the decision keeps moving" regulates better than "stressed."

Play · Upgrade the label

Why this matters: vague words hide the information an emotion carries. Five moments described in fog; pick the precise label, because the precise one tells you what to do next.

The 90-second version

Feel the spike → name it, precisely and silently ("I'm feeling defensive because that landed as criticism") → one breath → choose the response. The label is doing real neurological work in that pause; the science calls it affect labeling, your grandmother called it counting to ten, and both were right.

Go deeper

In practice

The study is Lieberman and colleagues, "Putting Feelings Into Words" (Psychological Science, 2007): labeling an emotion reduced amygdala response while engaging the prefrontal cortex, and participants weren't even trying to regulate; the naming alone did it. Two honest caveats: the effect turns intensity down, not off, and it works best with a rich emotional vocabulary, which is why psychologists prize emotional granularity: the difference between "mad" and "disappointed, and a little embarrassed I didn't push back" is the difference between a reflex and a plan.

On your own · 3 min

Why this matters: the label you draft now, calm, is the one your brain reaches for later, spiked.

  1. Recall your last flash of workplace emotion: the email, the meeting, the interruption.
  2. Label it three levels deep: the vague word, the precise word, and the "because" clause.
  3. Notice what the precise version suggests doing that the vague one didn't. Write that move down.

Section 05 · Listening

Listen to understand,
not to reply.

Active listening has three trainable moves: paraphrase what you heard, ask clarifying questions, and withhold judgment (and advice) until asked. Research on workplace listening keeps finding the same thing: the speaker can tell which mode you're in, faster than you think.

Play · Rate the reply

Why this matters: the difference between listening and waiting to talk is easiest to hear in someone else's replies first. Five moments; pick the reply that's actually listening.

The rubric, for the triad drill

1. Attention: eyes and body on the speaker, no glances at devices. 2. Paraphrase accuracy: "so what I'm hearing is…" lands close enough that the speaker says "yes, that." 3. Judgment withheld: no advice, no "that happened to me too," no fixing. Score each 1 to 3; the Observer holds the card.

Go deeper

In practice

Why "no advice" is the hard rule: the moment you offer a fix, the speaker stops exploring and starts evaluating your fix, and the conversation becomes about you. The silence right after someone finishes is where the real material surfaces; count three seconds before the paraphrase. And notice the connection to coaching: if your team took Coaching for Performance, this is the same muscle GROW runs on.

As a group · 9 min

Why this matters: being scored on listening, once, by a human holding a rubric, changes how you hear yourself in every conversation after.

  1. Triads: Speaker, Listener, Observer. Speaker takes a real, low-stakes work frustration.
  2. Three minutes: Listener may only paraphrase, clarify, and reflect emotion. No advice, no me-too, no interrupting.
  3. Observer scores the rubric (1 to 3 each) and gives 30 seconds of feedback.
  4. Rotate until everyone has sat in all three seats. The Observer seat teaches the most; notice that.

Flying solo? In your next real conversation, run the rubric on yourself: one paraphrase, one clarifying question, zero advice. Score yourself honestly after.

Section 06 · The Reframe

Accusation in.
Connection out.

Marshall Rosenberg's Nonviolent Communication rebuilds a charged statement in four parts: an Observation a camera would agree with, the Feeling it raises in you, the Need underneath, and a specific Request. Same honesty, none of the ammunition.

Practice · The Reframe Lab

Why this matters: feeling the reply change as each part sharpens is how OFNR moves from acronym to instinct. The charged version: "You never tell me what's going on with this project." Rebuild it, one part at a time, and see how your colleague responds. Moves open one at a time, and your first move shapes what comes next.

Choose all four parts
Go deeper

In practice

The two failure points, in Rosenberg's own teaching: observations that are secretly judgments ("you're always late" is a verdict; "the last two meetings started without you" is a camera), and requests that are secretly demands (a request survives hearing "no"; a demand punishes it). Faux feelings are the sneaky third: "I feel ignored" is a diagnosis of them, "I feel anxious" is a report about you. The four parts live at the Center for Nonviolent Communication if you want the source.

As a group · 6 min

Why this matters: converting fresh accusations, out loud, under light time pressure, is the rep that makes the structure automatic.

  1. Pairs. Convert these two, splitting the work: "You're always late to my meetings." and "You just don't listen to my ideas."
  2. Each conversion needs all four parts: camera-true observation, owned feeling, the need, a specific request.
  3. Read the best one aloud to the room. Listen for the difference in your own chest when you hear it.

Flying solo? Convert the accusation YOU'RE most tempted to make this week. Keep it; the capstone may want it.

Section 07 · Psychological Safety

Safe enough to say the hard thing.

When Google studied its own teams to find what made the best ones best (Project Aristotle), the strongest predictor wasn't talent density or seniority; it was psychological safety. Amy Edmondson, who named the concept, is blunt about what it isn't: it is not being nice. It's a climate where candid feedback can be given and heard, because the point is learning.

Prefer a new tab? Open on YouTube.

Why this video: the researcher who defined the field, on why safety and standards rise together. Play the first five minutes in class.

Play · Judge the opener

Why this matters: the scenario is real: a peer's approach to a shared project isn't working, and the deadline is close. Five opening lines; call each one candor with care, harshness, or avoidance dressed as kindness.

Go deeper

In practice

Edmondson's formula for candor with care has three moves you can hear in every good opener: name the shared goal ("we both want this launch to land"), state the concern as an observation about the WORK, not the person, and invite their view as a genuine question. Safety plus standards is the target quadrant; safety without standards is a book club, standards without safety is fear, and neither ships great work. If your team took Navigating Difficult Conversations, you'll recognize the DNA; this is the climate those tools work inside.

As a group · 5 min

Why this matters: drafting the line as a group, then hearing it read aloud, shows exactly where candor tips into harshness or dissolves into niceness.

  1. Small groups: draft ONE opening line for the scenario above: direct about the problem, unmistakably on the same team.
  2. Groups read their lines aloud. After each, the room calls it: candid AND caring, or missing one?
  3. Rewrite the room's harshest line and its softest line, live, until both land in the target quadrant.

Flying solo? Draft your line for a real shared-work concern, then test it: does it name the goal, the observable issue, and end in a question?
