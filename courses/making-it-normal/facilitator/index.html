05 · When things go sideways

If it breaks, you have a move

If Projector or the site fails mid-session: Teach from the whiteboard: the four words are already written there, and the 1:1 question, the permission talk outline, and the traffic light all draw in seconds. The narration drill and the permission-line swap never needed a screen; the capstone moves to the printed plan. The lab becomes a group walk-through: read each slot's three options aloud and have the room vote.
If Learner devices can't join (wifi or filters): Run facilitator-only: the trainers play well on the projector with the room voting A/B/C by hands; the permission talk builder and capstone move to paper. You lose typing, not learning.
If You're 10+ minutes behind by the Adoption Lab (05): Declare the Core path silently: pair drills become solo passes, debriefs shrink to one voice, and the closing tightens to the fist-to-five plus commitment round. Never cut the lab, the builder, or the capstone; they carry the session's practice objectives.
If Someone names a colleague or starts prosecuting a specific person's secret AI use: Protect the norm warmly: 'Hold that. Roles and rituals, never names. Secret use is a symptom of the permission gap, so the fix is the talk, never the prosecution.' If it keeps happening, a real trust problem is in the room; name the no-blame principle and move the person to a private follow-up.
If The room is skeptical: 'AI is overhyped, my team doesn't need this': Don't argue futures; play Guess the Number and let the adoption data speak. The frame that lands: whatever you think of the hype, about 40 percent of employees already use AI and most have never heard a rule from anyone. Silence is the riskiest policy on the menu, so the permission talk is worth giving even by a skeptic. Then point at the lab's skeptic slot: the course treats doubt as a resource, and so should the room.
If The room is the opposite: enthusiasts who want to mandate AI use tomorrow: Aim the enthusiasm at behavior: 'Great, you go first: what will you narrate at your next team meeting?' Then hold the line on mandates; the lab's skeptic slot and the Fix the Ritual trainer do the sobering for you. Mandates produce compliance theater, and the enthusiast-only failure mode in the trainer is written for exactly them.
If A manager says their own leadership has given them no rules to pass on: Honor it; this is the permission gap one level up, and it is common. The move: within the traffic light, which is already VU-wide, a manager can still name what they encourage on their own team, model their own use, and route gray-zone cases upward. Explicitly encourage them to ask their leader the same question this course asks them: what do you want us to do with AI? That question travels well.
If Someone raises a real data-privacy concern mid-flight: Stop and honor it; this is the one interruption that outranks the agenda. Restate the traffic light (green public, yellow internal in approved VU tools only, red private information about people, never), confirm their case against it, and if it's genuinely ambiguous, park it with a promise to route it to the right owner rather than improvising policy from the front of the room.

06 · Tough questions, ready answers

Say each answer out loud once before you teach

Honestly, I use AI all the time and have never told my team. Is this session telling me I've been doing it wrong?

It's telling you you've been doing it silently, which is different from wrong, and it is the single most common pattern in the data. The cost of the silence is real: your team gets no example to copy, no signal about what's allowed, and no evidence that imperfect use is safe. The good news is that your fix is the cheapest one in the room: you already have months of material to narrate. Pick one use, show the prompt and the fix at your next team meeting, and you've converted a private habit into leadership.

Isn't modeling my misses going to make me look incompetent in front of my team?

It reads the opposite way in practice. A manager who says 'the draft got this wrong and here's how I caught it' is demonstrating mastery of the whole loop: use, standard, verification. What actually damages credibility is the other path: modeling flawless speed, having the team copy it without the checks, and then owning the error that ships. The miss you narrate is cheap insurance against the miss nobody admits to. And the deeper effect is cultural: your admitted miss is what makes everyone else's miss safe to log.

What if I give explicit permission and someone ships an AI error to a client or a student?

The permission talk is what makes that LESS likely, because every encouraged use comes with its check attached, and the miss log means errors surface early instead of hiding. Compare the alternative: with no stated rules, people use AI anyway, unchecked and unadmitted, and the first error you hear about is the one that already reached someone. If an error does ship, run the no-blame play: fix it, log it, name what check would have caught it, and resist the urge to revoke permission; one public revocation restarts the secret-use era for everyone.

My team is already stretched. Where does an hour of learning time per person come from?

From the same place the AI wins come from: the tasks that get faster. The honest framing is a trade: the hour is the investment that makes the drafting, summarizing, and formatting wins real, and Gallup's data says integrated, supported use is where the frequent-use gains live. Start smaller if you must: thirty minutes, or one shared hour as a team. What you cannot do is call learning a priority and let the calendar say otherwise, because the team believes the calendar.

Shouldn't this come from the top? I'm one manager; I can't set AI strategy.

Strategy comes from the top; adoption happens on teams, and the research is specific about that: employees' comfort follows a communicated plan, and their usage follows their manager's visible behavior. You don't need to write policy; the traffic light already exists. What only you can do is model use, name what you encourage inside those boundaries, and install the rituals in your own meetings. And a manager who arrives at leadership's door with ninety days of adoption stories and a miss log is influencing strategy, from below, with evidence.

What do I do if my skeptic refuses even the pairing task?

First, check what the refusal is made of: most skeptic positions are one part principle and three parts nobody-asked-my-opinion. The pairing move works because it does ask: their standards become the verification. If it's still a no, don't force it; assign them the verifier role on someone else's AI-drafted work instead, which honors the doubt while keeping them inside the norm. A skeptic you mandate becomes a martyr; a skeptic you make the quality bar becomes your best control. And accept that one holdout in six, doing good work, is not a crisis.

How is the miss log different from a record my team could be punished with later?

By design choices you make out loud: no names on the misses, full credit on the catches, and the log reviewed only as a team learning artifact, never in performance conversations. Say that explicitly when you launch it, and then keep the promise, because the log lives exactly as long as your restraint does. If your organization's climate genuinely can't support a written log, run the verbal version: a standing 'what did AI get wrong this week' moment in the team meeting. The ritual matters more than the artifact.

Section 01 · The Modeling Effect

Your team adopts what it sees.

Most organizations pulled the obvious levers first: buy licenses, send a mandate, run an all-hands. Usage barely moved. Gallup's workplace research points somewhere less comfortable: the strongest predictors of a team actually using AI are a manager who visibly uses it and AI wired into real workflows. The tool budget matters less than what your team watches you do on a Tuesday. Before the method, the evidence; most rooms guess these numbers wrong.

What barely moves usage

Mandates and licenses

Tool access is nearly universal and adoption still stalls. A memo tells people AI exists; it gives nobody a reason, a rulebook, or an example to copy.

What moves it

A manager who shows their work

Opening the laptop and walking the team through a real prompt, a real draft, and a real fix. Visible use plus real-workflow integration outpredicts every other lever.

The permission gap

Silence reads two ways

Most employees have never been told what AI use is encouraged. Half your team reads that silence as prohibition; the other half improvises rules in the dark.

Go deeper

In practice

The numbers in the game come from Gallup's workplace AI research: about 40 percent of U.S. employees now use AI at work, roughly double the share of two years earlier, and 19 percent use it frequently. Only 22 percent say their organization has communicated a clear plan or strategy for it, and employees whose leadership has communicated one are 4.7 times as likely to feel comfortable using AI at work. Read those together and the management story writes itself: usage is spreading person by person, in silence, and comfort follows leadership behavior. The permission gap is not an HR abstraction. It is the specific people on your team who would use AI tomorrow if they were sure you wanted them to.

As a group · 4 min

Why this matters: the permission gap turns personal the moment you look for it on your own team.

  1. In pairs: has anyone on your team ever heard you say, in plain words, what AI use you encourage? Yes or no, honestly.
  2. Now the other side: could anyone on your team describe one way YOU use AI? If they couldn't, your use is invisible.
  3. Hands to the room on both questions. Count out loud. The distance between those counts and the room's headcount is the permission gap, sitting right here.

Flying solo? Answer both questions for yourself in writing: has your team heard your encouraged list, and could they describe one way you use AI? Every no you write down is a move this session will hand you.

Section 02 · Show

Model it out loud, misses included.

The most contagious behavior a manager has is narrated use: "I drafted this with AI, here is the prompt, and here is what I fixed before it went out." Ninety seconds of that teaches more than a semester of encouragement, because it shows the tool, the standard, and the check in one pass. And the check is the point. If your team only ever sees you being fast, they will copy the speed and skip the verification, and the first shipped error will get blamed on the tool.

1 · Narrate the prompt

Show what you asked

The actual prompt, on the screen, with a sentence on why you wrote it that way. Prompts are invisible craft until somebody senior shows theirs.

2 · Narrate the fix

Show what you checked

The draft next to what you shipped, and the two things you corrected against the source. This is the behavior that matters most, so it's the one to model hardest.

3 · Narrate the miss

Show what it got wrong

Say the miss out loud and treat the catch as the win. A leader who admits AI misses makes imperfect use safe in public, which is where learning happens.

Play · Judge the model

Why this matters: your team can't tell you what your modeling looks like from the outside, but you can train your own ear. Five manager behaviors; call each one.

Go deeper

In practice

The narration is four sentences and you can script it tonight: here is the task, here is the prompt I used, here is what the draft got right and what I fixed, and here is why I checked it against the source instead of trusting how it read. Pick a task that's real and slightly imperfect; a flawless demo models nothing because nobody's Tuesday is flawless. Two traps to avoid: performing expertise, where the demo becomes a lecture on prompt wizardry and AI starts to look like a specialist skill, and performing speed, where the takeaway becomes "this is fast" instead of "this is checked". You are aiming for ordinary. The day AI use on your team is boring, you've won.

As a group · 5 min

Why this matters: narrating to one colleague today is the rehearsal for narrating to your team this week.

  1. Pairs. Each person narrates one real AI use from their last two weeks: the task, the prompt, the draft, the fix. No hypotheticals.

Section 03 · Say

Nobody follows rules they've never heard.

Silence about AI is still a policy; it's just one you didn't choose. Half your team hears it as prohibition and quietly abstains. The other half hears it as a free-for-all and improvises rules you'd never approve. The fix is one explicit conversation, repeated until it's boring: what's encouraged, what's never OK, and who to ask when it's unclear. You'll draft yours in a minute.

The permission talk, three parts

1. The encouraged list: two or three uses you actively want, named specifically, with the check that goes with each. 2. The never list: the hard limits, stated as plainly as the encouragements. 3. The gray zone: who to ask when a case fits neither list, plus the promise that asking is applauded and never punished. Then say all three again next month, because a norm heard once is a rumor.

The never list starts here

Whatever else goes on your lists, the traffic light from the AI courses is the floor, and it outranks every technique in this course:

GreenPublic or generic information. Go.
YellowInternal work. Approved VU tools only (ChatGPT EDU, Amplify, Copilot).
RedPrivate information about people. Never.

If your team's work touches information about people, red is the first line of your never list, said out loud, in your voice, more than once.

Section 04 · Set

Norms live in rituals, not memos.

A norm is a behavior that happens on a schedule, which means it needs a home on the calendar you already have. The good news: your 1:1s and team meetings are already there, and installing AI into them costs minutes. One precondition sits under everything on this slide: psychological safety. Nobody shares a miss in a team that punishes misses, and every ritual below runs on people telling the truth about what went wrong.

1 · The 1:1 question

Two minutes, every week

"What did you try with AI this week, and what did it get wrong?" The second half is the clever part: it assumes misses exist and makes reporting them normal.

2 · The share-one round

One use, out loud

Two minutes of an existing team meeting: one person shares one real use, prompt and fix included. Rotate it so the quiet members present too, and misses count as shares.

3 · The miss log

Catches, without blame

A shared list of what AI got wrong and who caught it. No names on the miss, full credit on the catch. The log turns verification into a visible team sport.

4 · The pairing move

Enthusiast meets skeptic

Put your keenest user and your sharpest doubter on one real task together. The skeptic's standards become verification; the enthusiast's speed picks up a check.

Section 06 · Sustain

Month three is the real test.

Every adoption curve has the same dip. Novelty carries months one and two; then workload returns, the share-one round goes quiet, and usage slides back toward the two people who never needed encouragement. The dip is normal. Whether it becomes the end of the story depends on three things you control: protected time, what you celebrate, and how you measure.

Protect the time

An hour a week is a budget

Learning time treated as a perk dies at the first deadline. Treat it as a budget you defend: on the calendar, protected like a client meeting, spent on real tasks.

Celebrate the catch

Caught errors are wins

When someone catches an AI miss before it ships, that's the system working. Praise it publicly and the whole team relearns what the win actually is: the check.

Stories, never surveillance

Report adoption as stories

Uses shared, misses caught, hours returned to better work. Never leaderboard individuals; a usage ranking converts a learning norm into surveillance in one week.

Facilitator scriptFull 4 min · Core 1

Convert plans into spoken commitments, capture the Level 1 reaction check, and point at the follow-up chain.

Say

“The whole session in one breath: the data says your team adopts what it sees you do. So show your use, misses included. Say the rules out loud, twice. Set the norms into meetings you already run. And sustain it past month three with a defended hour, a celebrated catch, and a story instead of a scoreboard. You have a plan. Before you go: your first move, and its day. Say it out loud; spoken commitments stick.”

Do
  1. Read the featured card: the one next step is narrating one real AI use at the next team meeting, four sentences, misses included.
  2. Run the fist-to-five (Level 1): 'On a fist to five, how confident are you that you can make AI use normal on your team?' Scan and note the number; the 30-day re-poll asks it again.
  3. Run the commitment round, popcorn style: first move and its day, out loud or in chat.
  4. Point at the cheat sheet and adoption plan buttons; the cheat sheet is built to sit next to their 1:1 notes.
  5. Tell them the 7-day pulse is coming, then land the last line and stop on time.
Ask

“Around the room, one line each: your first move, and its day.”

Expect:

  • 'Narrating my board-update draft, Thursday's team meeting'
  • 'The permission talk, Monday'
  • 'The 1:1 question, starting tomorrow morning'

Respond: Thank each one, no commentary. If someone commits to finding out what their team already uses first, warmly: 'Good instinct. Ask what they'd hate to give back; that question surfaces the secret uses without making anyone confess.'

Debrief

The session's success metric isn't in this room; it's the 7-day pulse: how many first moves actually happened, and what the teams did in the moment.

Validate: Fist-to-five mostly 3+ (Level 1); a majority state a first move and a day out loud or in chat. Count both; with the pulse and the 30-day re-poll, that's your evidence chain.

Watch for: Plans that quietly skipped the date. The commitment round catches them: no day, no commitment; help them pick one on the spot.

Transition

“Go make the first move. And when someone on your team exhales because it turns out they were allowed all along, that exhale is the sound of adoption starting.”

60-min core: Core: fist-to-five plus a fast commitment round; the ending survives every cut.
