05 · When things go sideways

If it breaks, you have a move

If Projector or the site fails mid-session: Teach from the printed cheat sheet: the Big Idea formula, both structures, the delivery rules, and the bridge all work on a whiteboard. The live round and rapid fire never needed a screen; the capstone moves to the printed worksheet.
If Learner devices can't join (wifi/filters): Run facilitator-only: the trainers play well on the projector with the room voting A/B/C by hands; Big Idea drafting and the capstone move to paper (the worksheet covers both). You lose typing, not learning.
If You're 10+ minutes behind by the delivery section (06): Declare the Core path silently: cut the remaining video (assign it), run the live round at 60 seconds flat per speaker, and compress the rapid fire to two volunteers. Never cut the live round entirely or the capstone — a presentation course where nobody presented is a contradiction the room will remember.
If Participants didn't do the pre-work and have no real presentation to draft: Give the universal fallback prompt: 'pitch your leadership on one change that would make your team's work week better.' Everyone has one. It drafts, storyboards, and survives the rapid fire exactly like a real topic — and often becomes one.
If Someone freezes or visibly panics in the live round: Protect them without excusing them: 'Take a breath — start with just the Big Idea sentence, that's the whole rep.' If they can't, let them pass and re-offer at the end; most take the second chance when the pressure of the queue is gone. Never force it, never make it a moment. AMA's principle applies to facilitation too: no one cares about the stumble but them.
If A senior leader in the room dominates feedback or turns notes into speeches: Re-anchor the rule warmly: 'One specific note per speaker, framework-anchored — that discipline is half the exercise.' Deputize them as the timer-keeper or the rapid-fire executive; the role channels the energy and they're usually excellent at it.

06 · Tough questions, ready answers

Say each answer out loud once before you teach

My leadership only wants bullet points and dense slides. Won't 'one idea per slide' look unprepared?

Separate the two artifacts: the PRESENTATION is what you say with minimal slides behind it; the DOCUMENT is the dense leave-behind they can study. Gallo's rule applies to the first, not the second. Minto's Pyramid was literally built for the document. Teams that split deck-as-teleprompter from deck-as-reference find executives read the document and remember the talk — right now yours are getting neither.

Isn't leading with the answer risky? What if they reject it before I've made my case?

The suspense strategy carries the bigger risk: an executive interrupting minute six with 'what are you asking for?' — and now you're improvising your ask under pressure. Leading with the answer doesn't skip the case; it frames every supporting point as evidence for a decision the room can already see. If the answer is genuinely explosive, SCQA still helps: state the Question crisply and signal the answer is coming in one minute, not twenty.

The Sparkline feels theatrical for a budget review. Is this TED stuff really for internal meetings?

Match the tool to the job — that's Section 05's whole point. A routine budget review wants SCQA, maybe just the Answer and support. But the moment a presentation asks people to CHANGE — new process, reorg, strategy shift — you're in persuasion territory, and the What Is/What Could Be gap is the mechanism that moves people. The skill isn't using the Sparkline everywhere; it's noticing which meetings are secretly change talks.

I hate the sound of my own opening line. How do I know if it's good?

Test it on the three failure modes from today: is it an apology? A warm-up ('so, um, thanks for having me, a bit of background…')? A topic announcement ('today I'll talk about Q3')? If it's none of those and it delivers either stakes or the answer inside the first sentence, it's good enough to rehearse — and rehearsal, not redrafting, is what makes it feel natural. The tenth time out loud sounds nothing like the first.

What about nerves? You said fear isn't the barrier, but my hands still shake.

The Chapman data says fear isn't the TOP barrier for most people — not that nerves aren't real. Two honest answers: structure is itself the best anxiolytic (most speaking panic is really 'I don't know what comes next,' and a storyboard fixes that), and arousal reframing works — 'excited AND nervous' outperforms 'calm down' in performance research. Rehearse out loud, know your first sentence cold, and let the hands shake; the room can't see them from four feet away.

Can AI just build my presentation now?

AI drafts well and thinks poorly on your behalf. It can propose Sparkline beats or an SCQA skeleton in seconds — genuinely useful — but the Big Idea is a point of view, and outsourcing your point of view is how you end up presenting a talk you don't believe in front of people who can tell. Use it the way we used the lab today: generate options, then YOU choose and sharpen. If your team took Start Smarter or Working Smarter, this is the human-checkpoint pattern applied to your own deck.

Section 01 · The Case

The fear is a myth.
The stakes are not.

Every number in this game comes from published research, and most rooms guess them wrong. The pattern they add up to: what's holding presenters back isn't nerve, and what's at stake in presenting well is bigger than most people think.

Quick check: If fear isn't what's actually holding most presenters back, what did the research point to instead?

Go deeper

In practice

The sources: the Chapman University Survey of American Fears (2024 wave: public speaking 59th of 85, at 29 percent, a few points below sharks; the myth has outlived the data by decades). Coqual's Executive Presence research, led by Sylvia Ann Hewlett, surveying nearly 4,000 professionals including 268 senior executives. And Deloitte's CFO research, where "good communication skills, the ability to explain results in clear, simple terms" topped the list of qualities for the next CFO at 39 percent, ahead of every technical competency. Presenting well is not a stage skill; it's how the people who decide careers see your thinking.

  3. Collect a few out loud. Notice the answers are almost never "the slides were thorough." They're structure and presence, every time.

Flying solo? Write the one thing that made it land. Check it against the frameworks coming; it will map to one of them.

Section 02 · Presence

Three pillars, weighed by the people who promote you.

Hewlett's team asked 268 senior executives what signals leadership readiness. Gravitas, confidence, decisiveness, grace under fire, took 67 percent of the vote. Communication took 28, and it's the pillar that makes gravitas visible. Appearance took 5, but filters the other two. Those weights are from Hewlett's 2012 survey; her 2024 update in Harvard Business Review keeps confidence and decisiveness at the core of gravitas but adds inclusiveness and respect for others, and moves a listen-to-learn orientation and authenticity up the communication list (Hewlett, HBR, 2024). A presentation is the one moment all three pillars are on display at once.

Gravitas · 67%

How you hold pressure

Confidence, decisiveness, grace under fire. Going "six questions deep" in your domain without flinching. The core signal of readiness.

Communication · 28%

How the room sees it

Great speaking, commanding a room, reading an audience. Gravitas that can't be communicated might as well not exist.

Appearance · 5%

The filter

The smallest pillar, but a real filter: polish failures distract the room from the two pillars that matter. Manage it so it disappears.

Play · Spot the pillar

Why this matters: the model only helps if you can see it in real moments, especially the moments where a pillar is missing. Five presentation scenes; name the pillar at work or at fault.

Go deeper

In practice

Two nuances worth carrying out of the room. First, the appearance paradox: executives rated appearance least important, yet cited unkempt attire among the most damaging presence errors. Both are true: appearance can't win the room, but it can lose it, so the professional move is making it a non-event. Second, executives put presence at roughly 26 percent of what it takes to get promoted, which means every presentation is quietly a promotion interview. Hewlett's full talk on the research is this week's optional watch (search "Sylvia Ann Hewlett Executive Presence Talks at Google").

On your own · 3 min

Why this matters: naming your own weakest pillar, honestly, tells you which half of today matters most for you.

  1. Rank the three pillars for yourself, strongest to weakest. Gut call, ten seconds each.
  2. Write one recent presentation moment your weakest pillar cost you something.
  3. Keep the note; the capstone will aim the rebuild at exactly that spot.

Section 03 · The Big Idea

One sentence, or it isn't ready.

Nancy Duarte studied the structure of history's great talks and found they all rest on one thing: a single Big Idea, your point of view plus what's at stake, in one sentence. "Our Q3 results" is a topic. "Double down on enterprise, because SMB churn just outpaced our new-logo growth" is a Big Idea. If you can't say it in a sentence, the deck isn't unclear. The thinking is.

Why this matters: the difference is easiest to hear in other people's sentences first. Five openers; call each one.

Practice · Draft your Big Idea, privately

Why this matters: you brought a real upcoming presentation; this is where it starts becoming a talk. Nothing you type is saved or sent anywhere.

Fill in both halves

Prefer a new tab? Open on YouTube.

Why this video: the researcher herself, mapping the same shape under MLK's Dream speech and the great product launches. It sets up the next section.

Go deeper

In practice

Duarte's two tests for a real Big Idea: it must articulate YOUR point of view (not a neutral subject), and it must convey what's at stake (why the audience should care enough to act). The discipline is the compression itself: if the sentence needs an "and also," you have two talks. Her talk page at Duarte, Inc. and the HBR Guide to Persuasive Presentations carry the full method. The Big Idea also becomes your Q&A anchor in Section 07: every tough question gets bridged back to it.

As a group · 4 min

Why this matters: pressure-testing a Big Idea out loud, kindly, is the fastest way to find the fog in it.

  1. Two or three volunteers read their drafted Big Idea to the room.
  2. The room applies the tests: Is it one idea, or three? Could anyone disagree? Are the stakes real?
  3. Rewrite the fuzziest one live, together. Watch it get shorter and sharper at the same time.

Flying solo? Read yours out loud. If it takes two breaths, cut it. Then try it on a colleague cold: "does this make you want the evidence?"

Facilitator scriptFull 12 min · Core 9

Install Duarte's Big Idea discipline (point of view + stakes, one sentence) and get every learner a drafted Big Idea for their real presentation — the raw material for everything after.

Say

“Nancy Duarte studied the greatest talks ever given looking for their common architecture, and the foundation is always the same: one Big Idea. Not a topic — a point of view, plus what's at stake, in one sentence. 'Our Q3 results' commits to nothing; nobody can disagree with it, so nobody can care about it. 'We should double down on enterprise, because SMB churn just outpaced our new-logo growth' — that's a stance with a cost attached, and now the whole deck has a job: prove it. Here's the uncomfortable truth in the formula: if you can't compress your presentation into one sentence, the deck isn't unclear. The thinking is. Let's find out.”

Do
  1. Teach the formula with the contrast pair: 'Our Q3 results' is a topic; 'double down on enterprise because SMB churn outpaced new-logo growth' is a Big Idea.
  2. Send them to Topic or Big Idea: five sentences, call each one. The two-ideas-fused item teaches the most.
  3. Run the private drafter: point of view plus stakes, assembled on their own screens. Circulate and pressure-test two or three aloud: 'Is that one idea, or three?'
  4. Play the Duarte TEDxEast clip (8–10 minutes of 18) on the Full path — she applies the structure to MLK and Jobs, which sets up the next section.
  5. Run the group pressure-test from Go deeper: volunteers read theirs, the room applies the tests, rewrite the fuzziest one live.
Ask

“Why is it so hard to compress a presentation into one sentence — and what does it tell you when you can't?”

Expect:

  • Because we start in the slides instead of the idea
  • Because it's really three presentations wearing one deck
  • Because committing to a stance feels risky; a topic is safe

Respond: Anchor the third one when it comes: 'Exactly — a topic can't be wrong, which is why it can't be interesting. The Big Idea takes the risk a presentation exists to take. If the stance is wrong, better to find out in one sentence than in slide forty.'

Debrief

One sentence, one idea, stakes attached. That sentence is your Q&A anchor, your opening line's raw material, and the test every slide must pass: does it serve the Big Idea?

Validate: 4+ of 5 on the trainer; every learner has a drafted Big Idea sentence on their screen or paper — this is the section's non-negotiable output.

Watch for: Drafts that are secretly topics ('an update on the program') or two ideas fused with 'and also.' The kind pressure-test: 'What do you want them to DO? What happens if they don't?' — the answers usually contain the real Big Idea.

Transition

“Now the idea needs a shape. Duarte found one in the greatest speeches in history — and it isn't a straight line.”

60-min core: Core: contrast pair, trainer fast, drafter at full length (protect it), cut the video (assign as the week's watch), pressure-test one draft instead of three.

Section 04 · Structure I

What is. What could be.
Again and again.

Duarte's Presentation Sparkline is the shape she found under the great persuasive talks: alternate between today's reality (What Is) and the future you're proposing (What Could Be), raising the stakes with each pass, and end on New Bliss, the picture of the world once the room says yes. The alternation is the engine; the gap between the two is what makes an audience want to move.

Play · Tag the beat

Why this matters: once you can tag the beats in someone else's talk, you can storyboard your own. Five beats from one onboarding pitch, in order; tag each.

The storyboard template

What Is (today's pain, concrete) → What Could Be (the vision) → What Is (the honest obstacle) → What Could Be (the bigger vision) → New Bliss (the world after they say yes). Storyboard these five beats on sticky notes BEFORE touching slides; the deck is the last step, not the first.

Go deeper

In practice

Duarte traces the shape through MLK's "I Have a Dream" (the alternation between the broken promise of America as it is and the dream of what it could be) and Steve Jobs' iPhone launch (the clunky phones of 2007 against the device in his pocket). The craft points: make the What Is beats concrete enough to sting, return to reality after each vision (that return is what builds credibility), and never end on an obstacle. New Bliss is the last thing they hear.

As a group · 6 min

Why this matters: storyboarding beats before slides is the single biggest process change most presenters take from this session.

  1. Small groups of 3 to 4: pick one member's Big Idea from the last section.
  2. Storyboard 3 to 4 beats on sticky notes or a shared doc: What Is → What Could Be → What Is → New Bliss.
  3. Read the beats aloud as one paragraph. Does the gap between reality and vision widen each pass? If not, sharpen the What Is.

Flying solo? Storyboard your own four beats on paper now. The capstone will ask which structure you chose; this is your audition for the Sparkline.

Facilitator scriptFull 9 min · Core 6

Install Duarte's Sparkline shape (What Is ↔ What Could Be → New Bliss) as a storyboarding tool, and drill beat-recognition until learners can storyboard their own talk.

Say

“Duarte put the greatest speeches in history under a microscope and found the same shape: they alternate. Here's what is — the world as it stands, with its pain made concrete. Here's what could be. Back to what is — because the audience needs you to acknowledge the obstacle. Then a bigger what could be. And it ends on what she calls New Bliss: the picture of the world after the room says yes. The gap between reality and vision is the engine — every alternation widens it, and the audience crosses it for you. You don't build slides first. You storyboard these beats on sticky notes, and the deck comes last.”

Do
  1. Draw the shape as you teach it — literally sketch the alternating line on the whiteboard: reality, vision, reality, bigger vision, New Bliss.
  2. Name where it comes from: Duarte traced it under MLK's Dream speech (the broken promise against the dream) and Jobs' iPhone launch (2007's clunky phones against the device in his pocket).
  3. Send them to Tag the Beat: five beats from one onboarding pitch, tag each. The return-to-reality beat teaches the most.
  4. Walk the storyboard template on the gold card: beats on sticky notes BEFORE slides.
  5. Run the group storyboard: 3–4 beats for one member's Big Idea, read aloud as a paragraph.
Ask

“Why does RETURNING to 'What Is' after painting the vision make the talk stronger, not weaker?”

Expect:

  • It's honest — the room knows the obstacles exist
  • It builds credibility; pure optimism sounds like sales
  • It creates the tension that makes the resolution land

Respond: All three are right; land the second hardest: 'An audience that hears you name the obstacle trusts your vision twice as much. The Sparkline isn't hype — it's honesty with a shape.'

Debrief

Beats before slides, alternation as the engine, and never end on an obstacle. That's the shape for hearts and minds. But there's a room where suspense is a liability…

Validate: 4+ of 5 on the trainer; each group has a 3–4 beat storyboard read aloud.

Watch for: Groups skipping the return to What Is — straight-line optimism reads as salesmanship. The return to reality after each vision is what builds credibility; say it explicitly when you see it missing.

Transition

“…the executive room. Different audience, opposite structure.”

60-min core: Core: sketch the shape, trainer on devices, storyboard as a 3-minute solo exercise instead of groups. (Leadership-heavy Core rooms may compress this further in favor of full-depth SCQA.)

Section 05 · Structure II

For executives:
lead with the answer.

Barbara Minto built the Pyramid Principle at McKinsey for one audience: senior people with brutal calendars. SCQA runs Situation (facts the room accepts), Complication (what makes now untenable), Question, then the Answer, stated first and supported top-down. The Sparkline builds tension; SCQA respects scarce attention. Choosing between them IS the audience analysis.

Practice · The Exec Briefing Lab

Why this matters: feeling the executive room react as each part sharpens is how SCQA moves from acronym to instinct. The scenario: your support team is drowning and you have four sentences of leadership attention. Build the briefing, one part at a time.

Choose all four parts

Quick check: You have 10 minutes with the executive committee to get a decision. Sparkline or SCQA?

Go deeper

In practice

Two Minto craft points. First, the pyramid: the Answer sits on top, supported by grouped arguments that are MECE, mutually exclusive, collectively exhaustive, so no argument overlaps and nothing is missing. Second, the Situation trap: most presenters over-build it. It's the SHORTEST part, only what the room already agrees is true. And the honest tension with Duarte is worth naming: McKinsey optimizes for scarce attention, Duarte for emotional movement. Senior room and a decision needed: SCQA. A room you need to inspire toward change: Sparkline. Know both; choose deliberately.

As a group · 5 min

Why this matters: converting the same idea between structures shows you what each one is for.

  1. Take the group storyboard from the Sparkline activity and re-cast it as SCQA: what's the Situation, the Complication, the Question, the one-sentence Answer?
  2. Read both versions aloud, back to back.
  3. Debrief: which made the idea feel more urgent, and which would the CFO rather hear? (Those answers usually differ, and that IS the lesson.)

Flying solo? Write the four SCQA sentences for your own presentation. If the Answer can't be one sentence, revisit your Big Idea.

Facilitator scriptFull 10 min · Core 7

Install Minto's SCQA with the answer-first discipline, let learners FEEL the exec room react in the graded lab, and make structure choice an audience-analysis decision.

Say

“Barbara Minto built the Pyramid Principle at McKinsey for one audience: senior people with brutal calendars. The structure is SCQA. Situation — the facts the room already accepts, and it's the shortest part; resist the history lesson. Complication — what makes right now untenable: a deadline, named stakes. Question — the exact question the room should now be asking. And the Answer — stated FIRST, specific, costed, dated. Duarte builds tension; Minto respects scarce attention. Neither is better; they're for different rooms, and choosing between them IS the audience analysis. You're about to brief a leadership room with four sentences of their attention. Build it well and watch what happens.”

Do
  1. Teach the four parts with their traps: Situation (shortest part — only what the room accepts), Complication (deadline + named stakes), Question (crystallize it), Answer (first, specific, costed, dated).
  2. Name the lineage: Barbara Minto built this at McKinsey, and consultants still run on it because executive attention is scarce.
  3. Send them to the Exec Briefing Lab: build the support-team briefing one part at a time, watch the room's reaction change. The weak-Answer option teaches the most.
  4. Run the quick check (ten minutes with the exec committee: SCQA).
  5. Run the conversion activity from Go deeper: re-cast the Sparkline storyboard as SCQA, read both aloud, debrief which the CFO would rather hear.
Ask

“McKinsey's own playbook optimizes for scarce executive attention. What's the risk of building suspense with a leadership audience that just wants the bottom line?”

Expect:

  • They interrupt with 'what's the ask?' and you lose the thread
  • They decide you don't know your own answer
  • They mentally check out and read the deck ahead of you

Respond: All real, and the second is the killer: 'In an executive room, withholding the answer doesn't read as storytelling — it reads as not having one. Lead with it, and every minute after becomes evidence instead of preamble.'

Debrief

Answer first, Situation short, Complication with teeth. And the meta-skill: Sparkline for hearts and minds, SCQA for decisions — chosen deliberately, per room.

Validate: Most learners reach the strong reaction in the lab; the quick check lands; groups can articulate when each structure wins.

Watch for: The over-built Situation — most presenters' instinct is to give history lessons. Minto's rule: the Situation is only what the room already agrees is true, and it's the SHORTEST part. Also watch for hearing SCQA as 'the smart one' — it's the right tool for decisions, not universally superior.

Transition

“The talk is built. Now the part everyone thinks the course is about: saying it out loud.”

60-min core: Core: parts with traps in two minutes, lab at full length (it IS the instruction), quick check stays, cut the conversion activity. (This section gets full depth in leadership-heavy Core rooms.)

Section 06 · Delivery

Be a person, talking to people.

Chris Anderson has coached every TED speaker of the modern era; Carmine Gallo studied hundreds of them; the AMA has trained executive speakers for decades. Their advice converges: be conversational, never read bullet walls, modulate the voice, don't over-apologize, and rehearse out loud. Nothing on the list requires talent. All of it requires reps, and yours start on this slide.

Play · Fix the delivery

Why this matters: you'll hear these exact problems in the live round in a few minutes, and one of them is probably yours. Five moments; pick the fix the research would prescribe.

Prefer a new tab? Open on YouTube.

Why this video: the head of TED on the one thing all great talks share, and why delivery polish can't rescue a talk without it.

The live round, rules

60 to 90 seconds each, strictly timed: your Big Idea sentence plus your opening line, delivered to the group. Feedback is exactly ONE specific, actionable note per speaker, drawn from today's frameworks ("you read it instead of said it," "great pause before the ask," "cut the second sentence"). The goal is reps, not perfection.

Go deeper

In practice

The condensed playbook. Anderson's five keys: frame the story as a journey, plan delivery deliberately (never memorize into deadness), work on stage presence, plan multimedia last, and put the idea first. Gallo's five tips: be concise (18 minutes beats 60), never use bullet points, modulate your voice, give the audience something extra, and rehearse relentlessly. AMA's seven principles add the mindset: perception beats perfection ("no one cares about your mistake but you"), visualize success, and speak to serve the audience, not to perform. Full text: Anderson's "How to Give a Killer Presentation" and Gallo's "What It Takes to Give a Great Presentation," both Harvard Business Review.

As a group · 12 min

Why this matters: one timed rep with one honest note changes more than an hour of theory. This is the session's centerpiece; protect it.

  1. Every participant, in turn: stand (or unmute), deliver your Big Idea and opening line. 60 to 90 seconds, timed visibly.
  2. The group gives exactly one specific note per speaker, framework-anchored, starting with what worked.
  3. Facilitator keeps momentum; no re-dos, no pile-ons. Reps over perfection.

Flying solo? Deliver it to your phone camera, timed. Watch it once. Give yourself the one note; you'll know what it is.

“One skill left, and it's the one that happens after your last slide: the pointed question.”

Section 07 · Q&A

The question is not an attack.

The moment presenters fear most is the pointed question from a senior stakeholder, and it's also the moment gravitas is most visible. The AMA's executive-speaking curriculum trains one move for it: the bridge. Acknowledge the question honestly, answer it directly (or commit to a date you will), then return to your Big Idea. Composed is not the same as defensive, and the room can tell.

Play · Judge the bridge

Why this matters: the drill after this is live rapid-fire; calibrate your ear first. Five pointed questions; pick the composed bridge each time.

Before you present: SHRM's six questions

Who's the audience? What's the goal? What are the messages? What's the content? Where's the feedback? How do I refine it? Six questions, asked before you build, and half the tough questions never get asked because you answered them in the talk.

Go deeper

In practice

The prep that makes the bridge easy: before any leadership presentation, write the three questions you're most afraid of, and draft one-sentence answers. (You already know what they are; the fear is the tell.) The honest deferral is a full member of the toolkit: "I don't want to guess; I'll have it to you by Thursday" reads as gravitas, a fluent guess that collapses in the follow-up reads as the opposite. And if your team took Navigating Difficult Conversations, you'll recognize the DNA: acknowledge before you answer is the same move as paraphrase before you argue.

As a group · 6 min

Why this matters: composure under fire is trainable, and the training is fire.

  1. Facilitator (or a volunteer playing a skeptical executive) fires 2 to 3 pointed questions at each presenter from the live round: "What's this going to cost us?" "Why hasn't this been done already?"
  2. Presenter practices the bridge: acknowledge → answer directly → return to the Big Idea.
  3. The room calls out one thing per exchange: bridge landed, or where it wobbled.

Flying solo? Write your three most-feared questions for your real presentation, and the one-sentence answers. Keep them; the capstone assumes you have them.

Facilitator scriptFull 8 min · Core 5

Say

It's the room taking your idea seriously enough to test it — and every question you bridge is another rep of your Big Idea landing.”

  2. Send them to Judge the Bridge: five pointed questions, pick the composed response. The hostile-framing item teaches the most.
  3. Walk SHRM's six pre-speaking questions on the gold card: half the tough questions never come because you answered them in the talk.
  4. Run the rapid fire: you (or a deputized volunteer executive) fire 2–3 pointed questions at each presenter from the live round; presenter bridges.

Debrief

Acknowledge, answer, return. Composed is not defensive, the deferral-with-a-date is strength, and the three feared questions get drafted before the talk, not survived during it.

Validate: 4+ of 5 on the trainer; each rapid-fire presenter completes at least one full bridge (acknowledge, answer, return).

Watch for: Answers that never return to the Big Idea — the most common wobble. And defensiveness dressed as confidence: 'with respect, that's unfair' is a flag, not a bridge. Model the concession move: 'what's true in that question is…'
