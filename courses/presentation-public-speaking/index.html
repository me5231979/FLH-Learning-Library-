Presentation & Public Speaking · Vanderbilt · Manager Voyage

Build the talk.
Command the room.

Public speaking isn't even in America's top 50 fears anymore, so fear was never the real barrier. What separates presenters a room remembers is structure and presence, and both are learnable. Today you rebuild a real presentation with the frameworks the best talks in the world are built on.

By the end of this session, you will be able to

  1. Name your own executive-presence growth edge, and pick the gravitas or communication move that will change how your next message lands
  2. Distill a real presentation into one resonant sentence with Duarte's Big Idea: point of view plus what's at stake
  3. Structure a talk with Duarte's Sparkline, alternating What Is and What Could Be toward New Bliss
  4. Structure an executive briefing with Minto's Pyramid Principle and SCQA, leading with the answer
  5. Apply the delivery techniques of Anderson, Gallo, and the AMA in a live, timed speaking round
  6. Handle pointed questions with the bridge, and commit to rebuilding one real upcoming presentation, opening line written word for word

Section 01 · The Case

The fear is a myth.
The stakes are not.

Every number in this game comes from published research, and most rooms guess them wrong. The pattern they add up to: what's holding presenters back isn't nerve, and what's at stake in presenting well is bigger than most people think.

Play · Guess the number

Why this matters: a statistic you guessed at, and missed, sticks ten times better than one you were shown. Four findings; call each number before the reveal.

Quick check: If fear isn't what's actually holding most presenters back, what did the research point to instead?

Go deeper

In practice

The sources: the Chapman University Survey of American Fears (2024 wave: public speaking 59th of 85, at 29 percent, a few points below sharks; the myth has outlived the data by decades). Coqual's Executive Presence research, led by Sylvia Ann Hewlett, surveying nearly 4,000 professionals including 268 senior executives. And Deloitte's CFO research, where "good communication skills, the ability to explain results in clear, simple terms" topped the list of qualities for the next CFO at 39 percent, ahead of every technical competency. Presenting well is not a stage skill; it's how the people who decide careers see your thinking.

As a group · 4 min

Why this matters: the research becomes personal when it matches the best presentation you've actually watched.

  1. Think of the best presentation you've ever watched a leader give. In pairs: was it the content or the delivery that stuck with you?
  2. Name ONE specific thing that made it land: an opening line, a moment of composure, a picture you still remember.
  3. Collect a few out loud. Notice the answers are almost never "the slides were thorough." They're structure and presence, every time.

Flying solo? Write the one thing that made it land. Check it against the frameworks coming; it will map to one of them.

The core idea

You can coach out a weak delivery. You can't coach in a weak idea.

Section 02 · Presence

Three pillars, weighed by the people who promote you.

Hewlett's team asked 268 senior executives what signals leadership readiness. Gravitas, confidence, decisiveness, grace under fire, took 67 percent of the vote. Communication took 28, and it's the pillar that makes gravitas visible. Appearance took 5, but filters the other two. Those weights are from Hewlett's 2012 survey; her 2024 update in Harvard Business Review keeps confidence and decisiveness at the core of gravitas but adds inclusiveness and respect for others, and moves a listen-to-learn orientation and authenticity up the communication list (Hewlett, HBR, 2024). A presentation is the one moment all three pillars are on display at once.

Gravitas · 67%

How you hold pressure

Confidence, decisiveness, grace under fire. Going "six questions deep" in your domain without flinching. The core signal of readiness.

Communication · 28%

How the room sees it

Great speaking, commanding a room, reading an audience. Gravitas that can't be communicated might as well not exist.

Appearance · 5%

The filter

The smallest pillar, but a real filter: polish failures distract the room from the two pillars that matter. Manage it so it disappears.

Play · Spot the pillar

Why this matters: the model only helps if you can see it in real moments, especially the moments where a pillar is missing. Five presentation scenes; name the pillar at work or at fault.

Go deeper

In practice

Two nuances worth carrying out of the room. First, the appearance paradox: executives rated appearance least important, yet cited unkempt attire among the most damaging presence errors. Both are true: appearance can't win the room, but it can lose it, so the professional move is making it a non-event. Second, executives put presence at roughly 26 percent of what it takes to get promoted, which means every presentation is quietly a promotion interview. Hewlett's full talk on the research is this week's optional watch (search "Sylvia Ann Hewlett Executive Presence Talks at Google").

On your own · 3 min

Why this matters: naming your own weakest pillar, honestly, tells you which half of today matters most for you.

  1. Rank the three pillars for yourself, strongest to weakest. Gut call, ten seconds each.
  2. Write one recent presentation moment your weakest pillar cost you something.
  3. Keep the note; the capstone will aim the rebuild at exactly that spot.

Section 03 · The Big Idea

One sentence, or it isn't ready.

Nancy Duarte studied the structure of history's great talks and found they all rest on one thing: a single Big Idea, your point of view plus what's at stake, in one sentence. "Our Q3 results" is a topic. "Double down on enterprise, because SMB churn just outpaced our new-logo growth" is a Big Idea. If you can't say it in a sentence, the deck isn't unclear. The thinking is.

Play · Topic or Big Idea?

Why this matters: the difference is easiest to hear in other people's sentences first. Five openers; call each one.

Practice · Draft your Big Idea, privately

Why this matters: you brought a real upcoming presentation; this is where it starts becoming a talk. Nothing you type is saved or sent anywhere.

Prefer a new tab? Open on YouTube.

Why this video: the researcher herself, mapping the same shape under MLK's Dream speech and the great product launches. It sets up the next section.

Go deeper

In practice

Duarte's two tests for a real Big Idea: it must articulate YOUR point of view (not a neutral subject), and it must convey what's at stake (why the audience should care enough to act). The discipline is the compression itself: if the sentence needs an "and also," you have two talks. Her talk page at Duarte, Inc. and the HBR Guide to Persuasive Presentations carry the full method. The Big Idea also becomes your Q&A anchor in Section 07: every tough question gets bridged back to it.

As a group · 4 min

Why this matters: pressure-testing a Big Idea out loud, kindly, is the fastest way to find the fog in it.

  1. Two or three volunteers read their drafted Big Idea to the room.
  2. The room applies the tests: Is it one idea, or three? Could anyone disagree? Are the stakes real?
  3. Rewrite the fuzziest one live, together. Watch it get shorter and sharper at the same time.

Flying solo? Read yours out loud. If it takes two breaths, cut it. Then try it on a colleague cold: "does this make you want the evidence?"

Section 04 · Structure I

What is. What could be.
Again and again.

Duarte's Presentation Sparkline is the shape she found under the great persuasive talks: alternate between today's reality (What Is) and the future you're proposing (What Could Be), raising the stakes with each pass, and end on New Bliss, the picture of the world once the room says yes. The alternation is the engine; the gap between the two is what makes an audience want to move.

Play · Tag the beat

Why this matters: once you can tag the beats in someone else's talk, you can storyboard your own. Five beats from one onboarding pitch, in order; tag each.

The storyboard template

What Is (today's pain, concrete) → What Could Be (the vision) → What Is (the honest obstacle) → What Could Be (the bigger vision) → New Bliss (the world after they say yes). Storyboard these five beats on sticky notes BEFORE touching slides; the deck is the last step, not the first.

Go deeper

In practice

Duarte traces the shape through MLK's "I Have a Dream" (the alternation between the broken promise of America as it is and the dream of what it could be) and Steve Jobs' iPhone launch (the clunky phones of 2007 against the device in his pocket). The craft points: make the What Is beats concrete enough to sting, return to reality after each vision (that return is what builds credibility), and never end on an obstacle. New Bliss is the last thing they hear.

As a group · 6 min

Why this matters: storyboarding beats before slides is the single biggest process change most presenters take from this session.

  1. Small groups of 3 to 4: pick one member's Big Idea from the last section.
  2. Storyboard 3 to 4 beats on sticky notes or a shared doc: What Is → What Could Be → What Is → New Bliss.
  3. Read the beats aloud as one paragraph. Does the gap between reality and vision widen each pass? If not, sharpen the What Is.

Flying solo? Storyboard your own four beats on paper now. The capstone will ask which structure you chose; this is your audition for the Sparkline.

Section 05 · Structure II

For executives:
lead with the answer.

Barbara Minto built the Pyramid Principle at McKinsey for one audience: senior people with brutal calendars. SCQA runs Situation (facts the room accepts), Complication (what makes now untenable), Question, then the Answer, stated first and supported top-down. The Sparkline builds tension; SCQA respects scarce attention. Choosing between them IS the audience analysis.

Practice · The Exec Briefing Lab

Why this matters: feeling the executive room react as each part sharpens is how SCQA moves from acronym to instinct. The scenario: your support team is drowning and you have four sentences of leadership attention. Build the briefing, one part at a time. Moves open one at a time, and your first move shapes what comes next.

Choose all four parts

Quick check: You have 10 minutes with the executive committee to get a decision. Sparkline or SCQA?

Go deeper

In practice

Two Minto craft points. First, the pyramid: the Answer sits on top, supported by grouped arguments that are MECE, mutually exclusive, collectively exhaustive, so no argument overlaps and nothing is missing. Second, the Situation trap: most presenters over-build it. It's the SHORTEST part, only what the room already agrees is true. And the honest tension with Duarte is worth naming: McKinsey optimizes for scarce attention, Duarte for emotional movement. Senior room and a decision needed: SCQA. A room you need to inspire toward change: Sparkline. Know both; choose deliberately.

As a group · 5 min

Why this matters: converting the same idea between structures shows you what each one is for.

  1. Take the group storyboard from the Sparkline activity and re-cast it as SCQA: what's the Situation, the Complication, the Question, the one-sentence Answer?
  2. Read both versions aloud, back to back.
  3. Debrief: which made the idea feel more urgent, and which would the CFO rather hear? (Those answers usually differ, and that IS the lesson.)

Flying solo? Write the four SCQA sentences for your own presentation. If the Answer can't be one sentence, revisit your Big Idea.

Section 06 · Delivery

Be a person, talking to people.

Chris Anderson has coached every TED speaker of the modern era; Carmine Gallo studied hundreds of them; the AMA has trained executive speakers for decades. Their advice converges: be conversational, never read bullet walls, modulate the voice, don't over-apologize, and rehearse out loud. Nothing on the list requires talent. All of it requires reps, and yours start on this slide.

Play · Fix the delivery

Why this matters: you'll hear these exact problems in the live round in a few minutes, and one of them is probably yours. Five moments; pick the fix the research would prescribe.

Prefer a new tab? Open on YouTube.

Why this video: the head of TED on the one thing all great talks share, and why delivery polish can't rescue a talk without it.

The live round, rules

60 to 90 seconds each, strictly timed: your Big Idea sentence plus your opening line, delivered to the group. Feedback is exactly ONE specific, actionable note per speaker, drawn from today's frameworks ("you read it instead of said it," "great pause before the ask," "cut the second sentence"). The goal is reps, not perfection.

Go deeper

In practice

The condensed playbook. Anderson's five keys: frame the story as a journey, plan delivery deliberately (never memorize into deadness), work on stage presence, plan multimedia last, and put the idea first. Gallo's five tips: be concise (18 minutes beats 60), never use bullet points, modulate your voice, give the audience something extra, and rehearse relentlessly. AMA's seven principles add the mindset: perception beats perfection ("no one cares about your mistake but you"), visualize success, and speak to serve the audience, not to perform. Full text: Anderson's "How to Give a Killer Presentation" and Gallo's "What It Takes to Give a Great Presentation," both Harvard Business Review.

As a group · 12 min

Why this matters: one timed rep with one honest note changes more than an hour of theory. This is the session's centerpiece; protect it.

  1. Every participant, in turn: stand (or unmute), deliver your Big Idea and opening line. 60 to 90 seconds, timed visibly.
  2. The group gives exactly one specific note per speaker, framework-anchored, starting with what worked.
  3. Facilitator keeps momentum; no re-dos, no pile-ons. Reps over perfection.

Flying solo? Deliver it to your phone camera, timed. Watch it once. Give yourself the one note; you'll know what it is.

Section 07 · Q&A

The question is not an attack.

The moment presenters fear most is the pointed question from a senior stakeholder, and it's also the moment gravitas is most visible. The AMA's executive-speaking curriculum trains one move for it: the bridge. Acknowledge the question honestly, answer it directly (or commit to a date you will), then return to your Big Idea. Composed is not the same as defensive, and the room can tell.

Play · Judge the bridge

Why this matters: the drill after this is live rapid-fire; calibrate your ear first. Five pointed questions; pick the composed bridge each time.

Before you present: SHRM's six questions

Who's the audience? What's the goal? What are the messages? What's the content? Where's the feedback? How do I refine it? Six questions, asked before you build, and half the tough questions never get asked because you answered them in the talk.

Go deeper

In practice

The prep that makes the bridge easy: before any leadership presentation, write the three questions you're most afraid of, and draft one-sentence answers. (You already know what they are; the fear is the tell.) The honest deferral is a full member of the toolkit: "I don't want to guess; I'll have it to you by Thursday" reads as gravitas, a fluent guess that collapses in the follow-up reads as the opposite. And if your team took Navigating Difficult Conversations, you'll recognize the DNA: acknowledge before you answer is the same move as paraphrase before you argue.

As a group · 6 min

Why this matters: composure under fire is trainable, and the training is fire.

  1. Facilitator (or a volunteer playing a skeptical executive) fires 2 to 3 pointed questions at each presenter from the live round: "What's this going to cost us?" "Why hasn't this been done already?"
  2. Presenter practices the bridge: acknowledge → answer directly → return to the Big Idea.
  3. The room calls out one thing per exchange: bridge landed, or where it wobbled.

Flying solo? Write your three most-feared questions for your real presentation, and the one-sentence answers. Keep them; the capstone assumes you have them.
