05 · When things go sideways

If it breaks, you have a move

If Projector or the site fails mid-session: Teach from the printed cheat sheet: the one-sentence definition, the three steps, the traffic light, and the five superpowers all work on a whiteboard. The trainers run verbally with hands-up voting; the first try moves to the printed card.
If Learner devices can't join (wifi/filters): Run facilitator-only: trainers on the projector with the room voting out loud, prompt builder as a fill-in-the-blanks on the whiteboard, commitment on the printed card. You lose typing, not learning.
If Nobody in the room can access an approved AI tool for the live try: You demo on the projector with THEIR prompts: collect two built prompts from the room, run them live on your machine, improve each with one follow-up the room chooses. The first try becomes homework with the card; the pulse email catches it.
If Someone is visibly anxious or says 'I'm too old for this / not a tech person': The reframe that works: 'You already use AI every day: your phone's word suggestions, your spam filter. Today is just learning to use it on purpose.' Then make sure their first try lands in the room; success beats reassurance.
If An experienced AI user is bored or starts adding advanced caveats: Honor and redirect: 'That's exactly right, and it's AI 201 material: the sequel is in the Course Library.' Deputize them as a table helper for the prompt-builder moment; teaching beginners keeps experts happy.
If Fear talk takes over ('AI is dangerous / taking jobs / stealing data'): Don't dismiss, anchor: the traffic light exists exactly because the concerns are partly right. 'The risks are real, which is why we use approved tools and keep people-data out. The skill you're learning today IS the safe version.' Park policy debates; route them to the official channels.

06 · Tough questions, ready answers

Say each answer out loud once before you teach

Is AI going to take my job?

AI takes over chores, not judgment: drafts, summaries, first passes. What history shows with tools like this is that people who use them become more valuable, not less. Today's hour is exactly that move: you're not competing with AI after this session, you're the person in your office who knows how to use it safely.

Is it cheating to use AI for my work emails?

Is spellcheck cheating? Is asking a colleague to read your draft cheating? AI is a drafting tool; the thinking, the facts, and the final call stay yours. The professional standard is simple: you review everything, you check what matters, and your name means you stand behind it. Passing off unchecked AI output as finished work: that's the actual mistake.

Where does what I type actually go?

It depends entirely on the tool, and that's the whole point of the yellow light. Random free websites may keep and learn from what you type. Vanderbilt-approved tools like Copilot with your VU sign-in have contractual protections for work content. That's why internal content only goes in approved tools, and private information about people goes in none of them.

How do I know if the answer is true?

Assume it's a confident draft, not a verified fact. For anything that matters, a date, a number, a name, a claim you'll repeat, check the source the way you would if a smart new colleague told you something surprising. The tool is at its safest on tasks where truth isn't the question: drafting, rewording, brainstorming, explaining.

Which tool should I actually use?

For work content: Microsoft Copilot signed in with your VU account: that sign-in is what makes it yellow-light safe. For public, generic questions, approved tools of your choice. If you're unsure whether a tool is approved, that uncertainty IS your answer: use Copilot or ask IT.

I tried ChatGPT once and the answer was wrong or useless. Why bother?

One try with one vague question is like judging email by one message. Two fixes change everything: give it details (who it's for, what it's about, how it should sound), and use the follow-up: tell it what was wrong and watch it fix its own draft. The trainer in Idea 02 exists because the second message is where the tool gets good.

Idea 01 · What is AI?

One sentence, no jargon.

AI is a computer program that learned from millions of examples. Because it learned instead of following fixed rules, it can predict, suggest, and write things that fit your situation. It isn't a robot or magic, and it isn't always right either.

Prefer a new tab? Open on YouTube.

Why this video: the friendliest short explanation on the internet, made for total beginners. No technical background needed.

Go deeper

In practice

Think of AI as a very well-read assistant: fast, tireless, eager to help, and occasionally wrong in a confident voice. The distinction worth keeping: a regular program follows rules a person wrote, which is why a calculator never surprises you. AI learned patterns from examples, which is why your phone's word suggestions surprise you daily. The chat tools everyone is talking about, like Microsoft Copilot or ChatGPT, are that same word-suggestion idea trained on so much text that it can draft emails, summarize documents, and explain almost anything. Once you see that, most of the mystery is gone.

Idea 02 · Why & How

Type like you'd text a helpful person.

Why do people use AI? Because it takes the chores that eat your day (first drafts, long documents, blank pages) and does them in seconds. Federal Reserve researchers found that workers who use it are already saving around two hours a week. And the how is easier than you'd guess. Nothing to install, nothing to memorize. You type what you need in plain English, like texting a very capable assistant, and it answers. When the answer isn't quite right, you don't start over. You just tell it what to fix.

Step 1 · Ask

Plain English

"Write a short, friendly email inviting my team to a planning lunch on Thursday." Full sentences. Details welcome. No magic words needed.

Step 2 · Look

Read what came back

It answers in seconds. Usually good, sometimes off. Either way, you're not done yet, and neither is it.

Step 3 · Improve

One follow-up

"Warmer." "Shorter." "Mention the RSVP deadline." It rewrites instantly, and it never gets tired of feedback. This step is where it gets good.

Go deeper

In practice

Two habits carry beginners a long way. First, details make the difference: "write an email" gets you something generic; "write a warm, three-sentence email to my six-person team about Thursday's planning lunch" gets you something usable. Second, treat the first answer as a draft, always. AI writes the draft, and you stay the editor. It sounds confident even when it's wrong, so anything important (a date, a number, a name, a fact you'll repeat) gets checked by you before your name goes on it.

  3. Keep it. You'll build the real thing in Idea 05.

Idea 03 · When & When Not

The traffic light. Three seconds.

AI is great for drafts, ideas, summaries, and explanations. It is not the place for private information about real people, and it's not a decision-maker. Before you paste anything, one three-second question: what color is this?

🟢 Green · Go ahead

Public or generic

Questions about public topics, generic drafts, brainstorming, "explain this to me." Any approved tool, any time.

🟡 Yellow · Approved tools only

Internal work content

Your drafts, internal announcements, work documents. Fine, but ONLY in Vanderbilt-approved tools like Copilot signed in with your VU account. Never a random free website.

In practice

Why the caution about facts? AI predicts what a good answer LOOKS like, and sometimes it produces something that looks right but isn't. Inside the light: Vanderbilt classifies data in levels, and each approved tool has a ceiling. ChatGPT EDU (VU account) is approved up to Level 2. Amplify and Microsoft Copilot (VU account) are approved for Level 3 work. Children's names, photos, and records go in no AI tool.

The hard cases: ask "who is this about" before "how sensitive does it feel." A student's essay is about a student. Meeting notes naming colleagues are about colleagues until the names come out. A recommendation letter is about its subject.

Never, in any tool: Social Security numbers · Student records or grades (FERPA) · Health information (HIPAA) · Children's names, photos, and records · Salaries and personnel files · Passwords and credentials · Anything confidential about a real person.

Idea 04 · What It Can Do

Five superpowers, zero code.

You don't need a list of a hundred uses. You need five, and you'll recognize the chores they eat: Draft it (first versions of anything), Summarize it (long thing → short thing), Brainstorm it (ten ideas in ten seconds), Explain it (jargon → plain English), and Rewrite it (same facts, different tone or length).

What it can't do

It can't know what happened in your meeting this morning, and it can't take responsibility for what goes out under your name. That part stays yours. AI does the chore, you do the judgment, and that division of labor is the whole arrangement.

Go deeper

In practice

A realistic first month looks like this: week one, you draft two emails and summarize one long document. Week two, you brainstorm names for something and ask it to explain an acronym-filled message. By week four, you reach for it without thinking, the way you reach for search. In a 2024 Federal Reserve Bank of St. Louis survey, workers using these tools reported saving about 5% of their work hours, roughly 2.2 hours in a 40-hour week. The savings start with the first email.
