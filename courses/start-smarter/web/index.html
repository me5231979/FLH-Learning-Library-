Idea 01 · What is AI?

One sentence, no jargon.

AI is a computer program that learned from millions of examples. Because it learned instead of following fixed rules, it can predict, suggest, and write things that fit your situation. It isn't a robot or magic, and it isn't always right either.

Prefer a new tab? Open on YouTube.

Why this video: the friendliest short explanation on the internet, made for total beginners. No technical background needed.

Go deeper

In practice

Think of AI as a very well-read assistant: fast, tireless, eager to help, and occasionally wrong in a confident voice. The distinction worth keeping: a regular program follows rules a person wrote, which is why a calculator never surprises you. AI learned patterns from examples, which is why your phone's word suggestions surprise you daily. The chat tools everyone is talking about, like Microsoft Copilot or ChatGPT, are that same word-suggestion idea trained on so much text that it can draft emails, summarize documents, and explain almost anything. Once you see that, most of the mystery is gone.

On your own · 2 min

Why this matters: spotting AI in your own day makes the definition stick.

  1. Look at your phone. Find two things on it that learned from examples: word suggestions, photo face-grouping, map routes, music picks.
  2. Say the sentence once: "AI is a program that learned from examples." That's the whole foundation.

Idea 02 · Why & How

Type like you'd text a helpful person.

Why do people use AI? Because it takes the chores that eat your day (first drafts, long documents, blank pages) and does them in seconds. Federal Reserve researchers found that workers who use it are already saving around two hours a week. And the how is easier than you'd guess. Nothing to install, nothing to memorize. You type what you need in plain English, like texting a very capable assistant, and it answers. When the answer isn't quite right, you don't start over. You just tell it what to fix.

Step 1 · Ask

Plain English

"Write a short, friendly email inviting my team to a planning lunch on Thursday." Full sentences. Details welcome. No magic words needed.

Step 2 · Look

Read what came back

It answers in seconds. Usually good, sometimes off. Either way, you're not done yet, and neither is it.

Step 3 · Improve

One follow-up

"Warmer." "Shorter." "Mention the RSVP deadline." It rewrites instantly, and it never gets tired of feedback. This step is where it gets good.

Go deeper

In practice

Two habits carry beginners a long way. First, details make the difference: "write an email" gets you something generic; "write a warm, three-sentence email to my six-person team about Thursday's planning lunch" gets you something usable. Second, treat the first answer as a draft, always. AI writes the draft, and you stay the editor. It sounds confident even when it's wrong, so anything important (a date, a number, a name, a fact you'll repeat) gets checked by you before your name goes on it.

On your own · 3 min

Why this matters: one real ask-look-improve loop teaches more than an hour of reading about it.

  1. Think of one email or note you need to write anyway this week.
  2. Jot down the plain-English request you'd type, including two details: who it's for and what it should sound like.
  3. Keep it. You'll build the real thing in Idea 05.

Idea 03 · When & When Not

The traffic light. Three seconds.

AI is great for drafts, ideas, summaries, and explanations. It is not the place for private information about real people, and it's not a decision-maker. Before you paste anything, one three-second question: what color is this?

🟢 Green · Go ahead

Public or generic

Questions about public topics, generic drafts, brainstorming, "explain this to me." Any approved tool, any time.

🟡 Yellow · Approved tools only

Internal work content

Your drafts, internal announcements, work documents. Fine, but ONLY in Vanderbilt-approved tools like Copilot signed in with your VU account. Never a random free website.

🔴 Red · Keep it out

Private info about people

Names with grades, health details, personnel issues, passwords, anything confidential about a real person. No AI tool, full stop. Describe the situation without names, or don't.

Go deeper

In practice

Why the caution about facts? AI predicts what a good answer LOOKS like, and sometimes it produces something that looks right but isn't. It might cite a book that doesn't exist, or state a date with total confidence and be a year off. The professionals' habit is simple: use AI freely for drafts and ideas, where being wrong costs nothing, and verify anything you'll repeat, publish, or decide on. Decisions about people (hiring, grading, evaluations) stay with people. AI can inform your thinking, but it doesn't get a vote.

On your own · 2 min

Why this matters: the color call only helps if you make it on YOUR tasks.

  1. Think of three things from your real week you might hand to AI.
  2. Call the color on each. When in doubt: leave the names and details out.

The data rule · the same card in every AI course

Before anything goes into an AI tool, one three-second question: what color is this?

🟢
Green · Public or generic
Questions about public topics, generic drafts, brainstorming, "explain this to me." Any approved tool.
🟡
Yellow · Internal work
Your drafts, internal announcements, budgets, grant drafts, internal policies. Approved Vanderbilt tools only, signed in with your VU account. Never a personal account or a random free site.
🔴
Red · Private information about people
Names with grades, health details, personnel issues, passwords, anything confidential about a real person. No AI tool, full stop.

Inside the light: Vanderbilt classifies data in levels, and each approved tool has a ceiling. ChatGPT EDU (VU account) is approved up to Level 2. Amplify and Microsoft Copilot (VU account) are approved for Level 3 work. Children's names, photos, and records go in no AI tool.

The hard cases: ask "who is this about" before "how sensitive does it feel." A student's essay is about a student. Meeting notes naming colleagues are about colleagues until the names come out. A recommendation letter is about its subject.

Never, in any tool: Social Security numbers · Student records or grades (FERPA) · Health information (HIPAA) · Children's names, photos, and records · Salaries and personnel files · Passwords and credentials · Anything confidential about a real person.

This card is the working shorthand for Vanderbilt IT's data classification and approved-tool guidance: vanderbilt.edu/it/policies. When the two ever disagree, the policy wins.

Idea 04 · What It Can Do

Five superpowers, zero code.

You don't need a list of a hundred uses. You need five, and you'll recognize the chores they eat: Draft it (first versions of anything), Summarize it (long thing → short thing), Brainstorm it (ten ideas in ten seconds), Explain it (jargon → plain English), and Rewrite it (same facts, different tone or length).

What it can't do

It can't know what happened in your meeting this morning, and it can't take responsibility for what goes out under your name. That part stays yours. AI does the chore, you do the judgment, and that division of labor is the whole arrangement.

Go deeper

In practice

A realistic first month looks like this: week one, you draft two emails and summarize one long document. Week two, you brainstorm names for something and ask it to explain an acronym-filled message. By week four, you reach for it without thinking, the way you reach for search. In a 2024 Federal Reserve Bank of St. Louis survey, workers using these tools reported saving about 5% of their work hours, roughly 2.2 hours in a 40-hour week. The savings start with the first email.

The bigger picture · Why this is worth learning

This is already changing real lives.

Workers who use these tools save about 2.2 hours a week, per a 2024 Federal Reserve survey. And the same technology behind your first email draft is doing some remarkable good in the world.

Health

Finding cancer earlier

In a Swedish trial of 105,000 women (The Lancet Digital Health), AI-supported screening found 29% more cancers and cut scan-reading workload 44%. Doctors made every call.

Accessibility

Describing the world for blind users

Be My Eyes lets a blind person photograph anything (a menu, a label, the fridge) and hear what it says in seconds. The demo is on the next screen.

Voice

Giving voices back

People losing speech to ALS bank recordings, and AI rebuilds their voice so they keep speaking as themselves. Free through Bridging Voice and ElevenLabs.

Communication

A drafting partner for autistic adults

Researchers studying autistic professionals found many use AI to draft messages and decode workplace phrasing. One participant: "I use ChatGPT to humanize my words."

Go deeper

In practice

Notice the pattern in every one of these stories. The AI never works alone: doctors read the scans, blind users decide what to photograph and what to trust, ALS patients choose their words, autistic professionals edit every draft before sending. The technology does a chore that used to be a barrier, and a person stays in charge. That's the same arrangement you're learning today, just with higher stakes. Sources, if you want to read further: the MASAI trial in The Lancet Digital Health, the St. Louis Fed productivity survey, Be My Eyes, and Bridging Voice.

In practice

Where to actually go for your first try: use a Vanderbilt-approved tool. Microsoft Copilot signed in with your VU account is the safe default for work content.
