05 · When things go sideways

If it breaks, you have a move

If Projector or the site fails mid-session: Teach from the whiteboard: the four step types, the draft-verify-decide pattern, and the verifier's rubric all draw in seconds. The mapping and assignment drills never needed a screen; the capstone moves to the printed card. The lab becomes a group walk-through: read each step's three options aloud and have the room vote.
If Learner devices can't join (wifi or filters): Run facilitator-only: the trainers play well on the projector with the room voting A/B/C by hands; the private mapper and capstone move to paper. You lose typing, not learning.
If You're 10+ minutes behind by the Redesign Lab (05): Declare the Core path silently: pair drills become solo passes, debriefs shrink to one voice, and the closing tightens to the fist-to-five plus commitment round. Never cut the lab, the mapper, or the capstone; they carry the session's practice objectives.
If Someone names a colleague or starts prosecuting a specific person's job: Protect the norm warmly: 'Hold that. Workflows and roles, never names. The redesign works on steps, not people.' If it keeps happening, it usually means a real staffing anxiety is in the room; name the pilot standard's honest-hours rule and move the person to a private follow-up.
If The room is skeptical: 'AI is overhyped, this will blow over': Don't argue futures; play Guess the Number and let the adoption data speak. The frame that lands: whatever you think of the hype, 88 percent adoption means your team is already using it as unmanaged shortcuts, and shortcuts without design is the worst of both worlds. Redesign is the risk-management position, whatever the hype does.
If The room is the opposite: AI enthusiasts who want to automate everything today: Aim the enthusiasm at the pattern: 'Great, you're the pilot owners.' Then hold the line on the two human steps; the Rate the Check trainer and the lab's weak tier usually do the sobering for you. Enthusiasts fail redesigns through soft verification, and the automation-bias material is written for exactly them.
If Someone raises a real data-privacy concern mid-flight: Stop and honor it; this is the one interruption that outranks the agenda. Restate the traffic light (green public, yellow internal in approved VU tools only, red private information about people, never), confirm their case against it, and if it's genuinely ambiguous, park it with a promise to route it to the right owner rather than improvising policy from the front of the room.

06 · Tough questions, ready answers

Say each answer out loud once before you teach

Is this how our jobs get cut? Be honest.

The honest answer has two parts. Managers who run this pattern are moving mechanical volume to machines and moving people toward verification, judgment, and the work that was getting skipped; that is the design, and the pilot standard requires saying out loud what the saved hours are for. And no manager can promise what an organization decides years out. What you can control is whether your team's redesign is done with the people who do the work, with a visible metric, or done to them. The first kind builds the skills that stay valuable; the second kind builds the fear that makes everything fail.

Why not just wait? The models improve every few months; today's redesign will be obsolete.

The map doesn't expire. Steps, handoffs, checks, and decide points outlive any particular model, and a team that has mapped its work can re-assign steps in an afternoon when capabilities shift. Waiting has a real cost the survey makes visible: the shortcuts spread anyway, unmanaged, and the organizations getting value now are the ones that started designing. You're not betting on a model; you're building the muscle of assignment.

My team already uses AI constantly. Doesn't that mean we've done this?

Usage and redesign are different things, and the data splits them cleanly: 88 percent use, one in five redesigned. Personal shortcuts have no named verifier, no shared standard, and no metric; when one goes wrong it damages trust in everything. The test: if your best user left tomorrow, would the workflow keep its gains? If not, it's a shortcut. The session's whole job is converting shortcuts into designed, shared workflows.

Doesn't verification just move the work around? We save an hour drafting and spend it checking.

If the check costs what the work did, that step was a bad draft candidate, and the pattern says so explicitly: verification has a budget. On good draft candidates the math is lopsided: an hour of pulling and formatting becomes minutes of spot-checking against source. The design question is always the ratio, and the miss log tells you over time whether the ratio is holding or the step should go back to a human.

What about hallucinations? If AI makes things up, how can we trust any draft?

You don't trust drafts; that's the point of the word. The pattern treats every AI output as unverified by definition, which is why the verify step has an owner, sources, and a reject path. Notice the symmetry: human first drafts also contain errors, and mature workflows always had review. What's new is the failure texture: AI errors arrive fluent and confident, which is exactly why the check runs against sources instead of against how the draft reads.

I don't have authority to redesign processes. They come from above.

Some do, and start where your authority actually is: almost every team runs workflows the org chart never sees, the reports, updates, summaries, and preps that exist because your team invented them. Those are fully yours to map and redesign, and they're where the hours hide anyway. And a manager who arrives at the process-owner's door with a map, a pilot result, and a metric is making a request that tends to get granted.

Section 01 · The Case

The tool is the cheap part. The redesign is the advantage.

Before the method, the evidence. Every number in this game comes from McKinsey's State of AI research, the long-running global survey of how organizations actually use AI, and most rooms guess them wrong. The gap between what people expect and what the data says is exactly where this session lives.

Play · Guess the number

Why this matters: a statistic you guessed at, and missed, sticks far better than one you were shown. Four findings; call each number before it's revealed.

Quick check: Of everything McKinsey measured, which practice showed the strongest link to bottom-line impact from gen AI?

Go deeper

In practice

The source for the game is McKinsey's State of AI survey: 88 percent of organizations now use AI in at least one function, yet only around 6 percent clear the bar for high performers, where AI drives meaningful bottom-line impact. The high performers are 2.8 times more likely to have fundamentally redesigned workflows (55 percent versus 20 percent), and of everything the survey measured, workflow redesign showed the strongest link to EBIT impact. The careers publication draws the managerial conclusion: mapping the team's processes and deciding where AI fits is becoming the manager's core job. Notice what the data is really saying: your team almost certainly uses AI already. The open question is whether anyone has designed how.

As a group · 4 min

Why this matters: the adoption-value gap becomes personal the moment you look for it on your own team.

  1. In pairs: name one task where someone on your team already uses AI, officially or quietly.
  2. Call it: is that a redesigned workflow, or a personal shortcut nobody else can see?
  3. Hands to the room: shortcuts or redesigns? Count it out loud. Most rooms are nearly all shortcuts, which is the 88-versus-6 gap sitting in your own team.

Flying solo? List three tasks your team repeats every week. Mark any where AI already shows up, officially or not. Those marks are your redesign candidates for the rest of this session.

Section 02 · The Map

You can't redesign work you can't see.

Every workflow is a chain: something triggers it, steps run, handoffs happen, something ships. Most teams have never written theirs down, and the official version rarely matches what actually happens on a Monday. So the first managerial move has nothing to do with AI: map one workflow as it really runs. Once it's on paper, every step turns out to be one of four kinds.

1 · Gather

Collect the inputs

Pulling numbers from systems, rereading threads, assembling context. High volume, low judgment, and usually the step that eats the hours.

2 · Transform

Make the draft

Turning inputs into a first version: the written update, the summary, the formatted deck. Mechanical shaping, done to a standard someone else defined.

3 · Judge

Check it against standards

Is it accurate, is it good, is it safe to ship? Judging takes a human who knows the sources, the audience, and what wrong looks like.

4 · Commit

Decide and own it

Send, approve, spend, hire. The step where accountability lives. Whoever commits answers for the outcome, which is why this step behaves differently from all the others.

Play · Name the step

Why this matters: the four kinds only help if you can spot them in real work. Five moments from ordinary workflows; name the step type.

Why this matters: the rest of this session runs on a real workflow from your team, and writing it down is the first act of the redesign. Nothing you type is saved or sent anywhere.

Go deeper

In practice

Three rules make a workable map. Map one real instance, following what actually happened last Monday rather than the official process doc; the two usually differ, and the redesign has to work on the real one. Keep it at five to nine steps; finer than that and you're documenting keystrokes, coarser and the draft candidates hide inside vague blobs like "prepare the report." And interview the person who actually does the work, because the grind is invisible from one level up. Managers consistently misjudge which step eats the hours, and the person doing it can tell you in one sentence.

As a group · 5 min

Why this matters: hearing your own workflow read back to you is the fastest way to find the steps you forgot you do.

  1. Pairs. One person narrates the workflow they just mapped, start to finish, as it really runs.
  2. The partner writes the steps as a numbered list and reads them back. Fix what's missing; there's usually a hidden step.
  3. Together, tag each step G, T, J, or C. Count the tags: the G and T steps are tomorrow's draft candidates.

Flying solo? Write your workflow's steps as a numbered list and tag each one G, T, J, or C. Count how many are gather and transform; that number is the size of your redesign opportunity.

Section 03 · The Pattern

Draft. Verify. Decide.

Here is the pattern the whole session turns on. For every step on your map, make one assignment: AI drafts it, a human verifies it, or a human decides it. The assignment happens once, when you design the workflow, and then everyone knows who does what. That is the difference between a redesigned process and a pile of personal shortcuts.

AI drafts

The volume

Gather and transform steps: pulling, assembling, summarizing, first-pass writing. AI is fast, tireless, and confidently wrong just often enough that a draft is all it gets to be.

A human verifies

The check

A named person checks the draft against real sources and real standards, with the power to reject it. Verification is the hinge of the whole pattern; section 04 is entirely about it.

A human decides

The call

Judge and commit steps: what ships, what's spent, who's hired, what the client hears. Decisions carry accountability, and accountability doesn't delegate to a tool.

Play · Assign the step

Why this matters: the pattern is easy to recite and harder to apply. Five steps from real workflows; make the assignment.

YellowInternal work. Approved VU tools only.
RedPrivate information about people. Never.

If a step touches red data, the draft assignment is off the table in unapproved tools, whatever the efficiency case. The light outranks the pattern.

Go deeper

In practice

Two questions sort almost every step. First, volume against stakes: high-volume, low-stakes work drafts freely; low-volume, high-stakes work usually isn't worth drafting at all. Second, the error question: if this step is wrong, who gets hurt, and how fast would we find out? Fast-caught, cheap errors are safe to draft; slow, expensive ones need a human earlier. And one design principle worth saying out loud: assignments attach to steps, never to people. Nobody's job becomes "watch the machine all day." A good redesign leaves every person with a mix of verifying, deciding, and the work only they can do.

As a group · 6 min

Why this matters: assigning someone else's workflow is easier than assigning your own, and the disagreements are where the real learning is.

  1. Pairs. Swap the workflows you mapped in section 02.
  2. Assign every step of your partner's workflow: draft, verify, or decide. Five minutes, gut calls allowed.
  3. Compare with what the owner would have chosen. Bring your sharpest disagreement to the room; disagreements almost always sit on a judge step, which is exactly where they should.

Flying solo? Assign every step of your own mapped workflow, then mark the one assignment you're least sure of. That step is your pilot's first design question.

Facilitator scriptFull 12 min · Core 9

Install the draft-verify-decide pattern as a per-step assignment made once at design time, with the traffic-light data rule as a hard boundary on every draft assignment.

Say

“Here's the pattern everything else hangs on. For every step on your map, one assignment. AI drafts: the gather and transform volume, because AI is fast, tireless, and confidently wrong just often enough that a draft is all it gets to be. A human verifies: a named person checks the draft against real sources with the power to reject it. A human decides: the judge and commit steps, because decisions carry accountability and accountability doesn't delegate to a tool. You make the assignment once, when you design the workflow, and then everyone knows who does what. One more rule sits above all of this: the traffic light from the AI courses. Green, public or generic, go. Yellow, internal, approved VU tools only. Red, private information about people, never. The light outranks the pattern, every time.”

Do
  1. Walk the three cards: AI drafts the volume, a named human verifies against sources, a human decides where accountability lives.
  2. Stress the design move: the assignment happens once per workflow, at design time, and then everyone knows who does what. That is what separates a redesign from a pile of shortcuts.
  3. Send them to Assign the Step: five real steps, make the assignment.
  4. Walk the traffic light card and be unambiguous: green public, yellow internal in approved VU tools only, red private information about people, never. The light outranks the pattern.
  5. Run the pair swap from Go deeper: assign your partner's mapped workflow, then compare with what the owner would have chosen; surface one sharp disagreement to the room.
Ask

“From the pair swap: where did you and your partner disagree on an assignment, and what was the disagreement really about?”

Expect:

  • Judge steps: one person saw mechanical checking, the other saw judgment
  • The owner defended keeping a grind step manual 'because quality'
  • Someone wanted AI further into a commit step than their partner would allow

Respond: Name the pattern in the disagreements: 'Notice the fights are almost never about gather steps; they're about where judgment starts. That boundary is the real design decision, and the disagreement means you found it. When in doubt, keep the human and revisit after the pilot's miss log has data.'

Debrief

Every step on your map now has an owner: the machine drafts, a named human verifies, a human decides. The weakest link in that chain is the middle one, which is why it gets its own section.

Validate: 4+ of 5 on the trainer; every learner's mapped workflow has draft-verify-decide assignments on its steps, and the room can state the traffic light unprompted.

Watch for: Two drifts: assigning by person ('Sam is good with AI, Sam does the AI parts'), which the pattern explicitly forbids, and quiet red-light violations in draft enthusiasm ('AI could summarize the candidates!'). Correct both immediately and kindly; the traffic light correction matters more than the flow of the room.

Transition

“Because a draft without a real check isn't a workflow. It's a rumor with good formatting.”

60-min core: Core: cards and traffic light at full strength, trainer on devices; the pair swap becomes a solo pass on your own map with the least-sure assignment marked.

Section 04 · The Verify

A check that can't fail isn't a check.

Verification is where redesigns quietly die. AI output arrives fluent and confident, week one's careful reader becomes week six's skimmer, and researchers have a name for the drift: automation bias, the documented human tendency to accept a system's output as attention fades. The fix is never "try harder." The fix is designing the check so it can actually fail.

The verifier's rubric

1. A named owner: one person verifies this step, and it's in their job, not their spare time. 2. Source-checked: claims are checked against the tracker, the data, the meeting notes, never against "sounds right." 3. A reject path with a log: when the check fails, the draft goes back, and the miss gets written down. A check that has never caught anything is evidence that nobody's looking, and the log is how you tell the difference.

Play · Rate the check

Why this matters: rubber stamps look exactly like verification from a distance. Five setups; call each one real verification, a rubber stamp, or a wrong-sized check.

Quick check: What actually counters automation bias in a workflow?

Go deeper

In practice

Verification has a budget. If checking the draft costs as much as doing the work did, the step was never a draft candidate; send it back to a human and spend your verification budget where drafting actually pays. Sampling is the usual answer at volume: check three claims against source every time, rotate which three, and log every miss. The log turns verification from a vibe into a metric, because catch rate over time tells you whether the checks are working, whether the drafts are improving, and whether the verifier has gone to sleep. All three are things a manager can act on; "we trust it now" is not.

As a group · 5 min

Why this matters: designing a check from scratch, once, makes rubber stamps visible everywhere afterward.

  1. Pairs. Take this step: AI summarizes 40 open customer tickets into themes for the ops meeting.
  2. Design its verify in four lines: who owns it, what they check against, how often, and what happens on a miss.
  3. One pair reads theirs; the room stress-tests it with one question: how would this check fail quietly?

Flying solo? Design the verify for the draft step of your own mapped workflow: owner, source, cadence, reject path. Four lines, written down.

Facilitator scriptFull 11 min · Core 7

Make verification a designed artifact (named owner, real sources, logged reject path) and install automation bias as the failure mode that kills redesigns in week three.

Say

“Verification is where redesigns quietly die, and it dies through a well-documented pattern called automation bias: the drift toward accepting a system's output as attention fades. Week one, your verifier reads every line. Week six, the drafts have been right so many times that reading became skimming, and that's precisely when the expensive miss ships. You cannot fix this with 'be careful.' You fix it with design: a named owner whose job includes the check. Claims checked against sources, the tracker, the data, the notes, never against whether it sounds right, because sounding right is the one thing AI always does. And a reject path with a log, because a check that has never caught anything is not evidence of quality; it's evidence that nobody's looking.”

Do
  1. Land automation bias first: fluent output plus fading attention equals rubber stamps, and it happens to smart, careful people on schedule.
  2. Walk the verifier's rubric on the gold card: named owner, source-checked, reject path with a log.
  3. Land the counterintuitive tell: a check that has never caught anything is a warning light, and the log is how you tell diligence from theater.
  4. Send them to Rate the Check: five setups, call each one real, rubber stamp, or wrong-sized.
  5. Run the quick check on automation bias, then the pair drill: design the verify for the ticket-summary step in four lines, and stress-test one pair's design with 'how would this fail quietly?'
Ask

“Think of a check that already exists somewhere in your team's work. When it passes something, how would you know whether that was diligence or a rubber stamp?”

Expect:

  • Silence, then 'honestly, I couldn't tell the difference'
  • Someone names a real tell: comments in the doc, questions coming back
  • Someone realizes their approval step has never once rejected anything

Respond: Anchor the log: 'The only reliable tell is a record of catches. Rejections coming back are the heartbeat of a real check. If an approval step has passed everything for a year, you don't have a check; you have a signature with extra steps.'

Debrief

Owner, sources, reject path, log. Verification designed this way is cheap, and it's the difference between the lab's strong tier and its disasters, which you're about to experience directly.

Validate: 4+ of 5 on the trainer; the pair designs include all four lines (owner, source, cadence, reject path); the room can explain why zero catches is a warning sign.

Watch for: The over-correction: learners concluding everything needs heavyweight review. Keep the budget idea alive; wrong-sized checks are a real failure mode, and the trainer's agenda item exists to make that legitimate. Verification spends attention, and attention is the scarcest thing your team has.

Transition

“Enough theory. You have a Monday morning to redesign.”

60-min core: Core: bias framing in ninety seconds, rubric verbatim, trainer on devices; the pair drill becomes a solo four-line design for your own workflow's draft step.

Section 05 · The Lab

Rebuild the Monday report.

Your team ships a weekly client status report, and it eats half of every Monday: someone pulls numbers from three systems, someone writes the update, a peer glances at it, and it goes out. Redesign it one step at a time, then see how your design is performing four weeks in.

Practice · The Redesign Lab

Why this matters: every choice here is one you'll make on your real workflow this week, and the failure modes are the ones that actually happen. Choose how each step runs, then run the month.

Choose all four steps
Go deeper

In practice

Notice where the lab punishes you. Never for using AI too much or too little; always for the design around it: drafts with no real check, checks with no owner, and commits handed to automation. That mirrors the field data: when redesigns fail, the postmortem almost never says "the model wasn't good enough." It says a wrong thing flowed through a soft check and reached someone who mattered, and then the tool took the blame for the design. Week three is the classic failure window: late enough that vigilance has faded, early enough that the checks haven't been made structural yet.

As a group · 6 min

Why this matters: finding the week-three failure in your own design, before it happens, is the cheapest fix you will ever make.

  1. Pairs. Put your mapped, assigned workflow from sections 02 and 03 on the table.
  2. Find its week-three failure: the specific wrong thing most likely to slip through your verify step once attention fades.
  3. Strengthen that one check using the rubric: owner, source, cadence, reject path. Say the fixed version out loud.

Flying solo? Same drill on your own: name your redesign's week-three failure and rewrite the one check that catches it.

Facilitator scriptFull 14 min · Core 10

Give every learner a full felt rep of the method: four steps of a real workflow, one assignment each, and a four-week outcome that rewards design quality rather than AI enthusiasm. The session's deepest practice block.

Say

“Here's a workflow you all recognize: the weekly client status report. Half of every Monday. Someone pulls numbers from three systems, someone writes the update, a peer glances at it, and the manager sends it. You're going to redesign it step by step: for each of the four steps, pick how it runs. Then the lab runs four weeks and shows you what your design did. One warning from the field data: when these redesigns fail, it is almost never because the AI wasn't good enough. It's because a wrong thing flowed through a soft check and reached a client. Watch where the lab punishes you; it's never for how much AI you used.”

Do
  1. Set the scenario in one breath: the weekly client report eats half of every Monday, four steps, redesign it.
  2. Send them into the lab on devices: choose how each step runs, then run the month.
  3. Let people get their four-week outcome individually; the mid and weak tiers teach more than the strong one, so don't coach choices in advance.
  4. Ask for a show of hands by tier: who got the strong month, who got week-three problems. Have one weak-tier learner read their coach notes aloud; the room learns from the postmortem.
  5. Push the rerun: strengthen your weakest step and run the month again. The delta between runs is the lesson.
  6. Then the pair drill: find the week-three failure in your OWN mapped workflow and strengthen that one check with the rubric.
Ask

“For those who hit the mid or weak tier: which single choice cost you the month, and would your real team have made the same one?”

Expect:

  • The soft check: 'reads well' or the peer once-over
  • The auto-send: it felt efficient right up until the outcome
  • An honest 'my real team runs the weak version of this today'

Respond: Treat that last answer as the room's win: 'That recognition is the whole lab. The weak tier isn't a hypothetical; it's a description of unmanaged shortcuts, which is what most AI use looks like today. You now know the three fixes: owner, source, log.'

Debrief

The lab's scoring is the session's thesis in miniature: value comes from the design around the AI, and week three is where soft designs go to die. Your real workflow's week-three failure now has a name and a fix.

Validate: Every learner completes at least one lab run; most reach the strong tier on a rerun; every learner can name their real workflow's week-three failure and the check that catches it.

Watch for: Learners who game the lab by picking the obviously maximal option everywhere without reading the coaching. The question that re-engages them: 'which of these choices would your actual team push back on, and why?' Also watch clock discipline; this section has the least slack.

Transition

“One thing left, and it's the thing that decides whether any of this survives contact with your team.”

60-min core: Core: one lab run instead of run-plus-rerun (rerun only for learners who hit weak tier), tier hands stay, the pair drill becomes a solo week-three-failure hunt. This is the floor; never cut the lab entirely.

Section 06 · The Team

Redesign is a team sport.

A redesign announced from above sounds like "the machine is coming for your work." The same redesign run as a pilot with the people who do the work sounds like "we're buying back your Monday mornings." McKinsey's high performers pair workflow change with visible leadership attention, and the practical corollary lands here: the line manager is where a redesign lives or dies. Your announcement line is the first design decision your team will actually hear.

The pilot standard

One workflow, two to four weeks. The doers design the checks, because they know where the process bites. A visible metric: hours saved and errors caught, posted where the team can see both. A real rollback, genuinely available without shame. An honest answer to what the saved hours are for, said out loud. If people suspect saved time converts into cut roles, they will make sure there is no saved time.

Play · Judge the announcement

Why this matters: the scenario is real: you're announcing the report pilot to your team on Monday. Five opening lines; call each one a good setup, fear or hype, or too vague to change anything.

Quick check: Why should the people who do the work design the verify step?

Go deeper

In practice

The trust math is simple and unforgiving. A pilot that names its workflow, its owner, its metric, and its review date can be believed, because every claim in it is checkable. An announcement built from "leverage," "efficiency," and "transformation" cannot, so people fill the vagueness with the scariest available story. And the review meeting is half the point: the team looks at the metric together and decides what happens next, which means the redesign's second iteration belongs to them. That is how one pilot becomes a habit instead of an initiative.

As a group · 5 min

Why this matters: hearing announcement lines called out loud calibrates yours before Monday, when it counts.

  1. Small groups: draft ONE announcement line for the workflow you redesigned in the lab drill.
  2. Groups read their lines aloud. After each, the room calls it: good setup, fear or hype, or vague.
  3. Rewrite the room's scariest line and its vaguest line, live, until both pass the checkable-claims test.

Flying solo? Draft your announcement line, then test it: does it name the workflow, the owner, the metric, the review date, and what the saved hours are for?

Facilitator scriptFull 10 min · Core 6

Install the pilot standard (doers design checks, visible metric, real rollback, honest hours) and drill the announcement line, the first artifact a team actually hears from a redesign.

Say

“Everything you've built today can be killed in one sentence, and it's usually the first sentence your team hears about it. A redesign announced from above sounds like 'the machine is coming for your work,' and a team that hears that will make sure the pilot fails, quietly and completely. The same redesign run as a pilot sounds different: one workflow, two to four weeks. The people who do the work design the checks, because they know where it bites. The metric, hours saved and errors caught, posted where everyone can see it. A real rollback, available without shame. And the honest answer, out loud, about what the saved hours are for. Your announcement line is a design artifact. Draft it like one.”

Do
  1. Land the emotional reality first: an announced redesign sounds like 'the machine is coming for your work' unless it's designed not to.
  2. Walk the pilot standard card: one workflow, doers design the checks, visible metric, real rollback, honest answer about the hours.
  3. Say the hard part plainly: if people suspect saved time converts into cut roles, they will make sure there is no saved time.
  4. Send them to Judge the Announcement: five lines, call each one good, fear-or-hype, or vague.
  5. Run the quick check, then the group draft-aloud: each group drafts one announcement line for their lab redesign, the room calls each, and the scariest plus vaguest get rewritten live.
Ask

“What ARE the saved hours for on your team? Name the real work that's been getting skipped.”

Expect:

  • Client or stakeholder time that keeps getting cannibalized
  • The improvement work: documentation, cleanup, the backlog nobody touches
  • An honest 'I don't know yet,' usually from the busiest manager in the room

Respond: Push past the first answer: 'Say it as a sentence your team would believe, with the work named. If you can't finish the sentence, that's your homework before the mapping session, because the pilot standard requires saying it out loud in the first minute.'

Debrief

The pilot standard turns a scary announcement into a set of checkable claims, and checkable claims are the only thing teams trust. The doers design the checks; the metric hangs on the wall; the hours have a named destination.

Validate: 4+ of 5 on the trainer; drafted lines name the workflow, owner, metric, and review date; the room can say what the saved hours are for without the word 'efficiency.'

Watch for: The manager who says 'my team will just be told.' Don't argue authority; point at the lab: the weak tier is what compliance without ownership produces. And keep the hours conversation honest; overpromising job safety you can't guarantee is its own trust failure, and the tough-questions bank has the honest version.

Transition

“That's the full method. Quiz first, then the card that puts a date on it.”

60-min core: Core: pilot standard and the fear line at full strength, trainer on devices, quick check stays; the draft-aloud shrinks to one group's line judged by the room.
