Workflow & Process Redesign · Vanderbilt

Redesign the work.
Then add the AI.

Nearly every organization now uses AI somewhere, and almost none are getting real value from it. McKinsey's State of AI research found one practice separating the ones that are: they redesigned their workflows. That work happens at your level. The highest-impact managerial use of AI is mapping how your team's work flows and deciding, step by step, where AI drafts, where a human verifies, and where a human decides.

Self-paced · Core ~30 min · Full ~45 min (Core is the numbered sections, lab, and card; Full adds every Go deeper panel and the glossary)

  3. Apply the draft-verify-decide pattern to assign every step: AI drafts, a human verifies, a human decides

The data rule · the same card in every AI course

Before anything goes into an AI tool, one three-second question: what color is this?

🟢
Green · Public or generic
Questions about public topics, generic drafts, brainstorming, "explain this to me." Any approved tool.
🟡
Yellow · Internal work
Your drafts, internal announcements, budgets, grant drafts, internal policies. Approved Vanderbilt tools only, signed in with your VU account. Never a personal account or a random free site.
🔴
Red · Private information about people
Names with grades, health details, personnel issues, passwords, anything confidential about a real person. No AI tool, full stop.

Inside the light: Vanderbilt classifies data in levels, and each approved tool has a ceiling. ChatGPT EDU (VU account) is approved up to Level 2. Amplify and Microsoft Copilot (VU account) are approved for Level 3 work. Children's names, photos, and records go in no AI tool.

The hard cases: ask "who is this about" before "how sensitive does it feel." A student's essay is about a student. Meeting notes naming colleagues are about colleagues until the names come out. A recommendation letter is about its subject.

Never, in any tool: Social Security numbers · Student records or grades (FERPA) · Health information (HIPAA) · Children's names, photos, and records · Salaries and personnel files · Passwords and credentials · Anything confidential about a real person.

This card is the working shorthand for Vanderbilt IT's data classification and approved-tool guidance: vanderbilt.edu/it/policies. When the two ever disagree, the policy wins.

Go deeper

In practice

The source for the game is McKinsey's State of AI survey: 88 percent of organizations now use AI in at least one function, yet only around 6 percent clear the bar for high performers, where AI drives meaningful bottom-line impact. The high performers are 2.8 times more likely to have fundamentally redesigned workflows (55 percent versus 20 percent), and of everything the survey measured, workflow redesign showed the strongest link to EBIT impact. The careers publication draws the managerial conclusion: mapping the team's processes and deciding where AI fits is becoming the manager's core job. Notice what the data is really saying: your team almost certainly uses AI already. The open question is whether anyone has designed how.

Section 02 · The Map

You can't redesign work you can't see.

Every workflow is a chain: something triggers it, steps run, handoffs happen, something ships. Most teams have never written theirs down, and the official version rarely matches what actually happens on a Monday. So the first managerial move has nothing to do with AI: map one workflow as it really runs. Once it's on paper, every step turns out to be one of four kinds.

1 · Gather

Collect the inputs

Pulling numbers from systems, rereading threads, assembling context. High volume, low judgment, and usually the step that eats the hours.

2 · Transform

Make the draft

Turning inputs into a first version: the written update, the summary, the formatted deck. Mechanical shaping, done to a standard someone else defined.

3 · Judge

Check it against standards

Is it accurate, is it good, is it safe to ship? Judging takes a human who knows the sources, the audience, and what wrong looks like.

4 · Commit

Decide and own it

Send, approve, spend, hire. The step where accountability lives. Whoever commits answers for the outcome, which is why this step behaves differently from all the others.

Go deeper

In practice

Three rules make a workable map. Map one real instance, following what actually happened last Monday rather than the official process doc; the two usually differ, and the redesign has to work on the real one. Keep it at five to nine steps; finer than that and you're documenting keystrokes, coarser and the draft candidates hide inside vague blobs like "prepare the report." And interview the person who actually does the work, because the grind is invisible from one level up. Managers consistently misjudge which step eats the hours, and the person doing it can tell you in one sentence.

On your own

Do this now: Write your workflow's steps as a numbered list and tag each one G, T, J, or C. Count how many are gather and transform; that number is the size of your redesign opportunity.

Section 03 · The Pattern

Draft. Verify. Decide.

Here is the pattern the whole session turns on. For every step on your map, make one assignment: AI drafts it, a human verifies it, or a human decides it. The assignment happens once, when you design the workflow, and then everyone knows who does what. That is the difference between a redesigned process and a pile of personal shortcuts.

AI drafts

The volume

Gather and transform steps: pulling, assembling, summarizing, first-pass writing. AI is fast, tireless, and confidently wrong just often enough that a draft is all it gets to be.

A human verifies

The check

A named person checks the draft against real sources and real standards, with the power to reject it. Verification is the hinge of the whole pattern; section 04 is entirely about it.

A human decides

The call

Judge and commit steps: what ships, what's spent, who's hired, what the client hears. Decisions carry accountability, and accountability doesn't delegate to a tool.

Play · Assign the step

Why this matters: the pattern is easy to recite and harder to apply. Five steps from real workflows; make the assignment.

The data rule still runs

The pattern decides where AI helps. The traffic light from AI Basics decides what it may see, and it applies to every draft step you assign:

GreenPublic or generic information. Go.
YellowInternal work. Approved VU tools only.
RedPrivate information about people. Never.

If a step touches red data, the draft assignment is off the table in unapproved tools, whatever the efficiency case. The light outranks the pattern.

Go deeper

In practice

Two questions sort almost every step. First, volume against stakes: high-volume, low-stakes work drafts freely; low-volume, high-stakes work usually isn't worth drafting at all. Second, the error question: if this step is wrong, who gets hurt, and how fast would we find out? Fast-caught, cheap errors are safe to draft; slow, expensive ones need a human earlier. And one design principle worth saying out loud: assignments attach to steps, never to people. Nobody's job becomes "watch the machine all day." A good redesign leaves every person with a mix of verifying, deciding, and the work only they can do.

On your own

Do this now: Assign every step of your own mapped workflow, then mark the one assignment you're least sure of. That step is your pilot's first design question.

Section 04 · The Verify

A check that can't fail isn't a check.

Verification is where redesigns quietly die. AI output arrives fluent and confident, week one's careful reader becomes week six's skimmer, and researchers have a name for the drift: automation bias, the documented human tendency to accept a system's output as attention fades. The fix is never "try harder." The fix is designing the check so it can actually fail.

The verifier's rubric

1. A named owner: one person verifies this step, and it's in their job, not their spare time. 2. Source-checked: claims are checked against the tracker, the data, the meeting notes, never against "sounds right." 3. A reject path with a log: when the check fails, the draft goes back, and the miss gets written down. A check that has never caught anything is evidence that nobody's looking, and the log is how you tell the difference.

Go deeper

In practice

Verification has a budget. If checking the draft costs as much as doing the work did, the step was never a draft candidate; send it back to a human and spend your verification budget where drafting actually pays. Sampling is the usual answer at volume: check three claims against source every time, rotate which three, and log every miss. The log turns verification from a vibe into a metric, because catch rate over time tells you whether the checks are working, whether the drafts are improving, and whether the verifier has gone to sleep. All three are things a manager can act on; "we trust it now" is not.

On your own

Do this now: Design the verify for the draft step of your own mapped workflow: owner, source, cadence, reject path. Four lines, written down.

In practice

Notice where the lab punishes you. Never for using AI too much or too little; always for the design around it: drafts with no real check, checks with no owner, and commits handed to automation. That mirrors the field data: when redesigns fail, the postmortem almost never says "the model wasn't good enough." It says a wrong thing flowed through a soft check and reached someone who mattered, and then the tool took the blame for the design. Week three is the classic failure window: late enough that vigilance has faded, early enough that the checks haven't been made structural yet.

Section 06 · The Team

Redesign is a team sport.

A redesign announced from above sounds like "the machine is coming for your work." The same redesign run as a pilot with the people who do the work sounds like "we're buying back your Monday mornings." McKinsey's high performers pair workflow change with visible leadership attention, and the practical corollary lands here: the line manager is where a redesign lives or dies. Your announcement line is the first design decision your team will actually hear.

The pilot standard

One workflow, two to four weeks. The doers design the checks, because they know where the process bites. A visible metric: hours saved and errors caught, posted where the team can see both. A real rollback, genuinely available without shame. An honest answer to what the saved hours are for, said out loud. If people suspect saved time converts into cut roles, they will make sure there is no saved time.

Play · Judge the announcement

Why this matters: the scenario is real: you're announcing the report pilot to your team on Monday. Five opening lines; call each one a good setup, fear or hype, or too vague to change anything.

Quick check: Why should the people who do the work design the verify step?

Go deeper

In practice

The trust math is simple and unforgiving. A pilot that names its workflow, its owner, its metric, and its review date can be believed, because every claim in it is checkable. An announcement built from "leverage," "efficiency," and "transformation" cannot, so people fill the vagueness with the scariest available story. And the review meeting is half the point: the team looks at the metric together and decides what happens next, which means the redesign's second iteration belongs to them. That is how one pilot becomes a habit instead of an initiative.

On your own

Do this now: Draft your announcement line, then test it: does it name the workflow, the owner, the metric, the review date, and what the saved hours are for?
