Your team adopts
what you model.

Gallup's workplace research keeps finding the same thing: the strongest predictors of a team actually using AI are the manager visibly championing it and the tools being integrated into real workflows. Tools and mandates barely move the needle. Accenture's leadership research adds the uncomfortable part: only about 18 percent of leaders are doing this well, and what separates them is curiosity, courage, and connection. This session makes you one of them, starting with an honest audit of your own team.

Section 01 · The Model

They watch what you do.

Most managers who use AI use it invisibly, and invisible use teaches the team nothing except that the subject is unspoken. Visible modeling is specific: narrate a real use in a real meeting, show the check that caught the error, and share a miss now and then. Gallup finds employees whose managers actively champion AI use it dramatically more, and are more engaged; a mandate without modeling mostly produces quiet resistance and hidden shortcuts.

Play · Model, mandate, or undermine?

Why this matters: the difference between modeling and mandating is easy to see in someone else's behavior first. Five leader moves; call each one.

The three C's, from the 18 percent

Accenture's research on leaders who actually deliver on AI names three differentiators. Curiosity: staying openly interested, asking "what did you try?" instead of pretending fluency. Courage: using it before certainty arrives, and letting the team see the misses. Connection: making adoption a team practice with trust in it, never a compliance program. Notice all three are visible behaviors; none of them is a tool decision.

Go deeper

In practice

The evidence base: Gallup's 2026 workplace research finds manager championing and integration with real workflows are the top drivers of frequent AI use, with big engagement gaps between teams whose managers actively guide AI use and teams left alone with the tools. Accenture's leadership research puts the share of organizations leading well at about 18 percent and names curiosity, courage, and connection as what separates them. The practical translation is small and weekly: one narrated use in a team meeting ("here's what I drafted, here's the number the check caught"), one show-your-prompts round a month, misses included. Fifteen minutes of visibility beats any policy memo you will ever forward.

Section 02 · The Guardrails

Make it safe to try.

People hold back from AI for a sensible reason: nobody told them what's safe. Three written guardrails remove the guesswork, and they fit on one page. What data goes in. When a human signs off. Who owns the result. Write them with the team, post them, and watch experimentation start.

Rail 1 · Data

What goes in

The traffic light, written where everyone can see it. Green: public or generic, go. Yellow: internal, approved VU tools only. Red: private information about people, never.

Rail 2 · Sign-off

When a human approves

Anything outbound, high-stakes, or about people gets human review before it moves. Internal drafts and personal productivity don't. Name the line so nobody has to guess it.

Rail 3 · Ownership

Who answers for it

You own what you ship, whoever drafted it. "The AI got it wrong" is not a sentence this team uses; a named human stands behind every output that leaves.

Play · Which rail is missing?

Why this matters: every AI incident on a team is a missing rail wearing a costume. Five situations; name the rail that would have prevented each.

Go deeper

In practice

Write the rails with the team, in thirty minutes, and keep them to one page: the light, the sign-off line ("outbound, high-stakes, or about people gets eyes"), and the ownership sentence ("you own what you ship"). Two additions earn their place fast. An amnesty: the shortcuts people already run get grandfathered in for redesign, never punished, because punishing early adopters teaches everyone to hide. And a revision date: the rails are version one, reviewed in a quarter, which tells the team the rules will learn as they do. Guardrails written this way read as permission, and permission is what most teams are actually waiting for.

On your own

Do this now: Draft all three rails for your team in three sentences. That's the artifact your capstone move posts on the wall.

Section 03 · The Audit

Your team's AI reality, scored.

Before you can lead adoption, you need to know where your team actually stands, and most managers are guessing. This audit is five questions, answered honestly, scored live. It measures the things the research says matter: where AI shows up in the work, how visibly you model it, whether guardrails exist, and whether AI has entered your performance and coaching conversations. Nothing you enter is saved or sent anywhere.

Practice · The AI-enabled work audit

Why this matters: the audit turns "we should do more with AI" into a scored snapshot and a first move. Answer for your real team, current state, not aspirations.

2 · Where does AI show up in that work today?
3 · When did your team last SEE you use AI?
4 · The guardrails: data, sign-off, ownership?
5 · Is AI part of your performance and coaching conversations?
Answer all five
Go deeper

In practice

The audit's five dimensions come straight from the research: repeated outputs are where workflow integration pays (Gallup's second predictor), visible modeling is the first predictor, guardrails are what make trying safe, and AI in coaching conversations is how adoption becomes a skill you develop in people rather than a tool you issued once. Run it again with your team in the room, that's the braver version, and let them score you on question 3. The gap between your answer and theirs is the most useful number the audit produces. Repeat quarterly; rising scores are your adoption metric, and they cost nothing to collect.
