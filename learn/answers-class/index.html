Why this is worth your time.

Think about the last time you needed one number out of a forty-page PDF, or a quick comparison that turned into ninety minutes of open tabs. The one method this course teaches. The method / 02

Ask, anchor, check.

Here is the whole method. When you need an answer, you do three things in order: brief the question so the AI knows what you actually need, anchor it to a real source, then check the result before it goes anywhere. The next three pages show it working on real tasks.

01 · Ask

Brief the question

Tell the AI what you need to know the way you’d brief a colleague. CRIT gives you the four parts: Context (what you already know and why you’re asking), Role (who it should answer as), Interview (tell it to ask you questions before it answers), and Task (exactly what you want back, including format).

ContextRoleInterviewTask

The Interview line earns its keep here too. Writing “ask me up to three questions before you answer” means the AI fills its gaps by asking you instead of guessing.

4parts to every brief

The four-part brief comes from Geoff Woods; the Working Smarter course teaches it in full.

Geoff Woods, The AI-Driven Leader
02 · Anchor

Give it the source

An AI answering from memory is reciting patterns from its training data. Paste the document, name the site, or attach the file, so the answer comes from something real. Then ask for a page or section reference with every claim, so there is something to check.

58%+error rate, no source given

Chatbots answering questions about court cases from memory alone invented authority on 58% to 88% of them4.

Dahl et al., Journal of Legal Analysis (2024)
03 · Check

Verify before you use

Open the cited page. Confirm the number. The model answers confidently even when it’s wrong, and a wrong answer reads exactly like a right one. Checking one claim takes a minute; repeating a wrong one can cost far more.

1 in 6still wrong, sources attached

Purpose-built research tools that search real documents still hallucinated on roughly 17% to 33% of test queries5.

Magesh et al., J. Empirical Legal Studies (2025)

The AI does the finding, and you do the confirming before anything gets used.

Together

Turn to a partner and explain Ask, Anchor, Check back to each other in under a minute. Explaining it out loud is the fastest way to make it stick.

Worked example 1 of 3: the long document. Example / 03 · The document

Getting one answer out of a long policy.

The situation

A member of your team has lost a parent and asked you how much leave they can take. The answer is in the HR policy PDF, somewhere. You need the number of days and who counts as immediate family, and you need to be right, because someone grieving is waiting on your answer.

Before you paste anything: safe data only. A real internal policy belongs in Amplify or Copilot through your Vanderbilt account, never a personal one. When in doubt, use Amplify.

The prompt · CRIT shape 
Context: Pasted below is our leave policy. A member of my team has lost a parent and asked me how much bereavement leave they can take.
Role: You are an HR generalist who answers policy questions precisely and quotes the policy text.
Interview: Ask me up to three questions before you answer.
Task: Tell me how many bereavement days apply here and who the policy counts as immediate family. Cite the section number for every claim and quote the exact sentence you relied on.

Each example recommends a different tool, so by the end you’ll have tried all three.

What comes back

A clean answer with section citations: the number of days, the definition, the quoted lines you asked for. It can still misread a cross-reference or blend two sections together, and it will sound just as sure either way.

Your check pass
  • Open the sections it cites and read them yourself. A citation you haven’t opened is a claim, not a fact.
  • Confirm the number and the definition against the quoted text. Definitions often live in a different section than the rule that uses them.
  • Look for conditions the answer skipped, like travel days, eligibility rules, or deadlines. The person asking will hit them even if the summary didn’t.

Worked example 2 of 3: the quick research brief. Example / 03 · The web brief

Comparing three tools on the open web.

The situation

Your office of nine people has outgrown the shared spreadsheet it uses to book conference rooms and cover the front desk. You’ve been asked to recommend a scheduling tool by Friday. Nothing about this is sensitive; you just need a fair comparison that doesn’t eat an afternoon.

The prompt · CRIT shape 
Context: I run scheduling for a nine-person office: conference rooms, front-desk coverage, and the occasional visiting speaker. We use a shared spreadsheet today and it is failing us.
Role: You are a practical operations consultant who compares software without hype.
Interview: Ask me up to three questions before you answer.
Task: Compare three widely used scheduling tools for a small office. Give me a short table (cost, learning curve, calendar integration), one paragraph per tool, and a link to each vendor's pricing page. Tell me when your information is from.
Try it in
ChatGPT EDU Recommended Public web content and nothing internal: exactly what everyday, non-sensitive research is for. Amplify Also fine Vanderbilt’s own platform, and the right home the moment anything internal enters the thread. Copilot Also fine Handy if the recommendation will end up in an Outlook email anyway.
What comes back

A tidy comparison table with confident pricing. Prices and features change fast, and models remember old pages, so treat every number as a lead, not a fact, until you’ve seen it on the vendor’s page.

Your check pass
  • Open at least two of the links it gives you and confirm the pricing yourself. A plan tier from last year may not exist anymore.
  • Check when the information is from. The prompt asks for it; if the answer dodges, ask again before you rely on anything.
  • Add the one constraint only you know, like the badge system the front desk already uses. That constraint usually decides the comparison.
Worked example 3 of 3: the synthesis. Example / 03 · The synthesis

Three sets of notes into one update.

The situation

Your project has met three times in three weeks, and the sponsor wants a half-page status update by the end of the day. Everything you need is in the meeting notes; the job is pulling the decisions out of the chatter and putting them first.

Before you paste anything: meeting notes are internal content. They belong in Copilot or Amplify through your Vanderbilt account. When in doubt, use Amplify.

The prompt · CRIT shape 
Context: Pasted below are notes from our project's last three meetings. Our sponsor wants a status update and reads the first three lines of anything.
Role: You are a project coordinator who writes updates executives actually read.
Interview: Ask me up to three questions before you answer.
Task: Write a half-page status update. Decisions first, then progress, then open risks with an owner for each. Keep every number exactly as it appears in the notes.

No notes of your own handy? Use the practice notes below; they’re fictional, so they’re safe to paste anywhere.

What comes back

A smooth update in exactly the shape you asked for. Synthesis is where numbers drift: a date from one meeting attaches itself to a decision from another, and nothing in the phrasing warns you.

Your check pass
  • Check every number against the notes: dates, dollar figures, deadlines. Drift is silent, so this check is line by line.
  • Confirm each decision was actually decided rather than merely discussed. Models promote proposals into decisions all the time.
  • Read it as the sponsor. Does the first line answer “are we on track?” If not, move the line that does.

The rules that keep the method safe. Guardrails / 04

The four rules.

The method only works if you follow these. They take thirty seconds to learn.

Rule 01
Safe data only

Sensitive or internal Vanderbilt business belongs in Amplify or Copilot through your Vanderbilt account, never a personal account. When in doubt, use Amplify.

Rule 02
Open the source before you repeat the claim

Models fabricate citations that look completely real. Two lawyers were sanctioned in 2023 for filing a brief built on six court cases an AI invented6. If you didn’t open it, you don’t know it exists.

Rule 03
Dates matter

Models have training cutoffs, and web pages go stale. Ask when the information is from, and check the date on any page a decision rests on.

Rule 04
You own what you pass along

The tool found it, but you forwarded it. Pass an answer along as if your name is attached to it, because it is.

Three quick questions to check it stuck.

Three questions, none of them tricks. Knowledge check / 06

Did it stick?

Pick an answer on each; you’ll see right away how you did. Nothing here is scored or sent anywhere. If the LMS version of this course includes a graded check, it replaces this one.

1. Two briefs for the same question. Which one gets the better answer?

The second one. It anchors the AI to the actual policy, gives it a role, adds the Interview line, and demands section citations, which is what makes the check pass possible.

2. A colleague pastes a confidential internal report into a personal AI account to pull numbers out of it. What went wrong?

Rule one: safe data only. Logging out doesn’t retrieve data pasted into an unapproved tool, and no prompt improvement fixes a data problem.

3. The AI gives you a persuasive answer with no source attached. What’s your next move?

Asking a model whether it’s sure just buys you more confidence, not more accuracy. An answer you can’t trace is a lead, not a fact: anchor it to a source you’ve opened, or don’t pass it along.
