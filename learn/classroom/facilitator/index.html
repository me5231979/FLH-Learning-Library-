    The tough-questions bank

    Say these answers out loud once

    Four questions come up in almost every room. The course answers each of them in depth; these are the thirty-second versions for when a hand goes up early.

    “Will this take my job?”
    AI meets a role task by task, not job by job. The readiness section turns that exact worry into a task list and a 30/60/90 development plan you own, and the program’s whole stance is on the second page: amplify humans, never replace them.
    “Can I trust what it says?”

    In this course, yes: nothing anyone types is stored or sent anywhere. In the real tools it is a routing question: ChatGPT EDU for everyday non-sensitive work, Amplify or Copilot for anything internal, and the never-paste list is the hard edge. When in doubt, Amplify.
    “Why can’t I just use my personal ChatGPT?”
    Because no Vanderbilt agreement covers that account, so anything pasted there is unprotected. ChatGPT EDU is the same product under Vanderbilt’s agreement; use it for anything work-related instead.

    Module A

    The five terms

    In this activity, you will flip five cards: a plain definition and a Vanderbilt example for each term. (Click or press Enter.)

    You’ll learnWhat AI, ML, GenAI, LLMs (large language models), and agents really are You’ll doFlip five cards Time3 min
    The idea

    Five terms; each one is a more specific kind of the one before it. AI is the umbrella: any software doing things that used to need human judgment. Machine learning is how modern AI gets built: instead of a programmer writing rules, the system finds patterns in thousands of examples. Generative AI is machine learning that produces new things (text, images, code) instead of just sorting or scoring. And a large language model is the specific generative engine inside ChatGPT, Amplify, and Copilot. The newest of the five is agentic AI: an LLM handed tools and a loop so it can carry out multi-step work on its own. It runs on the same engine as the chatbots, so it inherits the same limits.

    Notice as you flip: none of these systems understands meaning the way you do; they find patterns. We’ll keep coming back to that one fact.

    Module B

    How an LLM actually works

    Every AI answer you will ever read is built one guess at a time: the model predicts the next word, over and over. In this module, you will watch the best visual explainer there is, then try the prediction engine yourself on the next page. You leave knowing why AI drafts so well and still needs checking.

    You’ll learnWhy AI writes so well and lies so confidently You’ll doWatch the explainer, then try the interactive demo Time3 min + optional 8-min video
    The idea

    The model learned one thing from a huge slice of human writing: which word tends to come next. It writes by predicting that word, over and over. It never checks anything; there is no lookup step, only pattern completion (unless the tool explicitly searches the web). That is also why it sometimes states made-up things as fact: a made-up detail can be the most likely-sounding next words, and the model has no way to tell the difference.

    The research

    453 professionals, randomized: with ChatGPT they finished 40% faster, with quality rated 18% higher. The machine in the video, and in the demo on the next page, is the one behind those numbers.

    Noy & Zhang, “Experimental evidence on the productivity effects of generative artificial intelligence,” Science, 2023

    Try it: the next-word demo

    This demo shows the single most important idea in the course: AI writes by predicting the next word, one word at a time. Watch it predict, and you will understand why AI can sound confident and still be wrong. Here is how to use it:

    1. Read the sentence. The gold underlined word is the one the AI picked to fill the blank.
    2. The bars below show the five words the AI considered, and how likely each one felt to it. Higher bar, more likely pick.
    3. Slide the temperature, the AI’s randomness dial. Low keeps it picking the safest word; high gives unlikely words a real chance. Watch the percentages change.
    4. Select “Shuffle sentence” to try another example.

    The demo runs on pre-set example data; nothing is generated live.

    “The staff meeting has been moved to ?”

    The five words it considered

    Each bar shows how likely that word felt to the AI. The gold word in the sentence is the winner.

    safer / predictableriskier / more creative

    Prediction. What you are watching: the AI scores every word it knows and usually picks a high scorer. No plan, no meaning, just statistics.

    Temperature. The slider you just moved: low keeps the odds stacked on the safe pick; high makes the output more random and creative. Useful for brainstorming, risky for facts.

    The catch: hallucinations. A made-up detail stated as fact. The same scoring you just watched predicts what sounds right, not what is right, so always verify.

    Your role, task by task.

    In this section, you will look at a role the way AI actually meets it: task by task.

    The idea

    AI does not show up for a job title; it shows up for a task. Some of your tasks sit in AI’s wheelhouse and others barely register, so the task list is the right unit to analyze. One rule underneath everything here: exposure, how much of a task AI could plausibly help with, is a signal for your development, not a threat to your role, and nothing here treats it that way.

    Module A

    How AI meets your work

    In this activity, you will read four short ideas. The assessment that follows uses them to look at your own task list.

    You’ll learnExposure, the jagged frontier, and how fast skills change You’ll doRead four ideas and the evidence under each Time3 min

    01AI exposes tasks, not jobs

    AI affects some parts of a job far more than others. It might draft most of your weekly report, yet contribute almost nothing to a vendor negotiation. The worry “will AI change my job?” is real, and it’s worth taking seriously. A more useful version of it is “which of my tasks can AI help with?” The first invites worry; the second gives you a list you can act on.

    The evidence

    About 80 percent of the US workforce could see at least 10 percent of their tasks affected by large language models, and about 19 percent could see half or more affected. The team rated occupations task by task: exposure lives in the task list, not the job title.

    Source: Eloundou, Manning, Mishkin, and Rock (2023), “GPTs are GPTs,” later published in Science, arxiv.org/abs/2303.10130.

    02Exposure is not elimination

    An exposed task is a task AI can help with, not a task that disappears. The practical question for each one: automate it with oversight, or redesign it as human plus AI?

    The evidence

    Real usage splits about 57 percent augmentation (AI helping a person with a task) to 43 percent automation (AI doing the task outright). Most exposed work is being reshaped with a person in the loop, not removed.

    Source: Anthropic Economic Index (2025), anthropic.com/news/the-anthropic-economic-index.
    Module A · continued

    The frontier & the horizon

    Ideas 03 and 04, then the four steps that turn all four into a plan.

    03The frontier is jagged

    AI is strong on some tasks and quietly bad at adjacent ones. The only way to find the line for your work is to test task by task, which is why verification matters as much as prompting.

    The evidence

    Inside the frontier, consultants with AI finished 12.2 percent more tasks, worked 25.1 percent faster, and produced over 40 percent higher quality. On a task outside the frontier, they were 19 percentage points more likely to get it wrong. A field experiment with 758 BCG consultants: same people, same tool, opposite results, depending on the task.

    Source: Dell’Acqua, McFowland, Mollick, Lifshitz-Assaf, Kellogg, Rajendran, Krayer, Candelon, and Lakhani (2023), Harvard Business School working paper 24-013, papers.ssrn.com. Ethan Mollick, whose work grounds this platform’s research base, is a co-author.

    04Skills churn is the planning horizon

    Skills churn means how fast the skills a role needs are changing. When they change fast, there is a right order to respond: upskill first (deepen people’s current skills), reskill where tasks shift (train for the new tasks), redesign roles where the shift is large, and only then hire.

    The evidence

    Employers expect 39 percent of workers’ core skills to change by 2030. Skills churn, not headcount, is the planning horizon.

    Source: World Economic Forum, Future of Jobs Report 2025, weforum.org.
    The four steps
    1. Inventory the tasks. Write down what your week actually contains; the list, not the job description, is the raw material.
    2. Sort into three buckets. Delegate with oversight, redesign together, or human-core, the work only a person should do. Every task goes in exactly one.
    3. Name the skills. Each bucket demands something: review habits for delegated work, fluency for redesigned work, depth for the human core.
    4. Turn it into action. A development plan for yourself, or per person if you manage the role. The task list only helps if you talk about it: bring it to your manager or team.

    Development plans are yours to run. Role redesign, reclassification, and staffing changes run through Human Resources.

    You are here: how to talk to it. CRIT / 03

    The CRIT prompt framework.

    In this section, you will learn CRIT, the four-part way to talk to AI: Context, Role, Interview, Task. CRIT is an outside framework, created by Geoff Woods, author of The AI-Driven Leader, and adopted here; the source page ahead has the full provenance. By the end, you will have built and copied a CRIT prompt of your own.

    You’ll learnThe four parts of CRIT, and why the Interview changes the result You’ll doWatch the creator walk it through, then build one in the builder Time4 min + video (optional)
    The idea

    Prompting is not magic: a prompt is instructions handed to a word-probability engine, and no four-letter formula is a spell. What a framework buys you is discipline; what sets CRIT apart is the Interview, which flips the roles so the model asks and you stay the thought leader, the one doing the thinking. No single formula is magic; structure and iteration are what matter.

    C · Context
    What is going on

    The background, the situation, and the constraints, so the model is not guessing in the dark.

    R · Role
    Whose perspective

    The professional lens you want the answer from: an editor, an analyst, a challenger.

    I · Interview
    What sets CRIT apart

    Tell it to ask you clarifying questions first and wait, so it shows you its blind spots instead of papering over them.

    T · Task
    The deliverable

    What type of output, what scope, what format: the model can only hit a target you name.

    The Interview step earns its keep fast: use it the next time your request would take more than three sentences to explain.

    Together

    Turn to a partner and explain the four CRIT parts back to each other in under a minute, without looking at the screen. If either of you stalls on Interview, that is the part to reread, because it is the part that sets CRIT apart.

    Better answers

    Three ways to get a better answer

    In this module, you will pick up three things you can add to any prompt to get a better answer back. AI’s first answer is rarely its best; these additions raise it. Copy an example, paste it into your AI tool, and compare what comes back. You leave with three add-ons that work on any prompt.

    The idea

    CRIT gets your request right. These three additions improve what comes back: show the AI an example of what good looks like, ask it to point out weaknesses, and ask for another version.

    1

    Show it an example

    AI copies patterns. Give it one or two examples of what good looks like and it will match them.

    Here are two reminder emails our office liked: “Open enrollment closes Friday at 5:00. Ten minutes now saves a phone call later.” and “Parking permits renew next week. It takes two clicks.” Write a reminder in the same style about the staff training deadline on March 14.
    2

    Ask it to find the weak spots

    It is a sharp editor, especially of its own work.

    Before you finish this meeting summary, list three places a staff member skimming it could miss something important. Then revise it to fix all three.
    3

    Ask for another version

    Treat the first answer as a draft. Say what to change and ask again.

    Good start on this announcement. Now make it half as long, warmer, and end with one clear next step.

    Next time a first answer comes back flat, use the third addition: say what to change and ask again.

    Using AI responsibly.

    In this section, you will learn six habits that keep you and Vanderbilt on solid ground, then test your judgment on four scenarios. By the end, you will know the never-paste list.

    You’ll learnSix guardrail habits and the never-paste list You’ll doWork through four workplace scenarios Time7 min
    The idea

    The model predicts rather than checks, so you verify, disclose, and keep a human on every decision. What you paste can leave Vanderbilt’s control, so anything sensitive goes to an approved tool, Amplify or Copilot, or nowhere.

    Verify outputs

    AI writes confidently even when it's wrong. Check names, numbers, dates, and citations before anything leaves your desk.

    Protect data

    FERPA and HIPAA don't pause for convenience. Personnel records, health info, and anything confidential go only to approved tools, Amplify or Copilot on your Vanderbilt account, or nowhere.

    Disclose AI use

    If AI helped draft something substantive, say so. Simple transparency ("drafted with AI assistance, reviewed by me") builds trust.

    Human makes the call

    AI advises; you decide. Hiring, promotion, funding, discipline: decisions about people always need a person accountable for them.

    Bias awareness

    Models learn from human writing, biases included. Watch for skewed assumptions, especially in anything touching hiring, promotion, or evaluation.

    Keep learning

    These tools change monthly. A little steady curiosity keeps your skills compounding while everyone else is still skeptical.

    Practice

    Four judgment scenarios

    These are four workplace situations with no obvious right answer; versions of them will reach you before any policy does. Read each one. Pick the response closest to what you would really do. Compare it with our take. You leave with a feel for where the lines are, before the stakes are real.

    The idea

    AI moves faster than any handbook, so the gray areas land on regular staff first. Practicing on low-stakes scenarios beats improvising on real ones. The safe-looking middle option is not automatically the right one.

    Scenario 01

    The confident citation

    A colleague asks you to look over a report before it goes to their director. It reads beautifully (clearly AI-assisted), but one citation is to a study you’re fairly sure doesn’t exist.

    Our take: the report can’t go out with a fabricated source; that part isn’t negotiable. But start with the colleague: this is almost always ignorance, not fraud. Escalate only if it doesn’t get fixed.

    The takeaway: fix it privately first; a made-up source never ships under Vanderbilt’s name.

    Principle: Accountability & integrity
    Scenario 02

    The medical-leave email

    You’re drowning in email. A long message arrives from an employee on your team describing the health situation behind their upcoming medical leave. You’re tempted to paste it into a public AI tool for a quick summary.

    Our take: this is protected health and personnel information, exactly the territory laws like HIPAA exist for. If AI touches it at all, only in an approved tool: Amplify, or Copilot on your Vanderbilt account. And for a short, personal, high-stakes message, your own attention might be the whole point.

    The takeaway: health details never go into a public tool; use an approved tool, or your own eyes.

    Principle: Human dignity & data protection
    Scenario 03

    The name test

    Your unit pilots an AI tool that pre-screens job applications. You run a quiet test: two nearly identical resumes, different names, and they come back with noticeably different scores.

    Our take: models inherit bias from the data they learned on. Document it, report it, and insist a human owns every hiring decision. This is exactly where “the AI ranked them” fails everyone.

    The takeaway: document the bias and raise it; never trust the tool blindly and never patch it quietly.

    Principle: Bias & fairness
    Scenario 04

    The grant question

    AI helped you substantially draft a grant proposal: structure, phrasing, a first pass at the narrative. The ideas and data are yours. The sponsor’s forms don’t ask about AI use. Say anything?

    Our take: where the rules are unsettled, default to the disclosure you’d be comfortable defending in two years. University trust is slow to earn and quick to lose, and norms are moving toward disclosure, not away from it.

    The takeaway: when disclosure is unsettled, disclose briefly; one honest sentence is cheap insurance.

    Principle: Transparency & integrity

    What you now know.

    In this section, you will revisit the seven ideas you picked up today.

    How the machine writes

    An LLM predicts the likeliest next word, over and over. There is no lookup step and no database behind the answer.

    At work: draft with it freely, and never treat it as a source of record.

    Where it breaks

    Its abilities form a jagged frontier: it aces tasks you would bet against, flubs tasks you would bet on, and sounds equally confident on both.

    At work: verify names, numbers, dates, and citations before anything ships.

    Tasks, not jobs

    AI meets a role task by task: delegate the routine with oversight, redesign the middle together, and invest in the human core. Exposure is a development signal, never a judgment of the person.

    At work: one task inventory turns AI anxiety into a development plan.

    How to steer it

    CRIT (Context, Role, Interview, Task) turns a vague request into a brief. What sets it apart: tell the model to ask clarifying questions first, and wait. You stay the thought leader.

    At work: prompts come back needing fewer rewrites, and the questions it asks up front are the gaps you would otherwise find while checking.

    Where the work belongs

    Tool choice is a data decision first: Amplify and Copilot are approved up to Level 3, ChatGPT EDU up to Level 2; Office work points to Copilot; when in doubt, Amplify.

    At work: one second of routing prevents the mistakes that are hardest to undo.

    Staying in charge

    You own every output, you disclose real AI help, and you decide where the saved time goes instead of letting new tasks swallow it.

    At work: judgment and boundaries are what make the new speed sustainable.

    What comes next

    Agents take goals instead of questions and work in steps under your oversight, on the same engine and with the same limits.

    At work: the skill they reward is the one you practiced here, clear and complete delegation.

    Go deeper: two ways people pair with AI

    The centaur divides the work with clean handoffs: you keep the strategy, the judgment calls, and anything with a name on it; AI gets the drafting, summarizing, and reformatting, with a clear line between the two. The cyborg weaves it together: drafts bouncing between you and the machine, every paragraph a little of both. Faster and more fluid, but it demands sharper verification because the seams disappear.

    Both styles come from Ethan Mollick’s essay on the 758-consultant experiment from the Limits module, and both beat working alone. Our suggestion: start centaur, because clean handoffs are easier to check, then grow cyborg as you learn the frontier. This platform’s stance is borrowed from his book Co-Intelligence: treat AI like a tireless, overconfident colleague, and always check its work.

    Source: Ethan Mollick, “Centaurs and Cyborgs on the Jagged Frontier” (One Useful Thing, 2023) · Co-Intelligence: Living and Working with AI (2024).
