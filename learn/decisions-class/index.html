Decisions, sharper.

Widen the view before the call: test scenarios, surface hidden assumptions, and decide with more confidence.

If your job includes making the calls other people wait on (schedules, budgets, coverage, a plan to pitch), this course is for you. You’ll learn a simple way to use AI to widen what you can see before you decide: test the scenarios, run a premortem, and hear the case against your favorite option, while the decision stays yours. It takes about fifteen minutes, and you’ll finish by laying out a real decision you own. In Deloitte’s latest survey of 3,235 leaders, 60% named better decision-making among the benefits they’re seeing from AI1.

The case for learning this, in two studies and one piece of advice. The evidence / 01

Why this is worth your time.

Think about the last decision you sat on for a week. Not because the choice was hard to say out loud, but because you couldn’t be sure what you were missing. Researchers have started measuring what happens when people bring AI into that moment, and the pattern is consistent: used as an extra set of eyes, it widens the thinking, and the deciding stays human. Here is the case, in two studies and one piece of advice from Stanford.

Global survey · Deloitte 60%

In Deloitte’s State of AI in the Enterprise survey of 3,235 leaders across 24 countries, 60% report enhanced decision-making and data-driven insights among the benefits their organization has achieved with AI. Only improved efficiency ranks higher.

Deloitte, State of AI in the Enterprise (2026)1
Leadership framing · Stanford GSB “You’re in charge”

Stanford GSB’s advice to leaders is in the title: you’re in charge. Judge AI by the job it does in a decision (supplying information and analysis so your call is better informed), and design that job yourself. The call never moves to the machine.

Stanford GSB Insights, “You’re in Charge” (2024)2
Field experiment · Harvard / BCG +40%

In a field experiment with 758 consultants, people working with AI on tasks it handles well finished 12.2% more tasks, 25.1% faster, at more than 40% higher quality. The same study warns the frontier is jagged: on work beyond the AI’s reach, leaning on it hurts, which is why your judgment stays in the loop.

Dell’Acqua et al., HBS Working Paper (2023)3

The one method this course teaches. The method / 02

Widen, weigh, decide.

Here is the whole method. When a real decision lands on you, do three things in order: lay it out for the AI so it can widen what you see, weigh it by testing scenarios and assumptions, then make the call yourself and write down why. The next three pages show it working on real decisions.

01 · Widen

Lay the decision out

Brief the AI the way you’d brief a trusted advisor, using CRIT: Context (the situation, with every person described by role, never by name), Role (who should advise you), Interview (have it ask before it answers), and Task (what you want back).

ContextRoleInterviewTask

Load the Context with what a skeptic would say about your favorite option and what information you don’t have. Then let the Interview line work: “ask me about anything I have not considered.”

4parts to every brief

The four-part brief comes from Geoff Woods; the Working Smarter course teaches it in full.

Geoff Woods, The AI-Driven Leader
02 · Weigh

Test it before it’s real

Ask for the best case, the expected case, and the worst case, and what would have to be true for each. Run a premortem: tell it that six months have passed, the decision failed, and you want the honest retrospective. Then ask for second-order effects, and who is affected that is not in the room.

30%more causes named

Imagining an event has already happened raises people’s ability to correctly identify reasons for future outcomes by 30%. That is the premortem’s whole trick4.

Klein, Harvard Business Review (2007)
03 · Decide

The call is yours

Design the AI’s role for each decision: it informs, it can recommend, and it never decides. Then make the call, write down your reasoning while it is fresh, and disclose substantive AI help to the people who rely on the decision.

Worked example 1 of 3: the conflicting recommendations. Example / 03 · Recommendations

Two trusted leads, two opposite answers.

The situation

Two team leads have each recommended a scheduling system for the same front desk, and they point in opposite directions. Both are careful people, both wrote solid one-pagers, and both are waiting on you. Picking one by gut feel means telling the other that careful homework lost a coin flip.

Together

Do this one together: the instructor lays the disagreement out live, and the room supplies the questions for each lead.

Pause + reflect

Try it now: copy this prompt into Amplify and run it. Then compare what comes back with the judgment pass below.

The prompt · CRIT shape 
Context: Two experienced team leads who run the same service point have recommended opposite scheduling systems. One argues for a fixed-block schedule that gives staff predictable weeks. The other argues for a demand-based schedule that follows our busiest hours. Both cite coverage gaps this term. I supervise both leads and I owe them a decision.
Role: You are a neutral operations advisor who has seen both approaches succeed and fail.
Interview: Before you answer, ask me about anything I have not considered.
Task: Map where the two recommendations actually agree, where they truly differ, and which facts would settle each difference. Then give me three questions to ask each lead. Do not pick a winner.
Try it in
Amplify Recommended The prompt carries internal operational detail and two colleagues’ positions. Vanderbilt’s own platform is the right home, and the when-in-doubt choice. Copilot Also fine Also approved for internal operational detail through your Vanderbilt account. ChatGPT EDU Sits this one out Internal operations stay in Amplify or Copilot, and this prompt describes staffing at your own service point.

The recommendation follows the data in the prompt, not habit. Two of these three examples stay inside Vanderbilt tools on purpose.

What comes back

A tidy map of the disagreement that treats both memos evenly, plus questions you may not have thought to ask. It does not know your people, your busiest Tuesday, or the history between these two leads. It found the shape of the disagreement; only you know the room it lives in.

Your judgment pass
  • Check the map against the memos. If it lists an agreement neither lead actually wrote, cut it; a clean-looking summary can smooth over the real dispute.
  • Take the three questions to each lead before you decide. The goal is a better-informed call, not a faster one.
  • Chase the settling facts first. If one fact would settle the biggest difference, go get that fact before anything else.
Worked example 2 of 3: the scenario test. Example / 03 · Scenario test

Changing the coverage hours before you commit.

The situation

Your service desk closes at five, and the requests to extend it keep coming. You’re weighing coverage until seven, two evenings a week. The change touches student workers’ schedules, staff rotations, and a budget line you would have to defend. Once announced, it is very hard to un-announce.

Together

Run this one as a room: the instructor takes real constraints from the floor and runs the prompt live. Tables split the output (best case, worst case, the premortem) and each reports one finding back.

Pause + reflect

Try it now: copy the prompt, swap in a change you’re actually weighing, and run it. Read the premortem twice; it’s the part managers skip and shouldn’t.

The prompt · CRIT shape 
Context: I manage a service desk that closes at 5 p.m. I am weighing extended coverage to 7 p.m. two evenings a week. The desk is staffed by student workers and two full-time staff members, described here by role only. Demand after 5 p.m. is real but not well measured. The budget for the extra hours would need my director's approval.
Role: You are an experienced service operations manager who has run coverage changes like this one.
Interview: Before you answer, ask me about anything I have not considered.
Task: Give me the best case, the expected case, and the worst case for this change, and what would have to be true for each. Then run a premortem: it is six months later and the extended hours failed; write the honest retrospective. Finish with the second-order effects on the students and staff affected, including anyone not in the room for this decision.
Try it in
Amplify Recommended Staffing and budget detail about your own operation. Vanderbilt’s platform is the right home, and the when-in-doubt choice. Copilot Also fine Also approved for internal work through your Vanderbilt account, and handy if the staffing plan already lives in Excel. ChatGPT EDU Sits this one out Internal operations stay in Amplify or Copilot, and this prompt carries staffing and budget detail.
What comes back

Three plausible futures, a retrospective that reads uncomfortably well, and at least one effect you had not priced in, like what evening shifts do to student workers’ course schedules. Some of it will be generic. The point is not that it’s right. The point is that your view of the decision just got wider.

Your judgment pass
  • Score each scenario against what you actually know. The AI guessed at demand; you have the door counts, the emails, and the complaints.
  • Treat the premortem as a to-check list. Every failure reason you can’t rule out becomes a question to answer before you commit.
  • Walk the second-order effects to real roles. Which student worker loses hours, who covers the late shift. If you can’t name the role, the thinking isn’t finished.
Worked example 3 of 3: the assumptions check. Example / 03 · Assumptions

Stress-testing the plan before the director does.

The situation

You’re pitching a budget request to your director next week: new equipment, a modest staffing change, savings that show up in year two. The plan is solid in your head. That is exactly the problem, because your head is where the untested assumptions live.

Before you paste anything: the plan carries internal figures, so it stays in Copilot or Amplify through your Vanderbilt account. ChatGPT EDU is fine only if the plan contains nothing internal, and a budget plan almost always does.

The prompt · CRIT shape 
Context: Pasted below is my draft budget request. I present it to my director next week. I want the weaknesses found now, in private, not in the meeting.
Role: You are a skeptical budget director who has heard a hundred pitches and approved twelve.
Interview: Before you answer, ask me about anything I have not considered.
Task: List every assumption this plan makes, stated or unstated. Rank them from most fragile to most solid, and say what evidence would firm up the top three. Then write the three hardest questions my director will ask, and name the parts of the plan they will probe.

What comes back

A ranked list that usually includes two or three assumptions you did not know you were making, such as year-two savings assuming current volumes hold. The hardest questions will sting a little. Better here than in the meeting.

Your judgment pass
  • Answer the three hardest questions in writing. If one has no good answer yet, that’s your homework, not a reason to soften the question.
  • Re-rank the fragile assumptions against real numbers. The AI ranked them by how they read; you rank them by what you know.
  • Fold the fixes into the plan and note what changed. Your director gets the stress-tested version, and your record shows the reasoning.

The rules that keep the method safe. Guardrails / 04

The four rules.

Decisions touch people, money, and records, so this course carries the strictest guardrails in the program. The first rule is the one that matters most.

Rule 01
Never personal or sensitive data

No Social Security numbers, no health information, no performance records, and no names in people decisions. Describe situations as roles, never names. Personnel matters go to HR and your HR business partner, not a chatbot. When in doubt, Amplify. When it’s about a person, HR.

Rule 02
It informs or recommends; it never decides

You design the AI’s role for each decision: it can inform your view or recommend options, and the call stays with you. Stanford GSB’s advice to leaders says it in the title: you’re in charge2.

Rule 03
Interrogate confident answers

Ask what’s missing, and check any factual claim before it moves your decision. The model can argue any side persuasively; confidence is its writing style, not its accuracy.

Rule 04
Own it and show your work

The decision and its consequences are yours. Keep your reasoning in the record, and disclose substantive AI help to the people who rely on the call.

Three quick questions to check it stuck.

Three questions, none of them tricks. Knowledge check / 06

Did it stick?

Pick an answer on each; you’ll see right away how you did. Nothing here is scored or sent anywhere. If the LMS version of this course includes a graded check, it replaces this one.

1. Two briefs for the same decision. Which one gets the more useful answer?

The second. It widens the view instead of outsourcing the call: the AI maps the disagreement and asks about what you missed, and the decision stays with you.

2. A manager pastes a team member’s performance review into an AI tool to help think through a role change. What went wrong?

Rule one, the strictest in the program. Approved tools are for operational detail, not personal records, and no prompt fix makes a performance review safe to paste. Take the matter to HR, and if you use AI to think about the situation at all, describe it as a role, never a name.

3. You asked for options and the AI strongly recommends option A. What’s your next move?

The model argues any side persuasively, so a strong case for A tells you little until you have heard the strongest case against it. Get the counter-case and the facts that would change the answer. Then the call is yours to make, and yours to own.
