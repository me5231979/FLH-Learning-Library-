First drafts, faster.

Save time and be more efficient in writing and editing communications.

If your job includes writing emails, announcements, or summaries, this course is for you. You’ll learn a simple way to have AI write your first draft, so your time goes into improving a draft instead of staring at a blank page. It takes about fifteen minutes, and you’ll finish by trying the method on a real piece of your own writing. Research puts the gain at about 40% faster with better results1.

The evidence that handing off the first draft actually works.

The case for learning this, in three studies. The evidence / 01

Why this is worth your time.

Think about the last email that took you half an hour. Most of that time went to getting started: staring at the page, typing an opening line, deleting it. Researchers have measured what happens when people hand that first draft to AI and keep the editing for themselves. In one set of case studies, business users produced 59% more routine documents per hour4. The three studies below found the same pattern: the work gets faster, and it gets better when a person edits the result.

Controlled experiment · Science 40%

Professionals who used ChatGPT finished mid-level writing tasks 40% faster, and the quality of their work rose 18%.

Noy & Zhang, Science (2023)1
Field experiment · Harvard / BCG 25%

Consultants working with AI finished 12.2% more tasks, 25.1% faster, and at 40% higher quality on the tasks AI handles well.

Dell’Acqua et al., HBS Working Paper (2023)2
Real workplaces · QJE ~15%

In a real workplace, an AI assistant raised productivity about 15% on average. The biggest gains went to the least experienced workers, so if writing isn’t your favorite part of the job, this method helps you the most.

Brynjolfsson, Li & Raymond, QJE (2025)3

The method those studies point to. It fits on one page.

The one method this course teaches. The method / 02

Brief, draft, edit.

Here is the whole method. When you have something to write, you do three things in order: tell the AI what you need (the brief), let it write the complete first draft, then edit that draft into something you’d actually send. The next three pages show it working on real tasks.

01 · Brief

Tell it what you need

Brief the AI the way you’d brief a colleague. CRIT gives you the four things to include: Context (the situation), Role (who it should write as), Interview (tell it to ask you questions before it writes), and Task (exactly what you want, including format and length).

ContextRoleInterviewTask

The Interview line matters most. Writing “ask me up to three questions before you draft” means the AI fills its gaps by asking you instead of guessing.

4parts to every brief

The four-part brief comes from Geoff Woods; the Working Smarter course teaches it in full.

Geoff Woods, The AI-Driven Leader
02 · Draft

Let it write the first draft

Send the brief, answer the questions it asks, and let it write the complete draft. Don’t worry that the result sounds generic or gets details wrong. Its job is to give you something to react to, which is much easier than starting from nothing.

40%less writing time

The saving in the Science experiment came from exactly this handoff: the AI drafts, you polish1.

Noy & Zhang, Science (2023)
03 · Edit

You do the editing

Make it sound like you, check every name, date, and number, and cut what isn’t needed. This step is where the quality gain in those studies comes from, and it’s the part only you can do.

+40%higher quality

The top-rated consultants reworked the AI’s first pass instead of accepting it2.

Dell’Acqua et al., Harvard / BCG (2023)

The AI writes the first draft, and you write the version that gets sent.

Together

Turn to a partner and explain Brief → Draft → Edit back to each other in under a minute. Explaining it out loud is the fastest way to make it stick.

Pause + reflect

Before you turn the page, look away from the screen and name the three steps out loud. If you can recall them now, you’ll remember them tomorrow.

Worked example 1 of 3: the everyday email. Example / 03 · Email

Saying no to a vendor you like.

The situation

A vendor you like has asked for a meeting during a week when you have no time. You need to say no without souring the relationship. This is the kind of email that takes twenty minutes, because every sentence has to be careful.

Together

Do this one together: the instructor takes edits from the room and runs the prompt live.

Pause + reflect

Try it now: copy this prompt into your AI tool and run it. Then compare what you get with the edit list below.

The prompt · CRIT shape 
Context: A vendor we have a good relationship with has asked for a meeting next week. My calendar is full and the timing is wrong, but I want to keep the door open for next quarter.
Role: You are an experienced professional who writes warm, direct business email.
Interview: Ask me up to three questions before you draft.
Task: Draft a short email declining the meeting, under 100 words, that keeps the relationship warm and offers one specific alternative.
Try it in
ChatGPT EDU Recommended Everyday, non-sensitive writing is exactly what it’s for, and it’s the fastest way in. Amplify Also fine Vanderbilt’s own platform, and the right home the moment anything sensitive enters the thread. Copilot Also fine Handy if you already live in Outlook, where this email will be sent from anyway.

Each example recommends a different tool, so by the end you’ll have tried all three.

What comes back

A polite, well-organized decline that sounds like no one in particular. It may invent an alternative meeting time and a warmth it can’t actually feel. That’s fine, because it’s a draft and you’re about to edit it.

Your edit pass
  • Swap in the real names and dates, and replace its suggested alternative with the one you would genuinely offer.
  • Rewrite the opening line in your own voice. The first sentence is the one your reader notices most.
  • Add one detail only you know, like the last project you worked on together. That detail is what makes the email warm.
Worked example 2 of 3: the all-staff notice. Example / 03 · Announcement

Telling everyone the lobby is closed.

The situation

The lobby is closing for maintenance next week, and three hundred people walk through it every morning. Everyone needs to know the dates and which entrance to use instead. That is the whole job of this announcement.

The prompt · CRIT shape 
Context: Our building's main lobby will be closed for maintenance next week. Staff should use the alternate entrance instead. Everyone needs to know the dates and the new route.
Role: You are a workplace communications writer who values clarity over cheer.
Interview: Ask me up to three questions before you draft.
Task: Draft an all-staff announcement, 120 words, subject line included, no exclamation points.
Try it in
Copilot Recommended An all-staff notice gets written and sent in Outlook or Word, and Copilot is already there on your Vanderbilt account. ChatGPT EDU Also fine A notice like this carries no sensitive details, so everyday drafting is fair game. Amplify Also fine The when-in-doubt choice; it handles anything you’d rather keep in-house.
What comes back

A clean, correctly organized notice built on guesses: dates it made up, an entrance it named on its own. It reads smoothly and is wrong about every detail it didn’t know. This is exactly why the brief tells it to interview you first.

Your edit pass
  • Replace every date and location with the real ones. The model wrote plausible placeholders; only you have the facts.
  • Walk the route in your head. Does the alternate entrance actually work for deliveries, wheelchairs, and badge access?
  • Cut anything that sounds like an apology. Readers just need the facts here.

Turning a long report into a short brief.

The situation

A 12-page report lands the afternoon before your director’s 8 a.m. meeting. They have ten minutes to prepare and need half a page: first the decisions the report asks of them, then what it found.

What comes back

A confident, tidy brief in exactly the shape you asked for, possibly including numbers it misread along the way. Summaries are where AI sounds most sure of itself, so this is where your checking matters most.

Your edit pass
  • Check every number against the original. A model can summarize confidently and still misread a table.
  • Reorder the decisions to match what your director actually controls. The model doesn’t know their job.
  • Add your recommendation. The report won’t make one, and your director will ask for it.
The practice report · fictional
Read the practice report (every name and number is invented; abridged from the “12 pages”) 
CAMPUS SHUTTLE PROGRAM: YEAR-END REVIEW
Practice report. Fictional; prepared for the Working Smarter course.

Summary of operations. The shuttle program ran three routes this
year: the Blue Loop (academic core), the Gold Line (residence halls
to the medical area), and the Crosstown Connector. Combined ridership
reached 412,000 boardings, up 18% from last year. The gain was
uneven: Blue Loop grew 31%, Gold Line grew 9%, and the Crosstown
Connector declined 22% for the second straight year.

Costs. Total operating cost was $2.41 million, or $5.85 per boarding.
Cost per boarding ranged from $3.10 on the Blue Loop to $14.75 on the
Crosstown Connector. Fuel and maintenance costs rose 7%; driver wages
rose 4% under the new contract.

Service quality. On-time performance averaged 87% against a target of
90%. The largest source of delay was the Fifth Street construction
detour, which added six minutes to the Gold Line in the fall term.
Rider satisfaction in the spring survey was 4.1 out of 5, up from
3.8. The most requested improvement was evening service, cited by 61%
of respondents.

Evening pilot. A limited evening pilot on the Blue Loop (service
until 10 p.m., October through March) averaged 240 boardings per
night at $4.20 per boarding. Student government has petitioned to
make the pilot permanent and extend it to midnight.

Fleet. Two of the eleven shuttles have passed 250,000 miles and are
beyond their recommended service life; both had unscheduled
breakdowns this year. Replacement cost is approximately $180,000
each. One replacement is budgeted for next year; the second is not.

Decisions requested. The program office asks the director to
(1) approve permanent evening service on the Blue Loop and decide
whether the midnight extension is included, (2) approve the
unbudgeted second shuttle replacement or direct staff to defer it a
year, and (3) decide whether the Crosstown Connector is consolidated
with the Gold Line at the end of the spring term, which would save an
estimated $310,000 annually but add eight minutes to the average
Crosstown trip.

Copy the prompt, copy this report in its place, and run it. Notice the report buries its three decisions at the end; your task asks for them first, and your edit pass checks whether the numbers survived the trip.

The rules that keep the method safe. Guardrails / 04

The four rules.

The method only works if you follow these. They take thirty seconds to learn.

Rule 01
Safe data only

Sensitive or internal Vanderbilt business belongs in Amplify or Copilot through your Vanderbilt account, never a personal account. When in doubt, use Amplify.

Rule 02
Verify everything

Every fact, name, number, and date. Checking was always part of writing; it now happens after the draft instead of during it.

Rule 03
You own every word you send

The tool drafted it, but you sent it. Read every draft as if your name is signed at the bottom, because it is.

Rule 04
Keep your own voice

Edit until it sounds like you. If everything you send starts sounding the same, people stop reading it.

Three quick questions to check it stuck.

Three questions, none of them tricks. Knowledge check / 06

Did it stick?

Pick an answer on each; you’ll see right away how you did. Nothing here is scored or sent anywhere. If the LMS version of this course includes a graded check, it replaces this one.

1. Two briefs for the same email. Which one gets the better draft?

The second one. It gives the situation, a voice, a deliverable, and, most important, the Interview line, so the AI asks about what it doesn’t know instead of guessing.

2. A colleague pastes a student’s disciplinary record into a personal ChatGPT account to summarize it. What went wrong?

Rule one: safe data only. Deleting a chat later doesn’t undo pasting sensitive data into an unapproved tool, and no prompt improvement fixes a data problem.

3. The AI’s draft of your announcement reads smoothly and looks finished. What’s your next move?

Smooth is what the draft is supposed to be; it says nothing about correct. The edit pass is yours: verify the facts, restore your voice, and tighten it.
