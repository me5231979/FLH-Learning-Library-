Ideas, faster.

Get unstuck: more options, better questions, clearer thinking.

If your job ever hands you a problem with no obvious answer (a stalled project, a falling number, a goal as vague as “make it better”), this course is for you. You’ll learn a simple way to use AI as a thinking partner when you’re stuck, so you start from twenty options instead of a blank page. It takes about fifteen minutes, and you’ll finish by running the method on a real problem of your own. For scale: in one Wharton experiment, a person working with AI generated 200 usable ideas in about 15 minutes1.

Vanderbilt will define the great university of the 21st Century and be it.

That is Chancellor Diermeier’s institutional vision, one sentence with no hedge in it. The how is equally blunt: operate at uncommon speed, agility, and scale to address society’s challenges through education and research, and help reclaim the essential role of leading research universities in driving American prosperity. Our motto has always been the dare, Crescere aude, dare to grow, and nobody grows while they’re stuck circling the same three ideas5.

With speed, agility and scale, Vanderbilt will distinguish itself with innovative and impactful capabilities to ensure that we have the talent, resources and reputation necessary to deliver on our vision.

How this course delivers

Ideas, faster feeds the bold side of the mission: more options on the table sooner is how increasingly Bold Strategic Initiatives begin, at every desk in the university.

The reputation flywheel

Reputation and resources feed each other in a loop, reputation attracts talent, funding, partnerships, and access, which build the reputation. The university spins that flywheel with execution with ambition, values-driven leadership, and athletics momentum. Here’s your place in it: execution with ambition needs a steady supply of ideas worth executing, and the filter step, where you choose with criteria you own, is what keeps your team’s work varied instead of alike. The evidence: what happens when the options come fast and the judgment stays yours.

The case for learning this, in three studies. The evidence / 01

Why this is worth your time.

Think about the last time you were properly stuck: a problem you kept circling without landing anywhere. Working people already reach for AI at exactly that moment. In Gallup’s 2025 workplace survey, generating ideas was one of the two most common uses of AI at work, named by 41% of employees who use it4. The three studies below explain why it works, and where it doesn’t: the options come faster, people feel less blocked, and your judgment still has a job that no tool can do.

Controlled comparison · Wharton / Cornell 200

One person working with ChatGPT-4 generated 200 product ideas in about 15 minutes. Survey respondents liked the AI’s ideas better on average (47% purchase interest vs 40% for human ideas), and 35 of the 40 best ideas in the pool came from the AI.

Girotra, Meincke, Terwiesch & Ulrich, SSRN working paper (2023)1
Global survey · Slack Workforce Index Top 3

Among daily AI users, brainstorming past creative blocks is one of the top three reasons they say AI makes them more productive: 45% cite it, just behind cutting research time (51%) and help with writing (50%).

Slack Workforce Lab, “The New AI Advantage” (2025)2
The honest caveat · Science Advances Alike

Writers given AI ideas produced individually more creative, better-written work, with the biggest lift for the least confident writers. But the AI-assisted work was more similar across writers. The flood is a starting point; your judgment supplies the variety.

Doshi & Hauser, Science Advances (2024)3

The method those studies point to. It fits on one page.

The one method this course teaches. The method / 02

Frame, flood, filter.

Here is the whole method. When you’re stuck, you do three things in order: frame the problem so the AI knows what you’re actually asking, flood the zone with more options than you’d ever produce alone, then filter with criteria you own. The next three pages show it working on real problems.

01 · Frame

Tell it what you’re stuck on

Frame the problem the way you’d brief a colleague. CRIT gives you the four things to include: Context (the situation), Role (who it should think as), Interview (tell it to ask you questions before it answers), and Task (exactly what you want back).

ContextRoleInterviewTask

The Interview line matters most. Writing “ask me up to three questions before you answer” makes the AI ask what you actually need instead of guessing at it.

4parts to every brief

The four-part brief comes from Geoff Woods; the Working Smarter course teaches it in full.

Geoff Woods, The AI-Driven Leader
02 · Flood

Ask for quantity and range

Ask for 20 options, wild ones included, then push for range: “give me the version a skeptic would propose,” “give me three that cost nothing.” Don’t stop at the first answer that sounds fine. Quantity is the point, because the delete key is cheap.

200ideas in about 15 minutes

That’s the pace one person hit working with AI in the Wharton experiment. Volume like that is exactly the part you can’t do alone1.

Girotra et al., SSRN working paper (2023)
03 · Filter

You pick, combine, and sharpen

Judge the flood with criteria you own: cost, effort, impact, fit. Push back (“argue against your own best idea”), combine the survivors, and develop the two or three worth developing. This step is yours, because the AI doesn’t know what your team can actually do.

In a controlled experiment, AI ideas made individual work better but made everyone’s work more alike3. Your judgment supplies the variety.

Doshi & Hauser, Science Advances (2024)

The AI supplies the options, and you supply the judgment about what is worth doing.

Worked example 1 of 3: the blank page. Example / 03 · The blank page

Twenty ideas for the fall event.

The situation

You’re planning the fall staff appreciation event, and the file is empty. Sixty people, a modest budget, and two straight years of the same catered lunch. Nobody is unhappy, but nobody is excited either, and the planning meeting is Thursday.

Pause + reflect

Try it now: copy this prompt into your AI tool and run it. Then compare what comes back with the filter pass below.

The prompt · CRIT shape 
Context: I'm planning our fall staff appreciation event. About 60 people, a modest budget, on campus, during the workday. The last two years were the same catered lunch, and it landed flat.
Role: You are an event planner who designs workplace celebrations people actually talk about afterward.
Interview: Ask me up to three questions before you answer.
Task: Give me 20 theme and format options, one line each. Include a few wild ones and a few that cost almost nothing. Then flag the three you'd shortlist and say why.
Try it in
ChatGPT EDU Recommended An event theme is everyday, non-sensitive work, so the fastest way in is the right one. Amplify Also fine Vanderbilt’s own platform, and the right home the moment budget details or names enter the thread. Copilot Also fine Handy if your event planning already lives in Outlook and Excel on your Vanderbilt account.

Each example recommends a different tool, so by the end you’ll have tried more than one.

What comes back

Twenty options in seconds: five obvious ones, ten workable ones, and a few genuinely odd ones, like a soup cook-off or a campus scavenger hunt. Several won’t fit your budget or your building. That’s fine, because the flood is raw material, not a plan.

Your filter pass
  • Pick the two you’d actually attend, not the two that sound most impressive in a meeting.
  • Combine them. The best option is often two of the twenty stapled together, like the scavenger hunt that ends at the cook-off.
  • Sanity-check the budget and the date before you pitch it. The AI guessed at both, and only you know the real numbers.
Worked example 2 of 3: the ambiguous problem. Example / 03 · The mystery

Nobody comes to the optional trainings.

The situation

Attendance at your optional lunchtime trainings has fallen all year, and you don’t know why. Sessions that drew thirty people last fall now draw eight. Before you fix the wrong thing, you need better guesses about what’s actually going on.

Describing an internal problem? Keep it general: no names, no personnel details. Framed that way it’s fine in ChatGPT EDU, but real internal detail belongs in Amplify or Copilot through your Vanderbilt account. When in doubt, use Amplify.

The prompt · CRIT shape 
Context: Attendance at our optional lunchtime trainings has dropped from about 30 people to about 8 over the past year. Topics and format haven't changed. I don't know why.
Role: You are an organizational consultant who looks for the boring explanation before the interesting one.
Interview: Ask me up to three questions before you answer.
Task: List 10 possible causes, from most to least likely. Then tell me what a skeptic would say the real reason is, and list what information I'm missing that would help me tell these causes apart.
Try it in
Amplify Recommended This is internal operational context, and Amplify is built for Vanderbilt data. It’s also the when-in-doubt choice. ChatGPT EDU Also fine Fine for a problem described in general terms like this one; keep names and specifics out of the thread. Copilot Also fine Also approved for internal content through your Vanderbilt account.
What comes back

A ranked list of causes you half-suspected (timing, calendar fatigue, competing meetings) plus a few you hadn’t considered, a skeptic’s answer that stings a little, and a list of what you don’t know yet. The missing-information list is the quiet star: it turns a vague worry into specific questions.

Your filter pass
  • Take the missing-information list to a real colleague who attends, or used to. Ten minutes of asking beats another hour of guessing.
  • Cross off the causes you can disprove today with information you already have, like the room calendar or past sign-up lists.
  • Pick the two causes worth testing and decide what one cheap test of each looks like. The list was the flood; the diagnosis is yours.
Worked example 3 of 3: the vague goal. Example / 03 · The vague goal

“Make onboarding better.”

The situation

Your director wants onboarding improved by spring, and that sentence is the whole assignment. New hires say the first week is a blur of logins and forms. You need to turn one vague goal into a short list of concrete changes you can actually propose.

The prompt · CRIT shape 
Context: I've been asked to improve onboarding for new staff in our department. Right now it's a first week of scattered logins, forms, and one welcome meeting. New hires say it's a blur. I have a small budget and one semester.
Role: You are an operations lead who turns vague goals into concrete, sized improvements.
Interview: Ask me up to three questions before you answer.
Task: List 20 specific improvements, one line each, from tiny to ambitious. Then shortlist 3 using these criteria: low cost, low effort to maintain, high impact on a new hire's first month. Show your scoring.
Try it in
Copilot Recommended Onboarding checklists and docs live in Word and Outlook, and Copilot works on internal content through your Vanderbilt account. Amplify Also recommended Equally right for internal process detail, and the when-in-doubt choice. ChatGPT EDU Also fine Fine while the goal stays this general; move to Amplify or Copilot before pasting internal documents.

By now you’ve seen all three approved tools recommended once each. Pick the one that fits where your work lives.

What comes back

Twenty improvements from “send the laptop a week early” to “assign every new hire a first-week buddy,” then a tidy shortlist of three, scored with full confidence. The scoring looks authoritative and isn’t. It doesn’t know your budget, your team, or what your director will actually fund.

Your filter pass
  • Re-score the shortlist against criteria you set, with your real weights, not the AI’s. Keep cost, effort, and impact, and add what it can’t know.
  • Ask it to argue against its own top pick. The rebuttal usually names the risk you’ll be asked about in the meeting.
  • Keep the other seventeen. The ideas you didn’t choose are your answer to “what else did you consider?”
The rules that keep the method safe. Guardrails / 04

The four rules.

The method only works if you follow these. They take thirty seconds to learn.

Rule 01
Safe data only

Describe internal problems in general terms in ChatGPT EDU, or take the real detail to Amplify or Copilot through your Vanderbilt account, never a personal one. When in doubt, use Amplify.

Rule 02
Ideas are starters, not decisions

The flood gives you options; it doesn’t make the call. You own what gets proposed, so filter with your own criteria before anything leaves your desk.

Rule 03
Watch the sameness trap

AI ideas cluster. In a controlled experiment they made individual work better and everyone’s work more alike3. Bring your own weird: add the option only you would think of.

Rule 04
Credit people, not the tool

The final plan belongs to you and your colleagues. When AI helped substantively, say so, the same way Vanderbilt’s norms expect you to disclose any substantial help.

The data rule · the same card in every AI course

Before anything goes into an AI tool, one three-second question: what color is this?

🟢
Green · Public or generic
Questions about public topics, generic drafts, brainstorming, "explain this to me." Any approved tool.
🟡
Yellow · Internal work
Your drafts, internal announcements, budgets, grant drafts, internal policies. Approved Vanderbilt tools only, signed in with your VU account. Never a personal account or a random free site.
🔴
Red · Private information about people
Names with grades, health details, personnel issues, passwords, anything confidential about a real person. No AI tool, full stop.

Inside the light: Vanderbilt classifies data in levels, and each approved tool has a ceiling. ChatGPT EDU (VU account) is approved up to Level 2. Amplify and Microsoft Copilot (VU account) are approved for Level 3 work. Children's names, photos, and records go in no AI tool.

The hard cases: ask "who is this about" before "how sensitive does it feel." A student's essay is about a student. Meeting notes naming colleagues are about colleagues until the names come out. A recommendation letter is about its subject.

Never, in any tool: Social Security numbers · Student records or grades (FERPA) · Health information (HIPAA) · Children's names, photos, and records · Salaries and personnel files · Passwords and credentials · Anything confidential about a real person.

This card is the working shorthand for Vanderbilt IT's data classification and approved-tool guidance: vanderbilt.edu/it/policies. When the two ever disagree, the policy wins.

Three questions, none of them tricks. Knowledge check / 06

Did it stick?

Pick an answer on each; you’ll see right away how you did. Nothing here is scored or sent anywhere. If the LMS version of this course includes a graded check, it replaces this one.

1. Two prompts about the same stuck problem. Which one gets the more useful flood?

The second one. It gives the situation, a way of thinking, a specific deliverable, and, most important, the Interview line, so the AI asks about what it doesn’t know instead of guessing.

2. A colleague wants ideas for handling a conflict between two named team members, so they paste both names and the whole story into a personal ChatGPT account. What went wrong?

Rule one: safe data only, and brainstorming counts. Names and personnel details belong in Amplify or Copilot through a Vanderbilt account, in general terms anywhere else, or in a conversation with a human instead.

3. You asked for 20 ideas and all 20 feel like the same safe idea wearing different hats. What’s your next move?

Push, don’t settle. Range shows up when you ask for it directly, and the research on AI ideas says variety is the part you supply, so add the option only you would think of.
