The role, honestly

A Vanderbilt manager holds three jobs at once.

Most managers were promoted because they were excellent at the work. The role that follows is three jobs, held at once: keeping the rules (compliance and duty), leading the people (employee relations), and running the processes (hiring, leave, pay, time). And beneath all three runs a fourth thing: the people skills that make the other jobs work. Pick a domain to begin. Each ends in a simulator where you make the calls a real manager makes.

Two Vanderbilt terms you'll see throughout: PCB is People, Culture and Belonging, our name for HR, and your EC is your Engagement Consultant, the PCB partner assigned to your unit.

Simulator · Report or resolve?

Some things you handle. Some things you are required to report.

The most consequential compliance mistake a manager makes is sitting on something the law says must go up immediately. Read each situation and make the call. Getting one wrong here is exactly how institutions end up in the headlines.

How to decide · the three lanes
Resolve it yourselfEveryday management calls: schedules, workload, ordinary disagreements, a first-time minor lapse. Nothing protected is involved and no legal duty applies.
Loop in PCB / ComplianceGray areas and patterns: possible conflicts of interest, gifts from vendors, repeat behavior, anything touching pay, leave, or policy where you want a second reader before acting.
Report immediatelyLegal duties, not judgment calls: suspected abuse of a minor, sexual misconduct (Title IX), safety threats, retaliation. You report the same day, and you never investigate it yourself.

Simulator · Handle or escalate?

Knowing your lane is a skill.

Great managers solve most things at their desk. The best ones also know the precise moment a situation stops being theirs to solve alone, and loop in Engagement Consultants (ECs), Employee Relations, or Title IX before it grows. Make the call on each.

How to decide · whose lane is it
Handle it yourselfCoaching, performance, everyday friction. Most of the job lives here, escalating it trains your team to route around you.
Partner with PCBPatterns that persist after coaching, accommodation requests, anything touching pay or leave, counteroffers. You stay involved; you just stop deciding alone.
Escalate to ER / Legal nowAnything touching a protected class, harassment, safety, wage violations, or retaliation, even when the person asks you to keep it quiet.

Deepens · Documentation & fairness

The Note Builder · document a real situation, properly

Here is why this tool exists. When something on your team is worth remembering, a missed deadline, a warning conversation, a pattern you are starting to see, the working rule in Employee Relations is blunt: if it is not written down, it did not happen. The five-minute note you write today is what makes a decision look fair, and be defensible, if it is questioned two years from now. It also protects your employee: a good note records the support you offered, not just the problem.

How to use it: bring a real situation from your own team and fill in the five parts below. As you type, the builder flags the words that sink files (labels like “lazy,” absolutes like “always,” medical guesses like “seems depressed”), because notes get read by lawyers, not just by you. It then assembles the finished note; keep it, it is yours.

Deepens · Pay & PTO

The Approval Queue · five requests, your name on each answer

Every approval you click is a policy decision with your signature on it. This deck hands you five requests the way they actually arrive: a routine PTO ask, a generous-sounding comp-time offer, a gift card, a flex request, a tiebreaker. You are practicing the judgment behind the click, not the Oracle steps.

The rules you are applying
Non-exempt timeOvertime worked is overtime paid. There is no informal comp-time swap, even when the employee suggests it.
Exempt timeMeasured by the role, not the clock. Metering a salaried person’s hours burns goodwill and can even threaten the exemption.
Anything that adds moneyCash, gift cards, and bonuses are taxable compensation with policy rules. Route them through PCB, never through goodwill.
ConsistencyApply tiebreakers as written. Bend one rule for a favorite and every future decision you make gets read as favoritism.
Deepens · Expense reports

The Friday Pile · approve, ask, or send back

When you approve an expense report, you are certifying that the spend is legitimate, documented, and on the right funds. That makes you Vanderbilt’s front-line financial control; auditors start with the approver, not the spender. First, five single lines. Then the audit below hands you a whole report.

The rules you are applying
Three tests per lineA stated business purpose, an itemized receipt, and the right funding source. A line missing any of the three is not ready for your signature.
Federal money is differentGrant funds carry extra rules: alcohol is never allowable, and neither is anything outside the award’s terms. Findings outlive everyone at the table.
Commuting is personalThe daily trip between home and campus is not reimbursable, however the line is labeled.
“Ask” is a real verdictA clarifying email costs a day. An undocumented exception becomes the precedent everyone cites next year.

Deepens · Timekeeping & FLSA

The Time Card · what counts as work?

The FLSA, the federal wage-and-hour law, has one rule managers break more than any other: if a non-exempt employee works, the time is paid, whether or not you asked for it, saw it, or approved it. Timesheets are legal records, not formalities. Five situations below; decide what counts as work and when the record needs fixing.

The rules you are applying
The core ruleFor non-exempt staff, all hours worked are paid hours. “Off the clock” work does not legally exist.
Suffered or permittedThe 9pm email counts even though nobody asked for it. If work happened and you knew or should have known, the clock ran.
Required means paidMandatory training, midday travel between work sites, and a lunch spent covering the phones are all work time.
Records cut both waysOverstated hours are as much a liability as understated ones. Fix the record, then fix the norm that produced it.

Deepens · Hiring & onboarding · Talent ID & succession

Hiring fills a seat. Succession fills the future.

Everyone has talent. Not everyone is talent, in the succession sense: someone the university should be deliberately preparing for bigger things. Gartner’s research puts the gap plainly: 61% of HR leaders say succession plans focus too narrowly on senior roles, and the fix starts with managers like you spotting the right people early.

Succession in four moves · the Gartner framework
1. Start with critical roles, not titlesMap the roles that are both business-critical and hard to fill, at every level. Succession is not only about who replaces the dean; it is about who can run payroll next June.
2. Define successors for the future, not the pastOnly 35% of organizations pick successors against future needs. Ask what the role will demand in three years, not what the current holder happens to be good at.
3. Find HIPOs with all three signalsGartner defines high potential as ability + aspiration + engagement, together. Performance alone is one leg of three. The deck below trains your eye.
4. Keep it alive with career conversationsA succession plan reviewed once a year is a document; a bench built through ongoing growth conversations is a pipeline. Tell people they have a future here, out loud, before a recruiter does.

Mastery 01 · The Interview Room

Eight interview questions. Which would you ask?

You're hiring a program coordinator. A colleague drafted questions for the panel. Some are excellent, some carry legal risk, and a few should never be said out loud in an interview. Sort them, Ask it, Rephrase it, or Never ask.

The rules before you sort
Ask itJob-related, about behavior or ability, and asked of every candidate in the same words. Behavioral questions (“tell me about a time…”) are the gold standard.
Rephrase itThe need is legitimate but the wording touches protected territory. Ask about the job requirement, the schedule, the travel, the lifting “with or without reasonable accommodation”, never about the personal circumstance behind it.
Never askNo compliant version exists: age, national origin, family plans, religion, health, and, in New York, California, and a growing list of states, salary history. If it’s about who they are rather than what the job needs, it doesn’t belong in the room.
Mastery 02 · The Calibration Lab

Spot the bias before it reaches the rating.

Review season. Six rating rationales are on the table, real quotes from real calibration meetings, lightly disguised. Diagnose each one: is it fair and specific, or is a bias doing the rating?

Know the biases before you judge
Recency biasThe last few weeks stand in for the whole year, a strong (or rough) finish rewrites twelve months of evidence.
Halo effectOne shining result (or one failure) colors every category, so a great launch makes someone a “great communicator” too.
Similar-to-meShared background, style, or career path quietly reads as performance. The most common bias in the room, and the hardest to spot on yourself.
Fair & specificEvidence from the whole period, tied to goals, context noted, written down. What every rating in the room should look like.

Summary · the eight ideas

What you now know.

Before you go, one pass through the ideas this whole course is built on. This recap is not a formality; a quick review right after learning is what moves ideas into long-term memory. Each card ends with the habit that puts the idea to work.

The three jobs

A Vanderbilt manager holds three jobs at once: keeping the rules, leading the people, and running the processes. Underneath all three sit the people skills that make them work.

At work: when a week feels chaotic, name which job each fire belongs to. The lane tells you the next move.

Compliance is state-specific

The federal floor is the same everywhere; the obligations stacked on top are not. Tennessee, New York, California, and Florida each change what a manager must know, do, and report.

At work: before advising anyone who works outside Tennessee, check their state profile first.

The three lanes

Handle it yourself, partner with your EC, or escalate now. Most of the job is Lane 1. Pay, leave, accommodations, and persistent patterns are Lane 2. Protected classes, harassment, safety, and retaliation are Lane 3, same day, every time.

At work: Lane 3 stays Lane 3 even when the person asks you to keep it quiet.

If it is not written down, it did not happen

Documentation is what makes decisions fair and defensible. A note that holds up has five parts: the date, facts a camera would record, the expectation you set, the support you offered, and a follow-up date.

At work: write the note the same day. December memory is not March fact.

Leave rarely arrives labeled

FMLA shows up as a life event in a hallway conversation. What you say in the next five minutes protects the person and the university, or exposes both. Its sibling, the ADA, covers changes to how someone works rather than time away.

At work: say the protective sentence, then stop: “You may be entitled to job-protected leave; let me connect you with Leave Administration today.”

Time and money are legal records

For non-exempt staff, all hours worked are paid hours; off-the-clock work does not legally exist. And your expense approval is the university’s front-line financial control, with your name on it.

At work: fix the record, then fix the norm that produced it. Read the lines, not the total.

Managers are talent scouts

Everyone has talent. Not everyone is talent in the succession sense. High potential is ability, aspiration, and engagement together, and spotting it early is part of your job, not a favor.

At work: tell people they have a future here, out loud, before a recruiter does.

Coach before you correct

The line between coaching and discipline is whether the person can’t yet or won’t anymore. Growth conversations, real 1:1s, and psychological safety are what keep your best people; discipline is for when coaching has genuinely run out.

At work: one piece of specific, behavior-based feedback every week, so hard conversations are never your first conversations.

If any card feels shaky, its section is one click up the page, and the pocket card carries the structure with you. The assessment is how you prove the rest.

Lead with judgment.

The people on your team will remember how you handled the hard moment. Start with the one you're most likely to face first.
