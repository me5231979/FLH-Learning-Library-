The one method this course teaches. The method / 02

Capture, condense, confirm.

Here is the whole method. When a meeting or a long document needs to become a summary, you do three things in order: capture a faithful record, have AI condense it into decisions, action items, and a short summary, then confirm the result against the source before it circulates. The next three pages show it working on real tasks.

01 · Capture

Get a faithful record

A summary is only as good as its source. In Microsoft Teams, select More actions > Record and transcribe > Start recording in the meeting controls; everyone in the meeting is notified when recording starts4. Afterward the transcript sits with the meeting’s Recap, and organizers can download it as a .docx or .vtt file5. Copilot’s intelligent recap needs a Teams Premium or Microsoft 365 Copilot license, so check what your account includes before you promise it6.

In Zoom, record locally or to the cloud; cloud recordings can generate an audio transcript automatically7. Where your account enables it, AI Companion writes a meeting summary and tells participants it is on8, and the recording disclaimer asks people to consent when a recording starts9.

02 · Condense

Brief the condense

Don’t say “summarize this.” Brief it with CRIT: Context (what the record is and who the summary is for), Role (who it should write as), Interview (tell it to ask you questions first), and a Task that dictates the shape: decisions first, then action items with an owner and a date each, then a short summary. 03 · Confirm

Check it against the source

Before the summary circulates, read it against the transcript or report: names, numbers, and who owns what. A transcript mishears, and a model summarizes confidently either way. Anything the record marks [inaudible] gets asked, not guessed.

Minutes for the Monday staff meeting.

What comes back

Minutes in exactly the shape you asked for, and confident everywhere, including the places the transcript wasn’t. It may pin an action item on the wrong speaker, turn the [inaudible] line into a specific number, or harden a half-decision into a firm one. That is what the confirm pass is for.

Your confirm pass
  • Check every action item’s owner and date against the transcript. Misassigned work is the fastest way to make minutes untrusted.
  • Chase the [inaudible] line to the person who said it. In the practice transcript the session cap got garbled; the minutes need the real number, never the model’s guess.
  • Read the decisions as written and confirm the chair actually closed them, then send the minutes with a link to the transcript.

What comes back

A tidy one-pager that reads like it understood the report. Check before you believe it: models misread tables, average away ranges, and state a number from page 12 with the confidence of page 1. Summaries are where AI sounds most sure of itself.

Your confirm pass
  • Check every number against the original. The 54 percent average hides a 38-to-71 range, and a summary that drops the range misleads politely.
  • Find the section that names your team and read it in full. The model’s paraphrase of your obligations is the one paraphrase you cannot afford to trust.
  • Note what the takeaway left out. A one-pager chooses, and you should know what it dropped before your director asks.

Worked example 3 of 3: the meeting you missed. Example / 03 · Catch-up

The meeting you missed.

The situation

You were out Tuesday, the project meeting happened anyway, and a colleague sends the transcript with “you may want to look at this.” You do not want fifty minutes of reading. You want five bullets on what happened and a straight answer to one question: what am I on the hook for?

The prompt · CRIT shape 
Context: Pasted below is the transcript of a meeting I could not attend. I need to catch up in five minutes, not fifty.
Role: You are a colleague catching me up: brief, specific, and honest about what is unclear.
Interview: Ask me up to three questions before you draft.
Task: Give me five bullets on what I need to know, then a separate list of the action items assigned to me, each with its date. If the transcript is ambiguous about whether something is mine, put it under "check with the owner" instead of deciding.

What comes back

Five clean bullets and a list of your action items, which is exactly what you asked for. The risk lives in the items the room left vague: “someone should check with facilities” becomes yours, or stays invisible, on the model’s coin flip. The “check with the owner” line in the Task exists to catch those.

Your confirm pass
  • Search the transcript for your own name and read those lines in full. The summary compresses exactly the parts you care most about.
  • Message the owner of anything ambiguous instead of guessing. “The summary says this might be mine; is it?” takes one line and prevents a dropped task.
  • Reply to the sender with the items you now own, so the record of who is doing what is shared, not private.
The rules that keep the method safe. Guardrails / 04

The four rules.

The method only works if you follow these. They take thirty seconds to learn.

Rule 01
Safe data only

Meeting transcripts and internal reports live in Amplify or Copilot through your Vanderbilt account, never personal tools. When in doubt, use Amplify.

Rule 02
Recording is announced, not assumed

Teams and Zoom notify participants when recording starts49, and you still say it out loud at the top of the meeting. Some conversations should not be recorded at all, and HR-sensitive ones need guidance before you hit record.

Rule 03
Confirm before it circulates

Names, numbers, owners, dates. A misheard transcript plus a confident summary is how wrong action items ship.

Rule 04
The summary is the start, not the record

Keep the transcript or recording per your unit’s retention practice, and link it from the minutes, so anyone can check the summary against what was actually said.

Context: Pasted below is a 30-page committee report. My team is named in it, and I brief them in thirty minutes. Role: You are a chief of staff who turns long reports into one-page takeaways people actually read. Interview: Ask me up to three questions before you draft. Task: Write a one-page takeaway with four sections: decisions the report requests, key findings, risks, and what it asks of my team. Keep every number exactly as the report states it, and say which section each number came from.

3 · The meeting you missed (catch-up in five bullets)

Context: Pasted below is the transcript of a meeting I could not attend. I need to catch up in five minutes, not fifty. Role: You are a colleague catching me up: brief, specific, and honest about what is unclear. Interview: Ask me up to three questions before you draft. Task: Give me five bullets on what I need to know, then a separate list of the action items assigned to me, each with its date. If the transcript is ambiguous about whether something is mine, put it under "check with the owner" instead of deciding.

The guardrails

  1. Safe data only. Meeting transcripts and internal reports live in Amplify or Copilot through your Vanderbilt account, never personal tools. When in doubt, use Amplify.
  2. Recording is announced, not assumed. Teams and Zoom notify participants, and you still say it out loud; HR-sensitive conversations need guidance before you hit record.
  3. Confirm before it circulates. Names, numbers, owners, dates, checked against the transcript or report.
  4. The summary is the start, not the record. Keep the transcript per your unit’s retention practice, and link it from the minutes.
