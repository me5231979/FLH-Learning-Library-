The one method this course teaches. The method / 02

Story, build, polish.

Here is the whole method. When you have a deck to make, you do three things in order: work out the story before any slide exists, let the tools build the rough deck, then polish it into something worth the room’s time. The next three pages show it working on real decks.

01 · Story

Brief the deck before it exists

The worst decks start in the slide software. Start with a CRIT brief instead: Context (who is in the room and what they care about), Role (who the AI should build as), Interview (tell it to ask about the audience before it builds), and Task (the one thing the room must remember, the decision you need, and an outline of 5 to 8 points).

ContextRoleInterviewTask

The Interview line matters most. Writing “ask me up to three questions about the audience before you build anything” makes the AI ask about the room instead of guessing at it.

4parts to every brief

The four-part brief comes from Geoff Woods; the Working Smarter course teaches it in full.

02 · Build

Let the tools build the rough deck

03 · Polish

You make it worth the room’s time

The rough deck is raw material. Cut it hard (one idea per slide), put it on the Vanderbilt template, check every number and chart against its source, and rehearse from the speaker notes. This pass is where a generated deck becomes your deck, and it’s the part only you can do.

Worked example 1 of 3: the leadership update. Example / 03 · Leadership update

Five slides for the dean’s leadership team.

The situation

Your program’s quarterly update is on the agenda for a dean’s leadership team. You have ten minutes, five slides, and one real request: a decision you need from them. This is the deck people burn a whole day on, because every slide feels like it has to prove something.

Together

Do this one together: the instructor takes the audience details from the room, briefs the deck out loud, and generates the outline live.

Pause + reflect

Try it now: copy this prompt into your AI tool and run it. Then compare what comes back with the polish pass below.

The prompt · CRIT shape 
Context: I'm presenting my program's quarterly update to the dean's leadership team. I have ten minutes and five slides. The room cares about results and risks, not activity, and I need one decision from them: approval to post a new position.
Role: You are a chief of staff who builds decision-first decks for senior leaders.
Interview: Ask me up to three questions about the audience before you build anything.
Task: Outline a 5-slide deck. Slide 1 is the decision we're asking for. Then results against goals, the one risk we're managing, next quarter's plan, and the ask again with what happens if we wait. For each slide give a headline sentence, up to three bullets, and two sentences of speaker notes.

The prompt is written as a chat brief, and it works pasted straight into Copilot’s prompt box in PowerPoint too.

Try it in
Copilot in PowerPoint Recommended You’re already in the deck, and internal program data suits the Vanderbilt account. Paste the brief into Copilot’s prompt box. Amplify Also fine Just as good for drafting the outline and the notes; paste the result into PowerPoint when you’re happy. ChatGPT EDU Sits this one out Internal program data belongs in Amplify or Copilot through your Vanderbilt account.

Each example recommends a different tool, so by the end you’ll have tried the full kit.

What comes back

A tidy five-slide skeleton with confident headlines, plausible numbers it invented, and speaker notes that sound like a consultant. That’s fine, because it’s a rough deck and you’re about to polish it.

Your polish pass
  • Replace every number with the real one, checked at its source. The outline’s job was structure; the facts are yours.
  • Lead with the decision the room can actually make. The AI doesn’t know what the dean’s team controls; you do.
  • Move it onto the Vanderbilt template and cut to one idea per slide. Ten minutes means five slides that each say one thing.
Worked example 2 of 3: the training session deck. Example / 03 · Training deck

A how-to document becomes a training deck.

The situation

Your office already has a written how-to for onboarding a new student worker, and now you have to teach it as a 45-minute session. The content exists; it’s just in the wrong shape. This is the deck AI builds best, because the source material is sitting right there.

Before you paste anything: safe data only. If the document is internal, use Amplify or Copilot through your Vanderbilt account, never a personal one. When in doubt, use Amplify.

The prompt · CRIT shape 
Context: Pasted below is our office's how-to document for onboarding a new student worker. I'm turning it into a 45-minute training session for supervisors doing this for the first time.
Role: You are a trainer who turns procedures into sessions people stay awake for.
Interview: Ask me up to three questions about the audience before you build anything.
Task: Turn the document into a 10-slide session skeleton: a title slide, why this matters, one major step per slide with a headline and up to three bullets, one practice moment mid-session, and a closing recap. Write speaker notes for every slide, and flag anything in the document that looks out of date.

What comes back

A ten-slide skeleton that follows the document’s order a little too faithfully, with speaker notes that repeat the slides. Useful bones, flat teaching. The polish pass turns the document’s order into a learner’s order.

Your polish pass
  • Reorder for the learner, not the file. Put the step people get wrong first; the document’s order is rarely the teaching order.
  • Check every name, system, and step against the current process. The document may be stale, and the AI can’t know that.
  • Make the practice moment real. Swap the generic “discuss with a partner” for the actual form or system your learners will touch.

ONBOARDING A NEW STUDENT WORKER
Practice document. Fictional; prepared for the Slides, Faster course.

Purpose. This guide covers the first two weeks for any new student
worker in the Dean's Office, from offer acceptance to first solo
shift.

Before day one. Once the offer is accepted, the supervisor submits
the hire request in the payroll system and requests a building badge
and a computer account. Allow five business days. Send the welcome
email with the parking map, dress guidelines, and the first-week
schedule.

Day one. Meet the student at the front desk at their scheduled start
time. Cover the tour, introductions, emergency exits, and where to
store personal items. Complete the I-9 verification before any work
begins; students cannot start a shift without it. End the day by
setting up their timekeeping login and watching them clock out
correctly.

Week one. Pair the student with an experienced worker for every
shift. Teach the three core tasks in order: front-desk coverage
(greeting, phones, and the visitor log), room reservations in the
scheduling system, and the daily mail run. Do not introduce the cash
box in week one.

Week two. The student runs the front desk with their mentor nearby.
Introduce the cash box procedure only after the supervisor has
observed one full front-desk shift. Schedule the 30-minute check-in
with the supervisor at the end of week two: review timekeeping,
answer questions, and set the regular schedule.

Common problems. Badge requests are the most frequent delay; submit
them the same day the offer is accepted. If the timekeeping login
fails, the student records hours on the paper sheet at the front
desk and the supervisor enters them later. Students who miss the I-9
deadline must pause work until it is complete.

Judgment calls a trainer should highlight: when a student may open
the cash box, and who approves schedule changes.

Copy the prompt, paste this document in its place, and run it in Amplify. Notice the document buries its two judgment calls (the cash box and schedule changes) in the last paragraph; a good session puts judgment calls where people will remember them.

Worked example 3 of 3: the public workshop talk. Example / 03 · Workshop talk

A talk for a room of strangers.

The situation

You’ve agreed to give a 30-minute public workshop on running shorter, better meetings. Nothing in it is sensitive; the hard part is shaping an hour of opinions into a talk with one memorable idea. You need an outline, a talk track, and a title that doesn’t sound like a policy memo.

The prompt · CRIT shape 
Context: I'm giving a 30-minute public workshop on how to run shorter, better meetings. The audience is anyone on campus who books meetings; nothing in the talk is sensitive or internal.
Role: You are a speaker coach who builds talks around one idea people can repeat in the hallway afterward.
Interview: Ask me up to three questions about the audience before you build anything.
Task: Give me three title options, a 10-slide outline with one headline sentence per slide, and a talk track of two or three sentences of speaker notes per slide, written the way people actually speak.
Try it in
ChatGPT EDU Recommended Everyday, public content is exactly what it’s for, and it’s the fastest way in. Amplify Also fine The when-in-doubt choice; it handles this fine and anything you’d rather keep in-house. PowerPoint Also fine Paste the outline in and let Designer suggest the layouts, license or no license5.
What comes back

Three usable titles, a clean outline, and a talk track that runs about a third too long, with a joke or two you would never tell. Generated talks run long and sound like everyone; the polish pass makes this one yours.

Your polish pass
  • Cut a third of the slides. Thirty minutes means about ten slides at most, and the room will thank you for nine.
  • Read the speaker notes aloud once, all the way through. Rewrite every sentence you stumble on; your mouth finds what your eyes miss.
  • Pick the title you can say without reading it, then make the last slide the one thing you want repeated in the hallway.
The rules that keep the method safe. Guardrails / 04

The four rules.

The method only works if you follow these. They take thirty seconds to learn.

Rule 01
Safe data only

Internal numbers, personnel matters, and unreleased plans go through Amplify or Copilot on your Vanderbilt account, never a personal one. When in doubt, use Amplify.

Rule 02
Check every number

Every figure and every chart gets checked against its source before the room sees it. A confident chart is not a correct chart.

Rule 03
The template and the credit are yours

Put the deck on your unit’s template, add alt text (PowerPoint can generate it; you confirm it), and disclose substantive AI help where it matters.

Rule 04
Slides support the talk

You are the presentation; the deck is scenery. If it reads like a document, cut it; the AI can always regenerate the detail as a handout instead.

The data rule · the same card in every AI course

Before anything goes into an AI tool, one three-second question: what color is this?

🟢
Green · Public or generic
Questions about public topics, generic drafts, brainstorming, "explain this to me." Any approved tool.
🟡
Yellow · Internal work
Your drafts, internal announcements, budgets, grant drafts, internal policies. Approved Vanderbilt tools only, signed in with your VU account. Never a personal account or a random free site.
🔴
Red · Private information about people
Names with grades, health details, personnel issues, passwords, anything confidential about a real person. No AI tool, full stop.

Inside the light: Vanderbilt classifies data in levels, and each approved tool has a ceiling. ChatGPT EDU (VU account) is approved up to Level 2. Amplify and Microsoft Copilot (VU account) are approved for Level 3 work. Children's names, photos, and records go in no AI tool.

The hard cases: ask "who is this about" before "how sensitive does it feel." A student's essay is about a student. Meeting notes naming colleagues are about colleagues until the names come out. A recommendation letter is about its subject.

Never, in any tool: Social Security numbers · Student records or grades (FERPA) · Health information (HIPAA) · Children's names, photos, and records · Salaries and personnel files · Passwords and credentials · Anything confidential about a real person.

This card is the working shorthand for Vanderbilt IT's data classification and approved-tool guidance: vanderbilt.edu/it/policies. When the two ever disagree, the policy wins.

Three questions, none of them tricks. Knowledge check / 06

Did it stick?

Pick an answer on each; you’ll see right away how you did. Nothing here is scored or sent anywhere. If the LMS version of this course includes a graded check, it replaces this one.

1. Two briefs for the same deck. Which one gets the better rough deck?

The second one. It names the room, the voice, the shape, and, most important, the Interview line, so the AI asks about the audience instead of guessing at it.

2. A colleague pastes unreleased enrollment numbers into a personal ChatGPT account to build chart slides for a board presentation. What went wrong?

Rule one: safe data only. Checking the charts later doesn’t undo pasting unreleased data into an unapproved tool, and no prompt improvement fixes a data problem.

3. The AI hands you back a 20-slide deck where every slide is a wall of text. What’s your next move?

Slides support the talk, not replace it. A deck that reads like a document loses the room; keep one idea per slide and move the depth to a handout people can take with them.
