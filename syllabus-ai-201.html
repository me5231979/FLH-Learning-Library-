About this course

For staff who can already prompt and want to know what is happening under the hood. Why does a chatbot lose the thread of a long conversation? Why does it invent a citation one day and quote the real policy the next? Once a tool starts acting on its own, who is watching it? This session explains the machinery in plain terms: context and attention, grounding with retrieval (RAG), when to prompt versus ground versus fine-tune, four advanced prompt patterns, the agent loop and its documented failure modes, and the governance tiers that keep all of it safe. You will finish by building one real, governed AI workflow for your own work.

Learning objectives

By the end of this session, participants will be able to:

  1. Explain how context and attention shape an answer, and why long conversations lose the thread
  2. Describe what Retrieval-Augmented Generation fixes, and what it doesn't
  3. Choose between prompting, RAG, and fine-tuning for a task, weighing cost, data, and maintenance
  4. Apply four advanced prompt patterns: Few-Shot, Chain-of-Thought, Template, and Flipped Interaction
  5. Recognize the agent loop, name its documented failure modes, and place the human checkpoints
  6. Classify information into governance tiers and build one real, governed AI workflow for their own work

Topics covered

  • Context & attention. Why the model weighs some words more than others, and why long chats drift (Vaswani et al., 2017)
  • Grounding with RAG. What retrieval fixes, what it doesn't, and why the answers are only as good as the library behind them (Lewis et al., 2020)
  • Choosing your method. Prompting, RAG, or fine-tuning: cost, data, and maintenance trade-offs for a real task
  • Advanced patterns. Few-Shot (Brown et al., 2020), Chain-of-Thought (Wei et al., 2022), and the Template and Flipped Interaction patterns (White et al., 2023)
  • Agents & the loop. The plan-act-observe loop, its documented failure modes, and where the human checkpoint belongs (NIST AI RMF)
  • Spot the hallucination. A graded lab in catching fluent, confident, wrong output before it ships
  • Governance. Information tiers, approved Vanderbilt tools, and the workflow you build and keep

How you'll learn

A worked demonstration of context loss, a grounding comparison you run yourself, a method-choice drill on real tasks, four pattern exercises with your own work, the graded hallucination-spotting lab, inline knowledge checks with a scored recap, a glossary, and the My AI Workflow capstone you keep.

Outcomes and how learning is measured

Learners leave with one governed AI workflow for their own work: the task, the tool, the data tier, the pattern used, and the human checkpoint marked. Learning is measured in session through the graded lab, per-section knowledge checks, and a scored recap, and at close through a confidence check. A 7-day follow-up asks whether the workflow ran and what the checkpoint caught; that artifact, not the confidence score, is the transfer evidence.

Assessment uses Bloom's-verb objectives with Kirkpatrick Levels 1 and 2 measured in session (reaction and scored checks) and a Level 3 indicator at 7 days (the workflow artifact). The facilitator edition follows an ATD runbook structure (Say, Do, Ask, Debrief, Transition). Data rules follow Vanderbilt's approved-tool guidance and the shared traffic light: green for public or generic, yellow for internal work in approved VU tools only (ChatGPT EDU, Amplify, Copilot), red for private information about people, never.
