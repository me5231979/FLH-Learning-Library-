Guardrails & Responsible Use

The four things every team needs written down

About this course

Start Smarter taught the traffic light for what is safe to paste. This session is the standalone governance course that writes down the rest. Stanford's AI Index tracks AI misuse incidents climbing in step with adoption, and at a university holding FERPA-protected student records and health-adjacent data, being careful is a habit rather than a policy. Learners build the Four Lines (the course's working method), a one-page team agreement mapped to the NIST AI RMF Govern-Map-Measure-Manage functions: what data may go into which tools, when a human must verify before something travels, when readers must be told AI helped, and who owns the output when it is wrong. Each line gets its own drill on the hard cases rather than the easy ones: student work, meeting notes naming colleagues, recommendation letters, grant drafts, the rejection email that mentions a person by name. The session ends with learners drafting the first two lines for their own team.

Learning objectives

By the end of this session, participants will be able to:

  1. Decide which of four AI outputs needs a named human check before it ships, and write the guardrail that makes that check routine
  2. Classify data with the traffic light, including the hard cases: student work, meeting notes, letters about people
  3. Decide which outputs need a named human check and when AI use must be disclosed to the reader
  4. Assign ownership of every AI-assisted output to the human who ships it
  5. Draft the Four Lines, a starter one-page agreement for your own team's real work

Topics covered

  • Why written rules. Incidents rising alongside adoption, and the difference between a team with written lines and a team with assumptions
  • Line one: what goes in. The traffic light with its hard cases, and why anything about a person stays out of unapproved tools
  • Line two: when a human verifies. Sizing the check to what the output can break, from no check at all to a full source check
  • Line three: when we disclose. The would-they-feel-misled test, applied to external communications, assessment, and the university's voice
  • Line four: who owns it. Output ownership, the unread-draft problem, and why AI never appears in an accountability chain
  • Write your Four Lines. Drafting the agreement, giving it a home people can find, and routing genuinely unclear cases to policy owners

How you'll learn

Four scenario trainers (a research guessing game, a hard-case traffic-light sort, a size-the-check triage, and a disclose-or-not drill), a private agreement drafter that produces a starter Four Lines, the graded Guardrails Lab following a rollout to month one, inline knowledge checks, a scored recap, and the capstone commitment card. Exercises run on the learner’s own screen and are not collected; see the privacy note.

Outcomes and how learning is measured

Learners leave with a drafted agreement covering their own team's real work, a named home for it, and a dated first move to bring it to the team. Learning is measured in session through trainer scores, the graded lab, and a scored recap; at close through a confidence check and a spoken commitment; and afterward through a 7-day pulse on agreements actually discussed and a 30-day re-poll on whether the lines survived contact with real work.

Built on Bloom's-verb objectives and instrumented with Kirkpatrick Levels 1 and 2 in session (a fist-to-five confidence check at close is Level 1; the scored recap and per-section validation checks are Level 2), with the capstone commitment card plus a 7-day pulse and a 30-day re-poll serving as Level 3 indicators; they are self-reported, so they signal transfer rather than prove it. The facilitator edition follows an ATD runbook structure (Say, Do, Ask, Debrief, Transition) with Full 90 and Core 60 timed paths. Data rules follow Vanderbilt's approved-tool guidance and the shared traffic light: green for public or generic, yellow for internal work in approved VU tools only (ChatGPT EDU, Amplify, Copilot), red for private information about people, never.
