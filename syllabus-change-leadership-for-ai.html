Change That Sticks

Naming fear, building safety, leading the conversation

About this course

Workflow and Process Redesign tags change management; this session makes change leadership the entire subject. Accenture's leadership research finds the minority of leaders who lead AI change effectively differ in curiosity, courage, and connection rather than tool fluency. AI change fails on human ground: it gets announced as technology and experienced as threat, and the unnamed question in the room converts quietly into resistance. The course's working method is Name, Frame, Invite, Hold: say the fear out loud before it says itself, frame honestly including what you do not know and never promising what you cannot deliver, invite the team into the design because people support what they help build, and hold the container over months rather than declaring victory at the announcement. The session pairs naturally with the Building Brave Teams workshop.

Learning objectives

By the end of this session, participants will be able to:

  1. Diagnose whether a stalled AI change is failing on human ground or technical ground
  2. Name fear and job-security concerns directly, without false promises
  3. Build psychological safety around experimenting with AI in public
  4. Facilitate your team's own ethics and governance conversation
  5. Sustain change leadership over months rather than announcements

Topics covered

  • Why AI change fails. The Accenture finding, and the pattern of change announced as technology and experienced as threat
  • Name the fear. The unsaid question in every AI meeting, with scripts and the honesty rules that keep them credible
  • Safety to stumble. Psychological safety applied to adoption, and why the quiet ones rather than the enthusiasts decide whether change sticks
  • The ethics conversation. Hosting it rather than outsourcing it, treating disagreement as material, and knowing when to escalate
  • The Change Lab. A graded six months, including the job-security question asked point blank in week two
  • Hold for the long arc. The revisit cadence, the quiet signals worth watching, and measuring trust rather than usage

How you'll learn

Four scenario trainers (a research guessing game, a judge-the-opening-line drill, a read-the-room drill, and a long-arc moves drill), a choose-the-next-line branching dialogue on the job-security question, a private conversation planner producing a naming line and a first ethics agenda, the graded Change Lab with rerun, inline knowledge checks, a scored recap, and the My Change Card capstone. Exercises run on the learner’s own screen and are not collected; see the privacy note.

Outcomes and how learning is measured

Learners leave with a dated card naming the change their team faces, the naming line they will use, the honest frame, the invitation, and a revisit date. Learning is measured in session through trainer scores, the graded lab, and a scored recap; at close through a confidence check and a spoken commitment; and afterward through a 7-day pulse on conversations actually held and a 30-day re-poll on trust rather than usage.

Built on Bloom's-verb objectives and instrumented with Kirkpatrick Levels 1 and 2 in session (a fist-to-five confidence check at close is Level 1; the scored recap and per-section validation checks are Level 2), with the capstone commitment card plus a 7-day pulse and a 30-day re-poll serving as Level 3 indicators; they are self-reported, so they signal transfer rather than prove it. The facilitator edition follows an ATD runbook structure (Say, Do, Ask, Debrief, Transition) with Full 90 and Core 60 timed paths. Data rules follow Vanderbilt's approved-tool guidance and the shared traffic light: green for public or generic, yellow for internal work in approved VU tools only (ChatGPT EDU, Amplify, Copilot), red for private information about people, never.
